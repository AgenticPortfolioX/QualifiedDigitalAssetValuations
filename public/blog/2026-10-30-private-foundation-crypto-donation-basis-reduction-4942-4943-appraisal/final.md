# Donating Digital Assets to a Private Foundation: The Section 170(e)(1)(B)(ii) Basis Reduction That Erases the Fair Market Value Deduction, the Twenty-Percent Ceiling, the 2026 Floor and 2/37 Haircut, and the Single Appraisal That Sections 4942 and 4943 Both Depend On
*Published: October 30, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

A donor holds forty bitcoin acquired in 2017 for $180,000. In the last week of October 2026 the position is worth approximately $4,000,000. He wants to endow a family foundation with it, and his accountant has summarized the plan in one sentence: "You deduct four million and never pay tax on the gain."

The second clause is correct. The first is wrong by $3,820,000.

The error is not a rounding difference or a matter of aggressive positioning. It is the single most expensive misconception in the charitable planning of digital assets, and it arises from a statutory rule that has been in the Code since 1969 and that almost no crypto-native advisor thinks to apply: Section 170(e)(1)(B)(ii) of the Internal Revenue Code. A contribution of appreciated property to a private foundation is reduced by the amount of gain that would have been long-term capital gain if the property had been sold at fair market value on the contribution date. The donor's deduction for the forty bitcoin is $180,000. Not $4,000,000.

The gift is still worth making. It simply is not the transaction the client believed it was. A private foundation gift of a low-basis digital asset is a gain-avoidance transaction, not a deduction-maximization transaction, and the two are not interchangeable. Understanding which one you are executing determines the vehicle, the appraisal scope, and the entire compliance architecture that begins the moment the coins land in the foundation's wallet.

## The recipient determines the measure of the deduction

Four vehicles accept a gift of digital assets, and they do not produce comparable deductions.

| Recipient | Measure of the deduction | Ceiling for appreciated property | Appraisal duty | Operating burden |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Public charity | Fair market value | 30% of contribution base | Qualified appraisal over $5,000 | None |
| Donor-advised fund | Fair market value | 30% of contribution base | Qualified appraisal over $5,000 | None; sponsor administers |
| Private operating foundation | Fair market value | 30% of contribution base | Qualified appraisal over $5,000 | Operating foundation tests, Section 4942(j)(3) |
| Private non-operating foundation | Cost basis | 20% of contribution base | Qualified appraisal over $5,000 | Sections 4940 through 4945 |

The fourth row is where family foundations live. The reduction that produces the fourth row is the subject of the rest of this analysis.

Three categories of private foundation escape the basis reduction: private operating foundations, flow-through foundations, and pooled common funds, the organizations described in Section 170(b)(1)(F). A donor who intends to rely on that exception should obtain the determination letter and the most recent Form 990-PF before signing anything, because the classification is a factual matter and the Form 8283 will be read against it.

## Section 170(e)(1)(B)(ii) in operation

The statute does not say "basis." It says the contribution is reduced by the amount of gain that would have been long-term capital gain if the property had been sold at fair market value. For capital gain property held more than one year, that reduction equals the entire appreciation, and the residue is basis. The two formulations produce the same number, and the statutory one explains the policy: Congress permitted a donor to fund a private foundation with pre-tax dollars only to the extent of the donor's actual investment in the property.

The arithmetic on the forty bitcoin:

- Fair market value on the contribution date: $4,000,000
- Adjusted basis: $180,000
- Long-term gain that would have been recognized on a sale: $3,820,000
- Section 170(e)(1)(B)(ii) reduction: $3,820,000
- Charitable contribution before limitation: $180,000

Nothing about the transfer changes. The donor does not recognize the $3,820,000 of gain, because Section 1001 requires a sale or other disposition and a gift is neither. The foundation receives forty bitcoin with a carryover basis of $180,000 under Section 1015. The only difference from the client's expectation is the deduction, and it is a difference of an order of magnitude.

There is a second consequence buried in that carryover, and it belongs in the planning conversation rather than in the post-mortem. The foundation's basis is the donor's basis. When the foundation eventually sells, the $3,820,000 of gain is realized in the foundation's hands, and Section 4940 imposes a 1.39 percent excise tax on the net investment income that includes it. The gain was deferred, not eliminated, and the deferral ends when the foundation liquidates to fund grants.

## Why a token can never be qualified appreciated stock

The only route to a fair market value deduction for appreciated property given to a non-operating private foundation is the qualified appreciated stock exception in Section 170(e)(5). The definition has two requirements, and a digital asset fails the first.

Qualified appreciated stock means stock of a corporation for which, as of the date of the contribution, historical trading prices are readily available on an established securities market, and which is capital gain property in the donor's hands. The exception exists because Congress accepted that a market quotation on a listed security is a reliable measure of value that does not need an appraiser to construct.

Cryptocurrency is property, not stock. IRS Notice 2014-21 established that treatment in 2014 and nothing since has displaced it. A digital asset is not an equity interest in a corporation, and an exchange quotation is a price on a trading venue rather than a quotation on an established securities market within the meaning of the securities laws. Both requirements are conjunctive, so failing either is dispositive. A token issued by a foundation-adjacent entity, a tokenized fund share, or a wrapped instrument does not cure the defect unless the interest is genuinely stock of a corporation and genuinely listed.

This is why the comparison in the first table is not a matter of degree. The four-vehicle comparison is not a spectrum from generous to stingy; it is a categorical difference in what is being measured.

## Basis is a valuation question, and it is usually unexamined

The reduction converts the deduction into a function of basis. That should change what the engagement is for, because in most digital asset matters basis is the least documented number in the file.

A donor who acquired bitcoin across three exchanges between 2017 and 2020, moved it between wallets, received staking rewards into the same address, and consolidated positions through a bridge does not have a basis figure. He has a reconstructed estimate. The lot identification method, the treatment of units received as income, the effect of the identification relief the Service provided for the 2026 broker reporting phase-in, and the allocation of fees all move the number, and every dollar of basis is a dollar of deduction.

An appraiser engaged only to opine on fair market value has not answered the question the deduction actually turns on. The scope of work for a private foundation gift should capture both the contribution-date value and the basis it is being measured against, or should state expressly that basis determination was outside the assignment and identify the records the donor's accountant relied on. Either approach is defensible. Silence is not, because a Form 8283 that reports a value without a basis trail invites the examiner to reconstruct both.

## The 2026 arithmetic: floor, ordering rule, and the 2/37 haircut

Three provisions enacted in 2025 apply for the first time to tax years beginning after December 31, 2025, and they compound the reduction.

**The floor.** Under Section 170(b)(1)(I), an individual who itemizes may deduct charitable contributions only to the extent they exceed 0.5 percent of the contribution base. On a $2,000,000 contribution base the first $10,000 of giving produces no deduction at all.

**The ordering rule and its trap.** Section 170(d)(1)(C) permits the amount disallowed by the floor to be carried forward, but only if the taxpayer's contributions in that year exceeded the applicable percentage ceiling. A donor whose contributions fall below the ceiling loses the floor amount permanently. For a private foundation gift the ceiling is 20 percent of the contribution base, so a $180,000 deduction against a $2,000,000 base sits far below the $400,000 ceiling and the $10,000 is simply gone.

**The 2/37 limitation.** Section 68, as amended, reduces itemized deductions by 2/37 of the lesser of the total itemized deductions or the amount by which taxable income computed without itemized deductions exceeds the threshold at which the 37 percent bracket begins. The effect is to cap the benefit of itemized deductions at 35 percent. It applies after every other limitation, and it reaches trusts and estates as well as individuals.

Run the full sequence on the example:

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Section 170(e)(1)(B)(ii) contribution | $180,000 |
| 20% ceiling ($400,000) applied | No limitation |
| 0.5% floor ($10,000) applied | $170,000 |
| Floor amount carryforward | None, ceiling not exceeded |
| Total itemized deductions assumed, including $60,000 other | $230,000 |
| 2/37 reduction, charitable share | $9,189 |
| Net charitable deduction | $160,811 |

At the 35 percent effective rate the tax benefit is roughly $56,000. The client expected approximately $1,400,000. The private foundation route has forfeited about 96 percent of the deduction he was promised.

Now run the same forty bitcoin through a donor-advised fund. The deduction is $4,000,000, the ceiling is 30 percent of the contribution base at $600,000, the year-one deduction after the floor and the haircut is approximately $590,000, and $3,400,000 carries forward for five years. Over a six-year horizon most of it is absorbed.

That comparison is the whole planning decision in one table. The private foundation buys perpetual family control, a grantmaking institution, and multi-generational governance. It pays for them with the deduction. A donor who wants both should not send a low-basis asset to the foundation. He should fund the foundation with high-basis assets or cash, where the 20 percent ceiling is the only constraint, and route the deeply appreciated position to a donor-advised fund or a public charity. The two gifts accomplish different things and the tax code rewards them accordingly.

## One appraisal, three consumers

Here is the part of the analysis that practitioners most often collapse, and it matters because a private foundation gift requires more valuation work than a DAF gift that produces a larger deduction.

The contribution-date fair market value is still required. It is reported on Form 8283 Section B. It becomes the foundation's opening asset record. It enters the Section 4943 permitted-holdings calculation, which is a percentage-of-value test. And it is the number a Section 6662 valuation misstatement analysis will examine if the donor claims the exception he does not qualify for. The appraisal is not made less necessary by the basis reduction. It is made necessary by the foundation.

Three consumers then read that appraisal for three different purposes.

| Consumer | Question asked | Governing rule | What changes the answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Donor's return | What was contributed, and is the reported value supportable? | Sections 170(f)(11) and 6662; Treasury Regulation 1.170A-13(c) | Appraisal date, identification of the interest, market data window |
| Foundation's minimum distribution | What is the asset worth for the 5 percent calculation? | Section 4942(e) | Valuation frequency, and the 10 percent cap on blockage reductions |
| Foundation's own return | What is the basis, and what gain arises on sale? | Sections 1015 and 4940 | Carryover basis, and the foundation's sale price |

A legitimate divergence between these numbers is not a defect. It is what the statutes require, and the appraisal should say so before an examiner says it for you.

## Section 4942 and the five percent that a volatile asset cannot easily fund

A private foundation must distribute an amount at least equal to its minimum investment return each year, and Section 4942(e)(1) defines that as 5 percent of the excess of the fair market value of assets not used directly in carrying out the exempt purpose over acquisition indebtedness. A $4,000,000 token position therefore generates a distribution obligation of roughly $200,000, payable in cash, whether or not the foundation holds any cash.

Section 4942(e)(2) then dictates how the asset is valued, and this is where digital assets sit in an unresolved place. Securities for which market quotations are readily available are valued monthly. All other assets are valued at the times and in the manner the regulations prescribe, which in practice means an annual determination on a consistent date. The Service treats cryptocurrency as property and has not classified it as a security with readily available market quotations for this purpose. A strict reading therefore produces annual valuation, and annual valuation of a volatile asset produces a distribution obligation that can be several times too high or far too low depending on a single day's price.

Two practices have emerged. The first is to value the position annually as an "other asset" and accept the volatility in the obligation. The second, urged by practitioners who have worked through the arithmetic, is to value it monthly on the same basis as a readily-quoted security and disclose the method. The second produces an obligation that reflects the asset's actual value across the year and is far easier to defend as reasonable. It also requires twelve monthly determinations rather than one, which is an engagement scope question the foundation should settle before the first gift, not in the 990-PF.

There is a statutory ceiling on the adjustment that follows, and it is frequently missed. Section 4942(e)(2)(B) permits a reduction in value for blockage or similar factors only where the foundation establishes that the size of the block, closely held status, or the prospect of a forced or distress sale would prevent liquidation within a reasonable period at fair market value, and it caps any such reduction at 10 percent of fair market value.

That cap does real work in digital asset practice. An appraisal prepared for the donor may support a substantially larger discount for lack of marketability on a restricted or thinly traded position, and that discount can be entirely appropriate for the purpose it serves. It cannot be carried into the minimum distribution calculation at the same magnitude, because the statute will not allow more than ten percent. The appraisal and the 990-PF will therefore report different numbers for the same asset, and the workfile should contain the reconciliation that explains why.

## Section 4943: when a token position is an interest in a business enterprise

Section 4943 taxes excess business holdings at 10 percent of the excess, with an additional 200 percent tax if the excess is not corrected within the taxable period. The rule was enacted to stop donors from parking controlling interests in businesses inside foundations, and its application to digital assets turns on a question the statute answers indirectly.

A unit of bitcoin or ether is not an interest in a business enterprise. There is no enterprise, so there are no holdings, and Section 4943 does not apply. The analysis changes as the instrument becomes equity-like:

- **Governance tokens in a decentralized protocol or a decentralized autonomous organization.** Section 4943(c)(3) substitutes beneficial interest for voting stock in any unincorporated enterprise. A governance position large enough to direct treasury decisions is a plausible beneficial interest in an unincorporated enterprise.
- **Limited partnership interests in a crypto fund.** Section 4943(c)(3) substitutes profits interest for voting stock. A gift of a fund interest is squarely within the rule, and it is the single most common way a foundation acquires a Section 4943 problem it did not anticipate.
- **Membership interests in an LLC holding a token treasury.** The LLC is an unincorporated enterprise; the membership interest is the holding.
- **Positions in a token issuer that is a corporation.** The 20 percent voting stock test applies directly.

Where the rule applies, the permitted holding is 20 percent of the voting or profits interest, reduced by the percentage held by disqualified persons, under Section 4943(c)(2)(A). Section 4943(c)(2)(B) permits 35 percent where non-disqualified persons have effective control, and Section 4943(c)(2)(C) provides a 2 percent de minimis allowance. Section 4943(d)(3) excludes a trade or business that derives at least 95 percent of gross income from passive sources, which is a real carve-out for a token that functions as a claim on a passive treasury rather than an operating business.

The timing rules are what make the section workable for gifts. Under Section 4943(c)(6), holdings received by gift rather than purchase are treated as held by a disqualified person for five years, so a gift that creates excess holdings does not generate tax during that window while the foundation reduces the position. Treasury Regulation 53.4943-6(b)(1) starts the clock for acquisitions under a will or trust on the date of distribution from the estate or trust, which can be years after death. Treasury Regulation 53.4943-2(a)(1)(ii) provides a 90-day window where the foundation learns of an event that created excess holdings. Section 4943(c)(7) permits the Service to extend the five years by another five on a filed plan that is also submitted to the state attorney general.

The practical instruction is blunt: identify the enterprise question before the gift, not after. A donor who gifts a fund interest to his own foundation and learns about the five-year clock when the Form 990-PF is prepared has already spent part of the window.

## Section 4941: the gift is not the problem, the custody is

A contribution to a private foundation is not an act of self-dealing. Section 4941 taxes transactions between the foundation and a disqualified person, and generosity is not a transaction. The exposure begins immediately afterward, in the arrangements.

Where the foundation's tokens sit in the donor's personal wallet, controlled by keys the donor holds, the foundation's assets are in the possession of a disqualified person. Where the foundation sells the position back to the donor or to an entity he controls, that is a sale between a foundation and a disqualified person, prohibited regardless of whether the price favors the foundation. Where the foundation lends against the position, pledges it as collateral for the donor's obligation, or permits the donor to display a foundation-held NFT, each is a separate use of foundation assets by a disqualified person. Where the foundation pays investment management fees to an entity the donor controls, the compensation arrangement requires care.

The taxes are 10 percent of the amount involved on the disqualified person and 5 percent on a foundation manager who knowingly participates, with additional taxes of 200 percent and 50 percent respectively if the act is not corrected within the taxable period and the manager-level amounts capped per act. The correction requirement, not the tax, is usually the larger problem.

The controls that answer this are unglamorous and effective: an independent custodian or a multi-signature arrangement in which no disqualified person holds a controlling share of keys; a board resolution accepting the gift and stating the custody arrangement; a written custody agreement; and contemporaneous documentation of every subsequent transfer. None of that requires the donor to give up governance of the foundation. It requires him to stop being the foundation's counterparty.

## Sections 4944 and 4940: concentration and the excise that follows the gain

Section 4944 imposes a 10 percent excise tax on the amount of an investment made in a manner that jeopardizes the carrying out of exempt purposes, for each year in the taxable period, plus 25 percent if the investment is not removed from jeopardy. Foundation managers who knowingly participate face 10 percent, capped at $10,000, and 5 percent additional, capped at $20,000, for refusal to agree to removal. Program-related investments are excluded under Section 4944(c).

Jeopardy is a facts-and-circumstances test, and the facts of a single-token foundation are unfavorable. A foundation whose entire corpus is one volatile asset, with no liquid reserve against a fixed distribution obligation, is the paradigm of the risk the section addresses. The defense is not that the asset is sound; it is an investment policy that states the concentration, the liquidity reserve, the rebalancing plan, and the board's consideration of the risk, adopted before the gift rather than after a bad quarter.

Section 4940 is the routine cost and the one donors consistently forget. Net investment income of a domestic private foundation is taxed at 1.39 percent. That base includes capital gain net income realized when the foundation sells the position, and it includes staking, lending, and yield income the foundation earns while it holds. Because basis carried over, the gain measured on that sale starts from the donor's $180,000, not from the $4,000,000 value at contribution. The 1.39 percent applies to the whole spread.

## The documentation chain

Five documents carry the position, and each has a distinct failure mode.

**Form 8283, Section B** reports the contributed property, the contribution date, the appraised value, the appraiser's declaration, and the donee's acknowledgment. Section 170(f)(11)(A)(i) disallows the deduction entirely where the form is not included with the return. Section 170(f)(11)(C) requires a qualified appraisal for property over $5,000, and digital assets do not qualify for the publicly traded securities exception. Section 170(f)(11)(D) requires the appraisal itself to be attached where the claimed value exceeds $500,000.

**The qualified appraisal** must be made not earlier than 60 days before the contribution date and received by the donor no later than the due date of the return including extensions, under Treasury Regulation 1.170A-13(c)(3)(i). It must be prepared, signed, and dated by a qualified appraiser within the meaning of Section 170(f)(11)(E), and the fee may not be based on the appraised value. A qualified appraisal prepared in accordance with USPAP satisfies the generally accepted appraisal standards requirement, which is the reason a standards-compliant report is worth its cost in this context rather than a decorative credential.

**The contemporaneous written acknowledgment** under Section 170(f)(8) is required for any gift of $250 or more and is separate from the Form 8283 acknowledgment. The two are not substitutes.

**Form 8282** reports the foundation's disposition of the property within three years of receipt, under Section 6050L. A foundation that liquidates the position in month two files both a Form 8282 and a Form 990-PF entry, and the Service will compare the sale price to the appraised value. A divergence is explainable; an unexplained divergence that consistently runs in one direction is an invitation.

**Form 990-PF** discloses the asset, its valuation method, the distribution calculation, and the excise tax computation, and it is a public document.

## What the appraisal workfile has to hold

The workfile is where a defensible position lives or dies, and for a private foundation gift it has more content than a comparable DAF engagement.

| Element | What it must establish |
|---|---|
| Contributed interest | The exact rights transferred: coin, custodied claim, fund interest, governance position, or membership interest |
| Contribution date and time | A timestamp and, where relevant, the block height, because value moves hourly |
| Basis trail | Acquisition records, lot identification, treatment of income-received units, and the reconciliation to the reported figure |
| Fair market value support | Principal market selection, the quotation window, and the source hierarchy where venues disagree |
| Restrictions and agreements | Any lockup, vesting, redemption queue, transfer restriction, or side letter affecting the interest |
| Foundation-purpose value | The value to be used in the Section 4942 calculation, and the reason it differs from the contribution-date figure |
| Blockage or marketability adjustment | The amount claimed and the statutory reconciliation where Section 4942(e)(2)(B) caps it |
| Section 4943 analysis | Whether the interest is in a business enterprise, the permitted-holding percentage, and the disposition window |
| Custody attestation | Who controls the keys, under what agreement, and confirmation that no disqualified person holds effective control |
| Standards compliance | The USPAP standards applied, the scope of work, and the extraordinary assumptions or hypothetical conditions |

## The decision rule

Four questions resolve the vehicle question, and they are worth asking in order.

**Is the basis close to fair market value?** If the position was acquired recently, the Section 170(e)(1)(B)(ii) reduction costs little and the private foundation produces nearly the same deduction as a donor-advised fund, subject to the lower ceiling. If the position has multiplied tenfold, the foundation route forfeits the deduction and the DAF route should carry the gift.

**Does the donor need perpetual control?** A foundation buys governance, employment, family participation, and a named institution. A donor-advised fund buys the deduction and administrative silence. Most donors who choose a foundation do so for reasons that have nothing to do with tax, which is a legitimate basis for the decision as long as the cost is stated honestly at the outset.

**Can the foundation meet a 5 percent obligation in cash?** If the only asset is the token, the first distribution requires a sale, and the sale triggers the carryover gain and the 1.39 percent excise. A foundation funded with a mix of liquid and illiquid assets has options that a single-asset foundation does not.

**Is the interest genuinely a token, or is it an interest in an enterprise?** A fund interest, an LLC membership interest, or a large governance position brings Section 4943 into the analysis and starts a clock. Plain bitcoin does not.

The appraisal answer follows from those four. A gift that will produce a basis-limited deduction still requires a qualified appraisal, still requires Form 8283 Section B, and still requires Defensible Data behind every figure. What changes is who needs it: the foundation, its auditors, its examiners, and the state attorney general, rather than the donor's Schedule A.

That is the reframe worth carrying out of this analysis. When a client says he is funding a foundation with his bitcoin, the useful reply is not a number. It is the question of which transaction he is actually executing, because the answer decides whether the appraisal is protecting a deduction or an institution.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I deduct the fair market value of crypto donated to my own private foundation?

Generally no. Section 170(e)(1)(B)(ii) reduces the contribution by the long-term gain that would have been recognized on a sale, which leaves a deduction equal to cost basis for property held more than one year. Private operating foundations, flow-through foundations, and pooled common funds described in Section 170(b)(1)(F) are excepted, but a standard family foundation is not.

### Is cryptocurrency qualified appreciated stock?

No. Section 170(e)(5) limits the exception to stock of a corporation for which historical trading prices are readily available on an established securities market. Cryptocurrency is property rather than stock under IRS Notice 2014-21, and an exchange quotation is not a quotation on an established securities market. Both requirements must be satisfied, so a digital asset cannot use the exception as currently written.

### What appraisal does a private foundation gift of digital assets require?

A qualified appraisal under Section 170(f)(11)(C) is required for property valued over $5,000, and the publicly traded securities exception does not reach digital assets. The appraisal must be made no earlier than 60 days before the contribution and received by the due date of the return including extensions, must be prepared and signed by a qualified appraiser, and must accompany Form 8283 Section B. Where the claimed value exceeds $500,000, Section 170(f)(11)(D) requires the appraisal itself to be attached to the return.

### How does a foundation value crypto for the five percent payout?

Section 4942(e)(1) sets the minimum investment return at 5 percent of the excess of asset fair market value over acquisition indebtedness. Section 4942(e)(2)(A) requires securities with readily available market quotations to be valued monthly and other assets at the times the regulations prescribe, which for a volatile property asset is usually an annual date. Many practitioners value digital assets monthly and disclose the method, because annual valuation produces a distribution obligation driven by a single day's price.

### Is a governance token an excess business holding?

It can be. Section 4943(c)(3) substitutes beneficial interest for voting stock in an unincorporated enterprise, so a governance position capable of directing treasury decisions is a plausible business holding, as is a limited partnership interest in a crypto fund, where profits interest is substituted. Permitted holdings are 20 percent, or 35 percent where non-disqualified persons have effective control, with a 2 percent de minimis allowance, and Section 4943(c)(6) gives five years to dispose of holdings acquired by gift.

### Can the foundation's crypto stay in the family's wallet?

That is where self-dealing exposure usually begins. Foundation assets held under keys a disqualified person controls, sold back to the donor, pledged for his obligations, or used by him are transactions between the foundation and a disqualified person under Section 4941, and the prohibition applies even where the terms favor the foundation. Independent custody or a multi-signature arrangement without disqualified-person control is the practical answer.
