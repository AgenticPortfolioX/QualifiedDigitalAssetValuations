# The IRS Digital Asset Question on Form 1040: What It Asks, Who Must Answer, and When a Qualified Appraisal Becomes Necessary

*Published: August 28, 2026 | By QDAV*

## The Checkbox That Started a Compliance Era

Every federal income tax return now begins with a question that did not exist a decade ago. Before the taxpayer reports a single dollar of income, before the first Schedule, before any calculation of tax, Form 1040 asks about digital assets. The question is short, mandatory, and signed under penalty of perjury. It does not ask what the assets are worth or when they were acquired. It asks only whether, at any time during the year, the taxpayer received or disposed of a digital asset or a financial interest in one.

That brevity is deceptive. For most taxpayers, the question is answered in seconds and forgotten. But for high-net-worth individuals, estate attorneys, CPAs, trust officers, and fiduciaries, the digital asset question is the entry point to a much larger compliance structure — one that increasingly terminates in a valuation obligation. Checking "Yes" for a gift, a charitable contribution, or an estate asset does more than flag a transaction. It triggers a chain of reporting requirements in which the value of the asset must be established to a standard the IRS actually recognizes.

This guide explains what the question asks, who must answer it, the precise rules for checking "Yes" or "No," and — most importantly — the five situations in which the question converts into a requirement for a **Fair Market Value** determination supported by a **Qualified Appraisal**.

## What the Question Actually Asks

The digital asset question appears at the top of the individual income tax return and reads as follows:

> At any time during the tax year, did you: (a) receive (as a reward, award or payment for property or services); or (b) sell, exchange, or otherwise dispose of a digital asset (or a financial interest in a digital asset)?

The IRS instructs that every taxpayer who files a Form 1040, 1040-SR, or 1040-NR must check one box — "Yes" or "No" — and that the question must be answered by all taxpayers, not only those who engaged in a digital asset transaction during the year. The requirement is not limited to individuals. A version of the question now appears on Form 1041 (estates and trusts), Form 1065 (partnerships), Forms 1120 and 1120-S (corporations), and, notably for the estate and gift practice, on Form 709.

The question is deliberately binary — a filter, not a ledger. It forces an affirmative representation, made under penalties of perjury, about whether the taxpayer crossed into digital asset activity in the taxable year. That representation is then tested against the information returns the IRS already receives — and increasingly, against the valuation documents that accompany gifts, donations, and estates.

## What Counts as a Digital Asset

Understanding the question requires understanding its subject. The term "digital asset" has a statutory definition. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021 added Section 80603, defining a digital asset for purposes of the tax law as "any digital representation of value which is recorded on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger or any similar technology as specified by the Secretary." The definition is codified at Internal Revenue Code Section 6045(g)(3) and implemented through Treasury regulations.

The definition reaches far beyond Bitcoin. Under the IRS's own materials, digital assets include:

- **Convertible virtual currency and cryptocurrency**, such as Bitcoin and Ether — assets that have an equivalent value in real currency or act as a substitute for real currency.
- **Stablecoins** — assets designed to maintain a stable value relative to a reference asset, such as the U.S. dollar.
- **Non-fungible tokens (NFTs)** — unique, non-interchangeable digital tokens, including digital art, collectibles, and tokenized property.

The IRS's frequently asked questions, updated December 15, 2025, confirm that the term covers "any digital representation of value that is recorded on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger, or any similar technology, without regard to whether each individual transaction involving that digital asset is actually recorded on that ledger." Cash — U.S. dollars or convertible foreign currency issued by a government or central bank — is not a digital asset, even in digital form.

The tax treatment follows from a single principle: **digital assets are property, not currency**. The general tax principles applicable to all property transactions apply — the recognition of gain or loss on sale, the character of income from mining and staking, and the gift and estate rules that govern value.

## Everyone Answers, and the Answer Is Under Oath

The mandatory nature of the question is its most underappreciated feature. A taxpayer who has never touched cryptocurrency, who holds no NFTs, who has no wallet and no exchange account, still checks a box. That taxpayer checks "No." The taxpayer who mined Bitcoin in 2014 and has held it untouched in cold storage since — with no transaction in the current year — also checks "No." The question is about activity in the tax year, not about ownership history.

Because the return is signed under penalties of perjury, the checkbox is not a suggestion. A taxpayer who engaged in digital asset transactions and checks "No" has not merely omitted information; the taxpayer has made an affirmative misstatement on a sworn return. Practitioners who have worked through foreign-account enforcement know how seriously the government treats checked boxes: in the FBAR context, courts have held that failing to check a reporting box can itself be evidence of willfulness. The digital asset question works the same way — it converts silence into a false statement and gives the IRS a clean evidentiary hook when broker data does not match the return.

The consequences run through the ordinary penalty structure: accuracy-related penalties under IRC Section 6662 (20 percent for a substantial valuation misstatement, 40 percent for a gross misstatement), failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties, interest, civil fraud penalties in deliberate cases, and criminal referral in the most serious ones. The practical point for advisors is simple: the checkbox deserves the same care as every other line on the return, because it is the line the IRS can test most cheaply.

## When to Check "No"

The IRS has been explicit about when "No" is the correct answer. A taxpayer may check "No" when any of the following is true:

1. **The taxpayer did not own any digital assets** during the tax year.
2. **The taxpayer only held digital assets** in a wallet or account and did not engage in any digital asset transaction during the year.
3. **The taxpayer purchased digital assets using U.S. or other real currency** — including through electronic platforms — but did not sell, exchange, or otherwise dispose of them.
4. **The taxpayer transferred digital assets between wallets or accounts the taxpayer owns or controls** — with one critical exception described below.

The fourth item deserves emphasis because it surprises many holders. Moving Bitcoin from an exchange to a hardware wallet, or from one wallet to another, is a non-taxable event for gain and loss purposes, and the IRS so confirms in its FAQs: a transfer between wallets, addresses, or accounts belonging to the taxpayer is not a taxable event. The digital asset question follows the same logic.

**The exception is the transaction fee.** If the taxpayer paid the transfer fee with digital assets — for example, paying a network "gas" fee in Ether to move tokens — that payment is itself a disposition, because the taxpayer used a digital asset to pay for a service. The IRS is explicit: transferring between your own wallets requires a "Yes" answer "unless you paid a transaction fee with digital assets. This would be a digital asset transaction." A holder who moved assets once and paid a trivial gas fee has, in the IRS's eyes, engaged in a digital asset transaction and must answer "Yes."

## When to Check "Yes"

The "Yes" scenarios are broader than most taxpayers assume. Check "Yes" if, at any time during the year, you did any of the following:

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Received digital assets as payment** | Payment for property or services provided; compensation as an employee or contractor |
| **Received digital assets as a reward or award** | Promotional rewards, platform bonuses, contest awards |
| **Received digital assets from mining, staking, or similar activities** | Proof-of-work rewards, proof-of-stake yields, validator income |
| **Received digital assets from an airdrop relating to a hard fork** | New tokens received in a blockchain fork event |
| **Sold digital assets** | Sale for U.S. dollars or other currency |
| **Exchanged digital assets** | Trading one digital asset for another (e.g., Bitcoin for Ether); exchanging for property, goods, or services in any amount |
| **Paid for goods or services with digital assets** | Any dollar amount — "a cup of coffee" is the IRS's own example |
| **Paid a transaction or transfer fee with digital assets** | Gas fees, network fees, transfer fees paid in digital assets |
| **Gifted or donated a digital asset** | Transfers to family members, friends, or charities |
| **Disposed of an exchange-traded fund (ETF) that held digital assets** | Sale or exchange of a digital-asset-backed ETF |
| **Had any digital asset transactions with stablecoins** | Including swaps, payments, or transfers of stablecoins |
| **Transferred ownership or a financial interest in a digital asset** | Any disposition of the taxpayer's ownership stake |

The last item in the table points to a defined concept. Under IRS guidance, a taxpayer has a **financial interest** in a digital asset if the taxpayer is recorded as the owner of the asset, has an ownership stake in an account that holds digital assets (including the rights and obligations to acquire a financial interest), or owns a wallet that holds digital assets. Disposing of a financial interest — for example, selling a position in a fund that holds digital assets — is reportable for purposes of the question.

The unifying principle is the same as for property generally: any receipt of a digital asset in exchange for something of value, and any disposition of one, triggers "Yes." Holding and buying do not. Everything else does.

## What "Yes" Triggers Downstream

Answering "Yes" is not itself a tax. It is an admission that the taxpayer engaged in activity that must be reported — "whether or not they result in a taxable gain or loss," and "regardless of the amount or whether you receive a payee statement or information return." The downstream reporting map depends on the nature of the transaction:

- **Sales and exchanges.** Capital gain or loss is computed on Form 8949 and summarized on Schedule D (Form 1040). Gain or loss is the difference between adjusted basis and the amount realized — cash received plus the fair market value of any property or services received, reduced by allocable digital asset transaction costs such as commissions and gas fees.
- **Compensation.** Digital assets received as employee wages are reported as wages. Digital assets received by independent contractors for services are self-employment income on Schedule C. In both cases, the fair market value of the assets on receipt is the measure of income.
- **Gifts.** A gift of a digital asset is a transfer subject to the gift tax regime. If the value of gifts to a single recipient exceeds the annual exclusion — **$19,000 per recipient for 2026** — the donor must file Form 709, where a version of the digital asset question now appears. The value reported on Form 709 is the **Fair Market Value** of the asset at the date of the gift.
- **Charitable contributions.** A donation of a digital asset held more than one year is generally deductible at its fair market value at the time of the contribution. A donation held one year or less is deductible at the lesser of basis or fair market value. Noncash contributions over $5,000 require a **Qualified Appraisal** under **IRS Section 170**, and contributions over $500,000 require the qualified appraisal to be attached to the return. Form 8283 documents the contribution; the donee's signature acknowledges receipt, not concurrence in value.
- **Estates and trusts.** For a decedent who held digital assets, the executor must inventory the assets at their date-of-death fair market value on the estate's Form 706 and income tax filings on Form 1041 — both of which carry a version of the digital asset question. The date-of-death value establishes the step-up in basis that determines the income tax consequences for heirs.
- **Retirement accounts.** Digital assets held in self-directed IRAs are subject to annual valuation attestation requirements imposed by custodians and to prohibited-transaction rules restricting in-account activity.

The thread that connects all of these reporting paths is value. Every one requires the taxpayer or fiduciary to state a dollar figure — and to be able to defend it.

## Five Situations Where "Yes" Becomes a Valuation Problem

The digital asset question itself requires no valuation. But for a meaningful minority of taxpayers, the answer to the question opens a file that does. Here are the five situations in which a "Yes" answer transforms into a requirement for a professionally supported **Fair Market Value** — and, in several cases, a **Qualified Appraisal**.

### 1. Gifts Above the Annual Exclusion

A parent who gifts $25,000 of Bitcoin to an adult child has made a taxable gift to the extent it exceeds the $19,000 annual exclusion, and must file Form 709. The gift tax is computed on the fair market value of the Bitcoin at the date of the transfer. For a widely traded asset with a liquid market, the date-of-gift value may be reasonably determinable from exchange data — though the IRS's regulations require that value be established using all relevant facts and circumstances. For assets with thin or erratic markets — a specific NFT, a private token, a DeFi position — exchange spot data is not a defensible measure, and the transfer may require a formal appraisal to support the reported value.

There is a second, frequently overlooked dimension to gifts. The recipient's basis in gifted digital assets is generally the donor's basis, carried over, plus any gift tax attributable to the appreciation. But the IRS FAQs are explicit when documentation is missing: **if the recipient has no documentation to substantiate the donor's basis, the recipient's basis is zero.** A taxpayer who receives gifted cryptocurrency and later sells it without basis documentation may owe tax on the full sale proceeds. A contemporaneous appraisal or documented valuation at the date of the gift is the only reliable way to preserve the basis story for the recipient's future filings.

### 2. Charitable Contributions Over $5,000

The charitable contribution rules contain the clearest appraisal mandate in the digital asset space. Under IRC Section 170(f)(11), a taxpayer claiming a deduction for a noncash contribution with a value over $5,000 must obtain a **Qualified Appraisal** prepared by a qualified appraiser, and must file Form 8283 with the return. Contributions over $500,000 require the qualified appraisal itself to be attached. The IRS has confirmed in Chief Counsel Advice (CCA 202302012) that a charitable contribution of cryptocurrency exceeding the threshold requires a qualified appraisal, and that the value reported by an exchange or data vendor does not substitute for one.

The point is often misunderstood by donors and even by well-meaning charities. A receipt from the donee organization stating "received 2.5 Bitcoin valued at $X" is not a qualified appraisal. A screenshot of a price at donation time is not a qualified appraisal. A transaction report from crypto tax software is not a qualified appraisal. Each fails on the statutory requirements: a qualified appraisal must be performed by a qualified appraiser who meets the education and experience tests for the digital asset category, must comply with the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP), and must be signed and dated with the appraiser's qualifications stated on the face of the document. When substantiation fails, the deduction can be denied in its entirety — with accuracy-related penalties to follow.

### 3. Estates and Date-of-Death Valuation

When a decedent held digital assets, the estate administration process requires a value for every asset at the date of death. That value drives the estate tax on Form 706, the income tax basis that heirs receive (the step-up), and the fiduciary's accounting to beneficiaries. For digital assets, the date-of-death valuation is complicated by the market characteristics that make the assets volatile: thin order books, exchange outages, wash trading in NFT markets, and the difference between a quoted spot price and a defensible opinion of value.

Executors and trustees carry fiduciary duties to value estate assets accurately and to administer the estate for the benefit of all beneficiaries. An undervaluation understates estate tax; an overvaluation overstates basis to the detriment of the estate. The professional standard is a USPAP-compliant appraisal that identifies the asset, establishes the effective date of value, applies the appropriate approach (market, income, or cost), and documents the data behind the conclusion. For estates with significant digital assets, the appraisal is a fiduciary obligation, not a luxury.

### 4. Lost Basis and the Zero-Basis Trap

The zero-basis rule for undocumented gifts is one example of a broader problem: digital asset positions whose acquisition history is incomplete. Early adopters who bought Bitcoin on defunct exchanges, users of wallets whose records were lost, recipients of airdrops and hard-fork tokens, and heirs of decedents with undocumented positions all face the same question when they sell: what is my basis?

The IRS requires taxpayers to maintain records sufficient to establish the positions taken on their returns — records documenting purchase, receipt, sale, exchange, disposition, and the fair market value of digital assets in U.S. dollars. When records are incomplete, taxpayers must reconstruct the acquisition history, establish values at key dates through professional valuation, or accept unfavorable default rules (including zero basis). A qualified appraisal that establishes the fair market value of an asset at a historical date — the date of a gift, the date of death, the date of a hard fork — can convert an unprovable position into a documented one, and can be the difference between a modest gain and tax on the entire proceeds.

### 5. Form 1099-DA Mismatches and Audit Exposure

The 2026 filing environment is defined by information matching. Under the final broker regulations (T.D. 10000, published July 29, 2024), brokers must report gross proceeds and basis for digital asset transactions occurring on or after January 1, 2025, on **Form 1099-DA**. Real estate professionals treated as brokers face reporting obligations for digital assets in real estate transactions with closing dates on or after January 1, 2026. The result is a data environment in which the IRS can compare every "No" answer and every reported figure against third-party records.

When a return does not reconcile with Form 1099-DA — a missing schedule, a different gain, a "No" answer contradicted by broker data — the discrepancy feeds the automated underreporter system and the CP2000 notice pipeline, and it can trigger examination. Defensible documentation is the response: transaction records, wallet histories, cost basis documentation, and, where valuation is contested, a qualified appraisal that supports the reported figures. The taxpayer who can produce professional valuation evidence is in a fundamentally different position than the taxpayer who produces a screenshot.

## Recordkeeping: What the IRS Requires

The recordkeeping obligation deserves its own emphasis because it is the quiet engine of the entire system. The Internal Revenue Code and the regulations require taxpayers to maintain sufficient records to establish the positions taken on their federal income tax returns. For digital assets, the IRS specifies the documentation: records of purchase, receipt, sale, exchange, or other disposition, and the fair market value in U.S. dollars of all digital assets received as income or payment.

The calculation of gain or loss requires five data points: the type of digital asset, the date and time of the transaction, the number of units, the fair market value at the time of the transaction, and the basis of the asset sold or disposed of. The basis of a purchased asset is generally its cost plus qualifying transaction costs. The basis of a gifted asset is the donor's basis, documented. The basis of an inherited asset is its date-of-death fair market value.

Advisors should treat digital asset recordkeeping as an ongoing discipline, not a filing-season scramble. The client who can produce a complete transaction ledger, wallet history, and valuation documentation has a filing that can be defended. The client who produces a tax software report and nothing else has a filing that depends on the software's assumptions — and software reports are transaction logs, not valuations.

## The 2026 Landscape: Enforcement, Reporting, and One Scam

Three developments define the current compliance environment and are worth knowing before preparing a return.

**First, the broker reporting regime is live.** Form 1099-DA is in its first full reporting cycle for transactions beginning January 1, 2025. The IRS's final regulations (T.D. 10000) cover custodial brokers, with continued work signaled on non-custodial and decentralized exchange reporting. The practical consequence: the IRS now receives third-party data for a large share of digital asset transactions — data that will be matched against returns.

**Second, the real estate rule expands the reporting surface.** Real estate professionals treated as brokers must report the fair market value of digital assets paid by buyers and received by sellers in real estate transactions with closing dates on or after January 1, 2026. A home purchase funded partly in cryptocurrency now generates an information return to the IRS that includes the digital asset value.

**Third, be alert to a specific scam.** The IRS has warned that taxpayers are receiving letters with QR codes claiming to be from a "Digital Assets Compliance Portal" and directing recipients to "register" their cryptocurrency. This is a scam. The IRS does not require registration of digital assets and is not sending these letters. Any such letter should be reported and ignored. The existence of the scam is itself evidence of the anxiety the digital asset question has created — anxiety that legitimate professional guidance, not QR-code portals, is the right remedy.

## A Filing Checklist for Attorneys, CPAs, and HNW Clients

For advisors preparing clients for the coming filing season, this checklist covers the question and its consequences:

1. **Inventory activity, not just holdings.** Ask every client whether they received, sold, exchanged, gifted, donated, or paid with digital assets during the year — including gas fees, stablecoin transactions, and ETF dispositions. Assume the answer requires verification, not recollection.
2. **Reconcile the checkbox against Form 1099-DA.** Before the return is filed, compare every broker statement to the client's transaction records. A "No" answer in the presence of a Form 1099-DA is an invitation to CP2000.
3. **Document basis for every position.** Confirm that acquisition records, donor basis documentation, and date-of-death values exist for every asset with a potential disposition. Where documentation is missing, reconstruct it — including through professional valuation of historical dates.
4. **Identify appraisal thresholds.** Flag gifts above the $19,000 annual exclusion, contributions over $5,000 (qualified appraisal required), contributions over $500,000 (appraisal attached), and estates with significant digital assets (date-of-death valuation).
5. **Engage the appraiser early, not at filing.** A qualified appraisal is a document with a defined structure — property description, effective date of value, fair market value conclusion, method of valuation, appraiser qualifications, signature. That work cannot be compressed into a deadline week. For gifts, donations, and estates, the appraisal date should track the transaction date, not the filing date.
6. **Keep the record file.** Transaction ledgers, wallet histories, broker statements, appraisal reports, and the qualified appraiser's engagement documentation should be retained with the client's permanent tax records.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the digital asset question on Form 1040?**
A: It is a mandatory question at the top of Form 1040 (and 1040-SR, 1040-NR, 1041, 1065, 1120, 1120-S, and a version on Form 709) asking whether the taxpayer received or disposed of a digital asset or a financial interest in a digital asset during the tax year. Every taxpayer must check "Yes" or "No," under penalties of perjury.

**Q: Who has to answer the digital asset question?**
A: Everyone who files a covered return, whether or not they own or transacted in digital assets. A taxpayer with no digital asset activity checks "No." The question is mandatory for all filers, not just those with digital asset transactions.

**Q: When should I check "Yes" on the digital asset question?**
A: Check "Yes" if you sold, exchanged, or otherwise disposed of a digital asset; received digital assets as payment, reward, award, or from mining, staking, or a hard-fork airdrop; paid for goods or services with digital assets; paid a transaction fee with digital assets; gifted or donated digital assets; disposed of a digital-asset ETF; or engaged in stablecoin transactions.

**Q: When can I check "No" on the digital asset question?**
A: Check "No" if you did not own any digital assets, only held digital assets in a wallet or account without transacting, purchased digital assets with U.S. or other real currency without selling, or transferred digital assets between wallets or accounts you own or control — provided you did not pay a transaction fee with digital assets.

**Q: What happens if I answer the digital asset question incorrectly?**
A: The return is signed under penalties of perjury, so a wrong answer is an affirmative misstatement. Inaccurate reporting can trigger accuracy-related penalties under IRC Section 6662 (20 percent for substantial valuation misstatements, 40 percent for gross), interest, civil fraud penalties in deliberate cases, and criminal referral in serious ones.

**Q: Do I need an appraisal to answer the digital asset question?**
A: No. The checkbox itself requires no valuation. An appraisal becomes necessary when the activity behind a "Yes" answer creates a valuation obligation: gifts above the annual exclusion, charitable contributions over $5,000, estates requiring date-of-death value, lost-basis reconstruction, and audit defense against challenged figures.

**Q: How does the digital asset question relate to Form 1099-DA?**
A: Form 1099-DA is the broker information return reporting gross proceeds and basis for digital asset transactions. The digital asset question on the tax return must be consistent with the activity reported on Forms 1099-DA. Mismatches between the return and broker data can trigger CP2000 notices and examination.

**Q: What records do I need for digital asset transactions?**
A: The IRS requires records documenting purchase, receipt, sale, exchange, or other disposition of digital assets, plus the fair market value in U.S. dollars of assets received as income or payment. Retain the type of asset, date and time, number of units, fair market value, and basis for each transaction — and keep donor basis documentation for gifts and date-of-death appraisals for inherited assets.

**Q: Do gifts of crypto require a qualified appraisal?**
A: Not always, but the valuation must be supportable. Gifts above the $19,000 annual exclusion require a Form 709 filing reporting the date-of-gift fair market value. For assets with thin markets, a qualified appraisal provides the defensible value and also documents the donor's basis for the recipient — important because a recipient without basis documentation is treated as having zero basis.

**Q: What is the penalty for donating crypto without a qualified appraisal?**
A: A charitable contribution deduction over $5,000 generally requires a qualified appraisal under IRC Section 170(f)(11), and over $500,000 requires the appraisal to be attached to the return. Without proper substantiation, the deduction can be denied, with accuracy-related penalties potentially applying to the underpayment.

## The Question Is the Door

The digital asset question on Form 1040 is a study in regulatory design. It asks almost nothing — a single checkbox — and yet it compels every taxpayer to confront whether they crossed a line the IRS cares about. For most filers, the answer is "No" and the matter closes in seconds. For a smaller but growing group — the donors, the gifting parents, the executors, the early adopters with undocumented positions, the taxpayers whose broker data is about to reach the IRS — the checkbox opens a file that requires value to be established to a professional standard.

That standard is not a price ticker. It is a **Fair Market Value** determined by a qualified appraiser using USPAP-compliant methodology, documented with **Defensible Data**, and capable of surviving scrutiny. Whether the underlying transaction is a gift, a charitable contribution under **IRS Section 170**, an estate administration, or a position under audit, the appraisal that supports it must be a **Qualified Appraisal** in the full statutory sense — performed by a qualified appraiser with verifiable education and experience in the digital asset category, and prepared in accordance with the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice.
