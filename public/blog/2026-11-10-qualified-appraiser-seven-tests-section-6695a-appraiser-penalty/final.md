# The Qualified Appraiser Is a Term of Art: Section 1.170A-17(b)'s Two Credentialing Pathways, the Verifiable-Education Declaration the Preamble Refused to Waive, the Statutory Independence Tests, and the Section 6695A Appraiser Penalty That Runs on Its Own Three-Year Clock
*Published: November 10, 2026 | By QDAV*
---
## The signature is the product

A valuation report is a set of opinions until someone signs it. The signature is what converts a document into an instrument the Internal Revenue Service can rely on, challenge, or penalize. For a charitable contribution of digital assets above the threshold in IRC Section 170(f)(11)(C), the signature must belong to a *qualified appraiser* — and that phrase is a defined term of art with two credentialing pathways, six disqualifications, and a companion penalty provision that reaches the person who signed.

Most of the professional conversation about digital asset valuations concerns methodology: which market data, which comparable sales, how to treat a locked position. That conversation is necessary and it is not the one that decides whether a return survives contact with an examiner. An impeccably reasoned appraisal signed by someone the regulation excludes is not a qualified appraisal. It is an appraisal summary attached to a deduction that fails on a technical ground no amount of analytical rigor can cure.

This analysis takes the appraiser as the subject. It sets out the statutory definition, the two ways to satisfy it, the test that digital assets make genuinely difficult, the documentation the regulation will not let an appraiser skip, the six exclusions, the Section 6695A penalty and how it is computed, the separate procedural machinery that penalty travels through, and a vetting protocol that attorneys and CPAs can run before an engagement letter is signed.

## Key takeaways

- "Qualified appraiser" is defined by IRC Section 170(f)(11)(E) and implemented by Treasury Regulation Section 1.170A-17(b). Relevance, reputation, and years in the crypto industry are not elements of the test.
- Regulation Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(i) provides two alternative pathways: qualifying coursework plus two years of experience, or a recognized appraiser designation for the type of property.
- Section 1.170A-17(b)(3) defines the type of property by reference to what is customary in the appraisal field, and the regulation's three examples show how narrowly that reads. Bitcoin, NFTs, and DeFi positions may not share a type.
- Verifiability under Section 1.170A-17(b)(4) requires the appraiser to state actual education and experience in the report and declare qualification. Treasury expressly refused to accept a generic "I am a qualified appraiser" statement.
- Section 1.170A-17(b)(5) contains six categories of individuals who are not qualified appraisers, including anyone receiving a value-based fee.
- IRC Section 6695A penalizes the appraiser — not the taxpayer — and the penalty is the lesser of the greater of 10 percent of the underpayment or $1,000, or 125 percent of the gross income from the appraisal.
- The appraiser penalty is examined as a separate case with its own three-year statute, and extending the taxpayer's statute does not extend the appraiser's.

## Part One: A defined term, not a description

The statute supplies the definition. IRC Section 170(f)(11)(E)(ii) provides that a qualified appraiser is an individual who has earned an appraisal designation from a recognized professional appraiser organization or has otherwise met minimum education and experience requirements prescribed by the Secretary, who regularly performs appraisals for which the individual receives compensation, and who meets such other requirements as the Secretary prescribes.

Section 170(f)(11)(E)(iii) then adds a property-specific overlay: an individual is not treated as a qualified appraiser with respect to a particular appraisal unless that individual demonstrates verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property subject to the appraisal, and has not been prohibited from practicing before the Service under 31 U.S.C. Section 330(c) at any time during the three-year period ending on the date of the appraisal.

Three features of that definition do most of the work in practice. First, it is an *individual* test. A firm can hold every credential in the market and still need a specific human being to satisfy the elements, because the regulation's machinery — the declaration, the education statement, the disqualifications — attaches to a person who signs. Second, the "regularly performs appraisals for which the individual receives compensation" element is statutory and is not displaced by the regulation's credentialing rules. A respected academic who has published on blockchain price discovery but has never performed a compensated appraisal is a poor fit for the definition regardless of expertise. Third, the test is applied *as of the date the appraiser signs*, which is the point the regulation makes explicit in Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(i) and which matters because credentials lapse, coursework goes stale, and a three-year disciplinary lookback can begin inside an engagement.

The regulation implements the definition in Section 1.170A-17(b)(1): a qualified appraiser is an individual with verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property for which the appraisal is performed, as described in paragraphs (b)(2) through (4). Everything else in the section elaborates on those two words — *verifiable* and *type*.

The value the process produces is Fair Market Value within the meaning of Section 1.170A-1(c)(2): the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or sell and both having reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts. IRS Section 170 does not ask whether that number is plausible. It asks, first, whether the person who produced it was eligible to.

## Part Two: Two pathways, and what the preamble did to the easy one

Regulation Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(i) treats an individual as having education and experience in valuing the type of property if, as of the date the appraisal is signed, the individual has either completed qualifying coursework and two or more years of experience in valuing that type of property, or earned a recognized appraiser designation for that type of property.

| Element | Coursework pathway — Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(i)(A) | Designation pathway — Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(i)(B) |
|---|---|---|
| Core requirement | Professional or college-level coursework in valuing the type of property, *plus* two or more years of experience in valuing that type | A recognized appraiser designation for the type of property |
| What qualifies | Coursework from an educational organization described in Section 170(b)(1)(A)(ii), a generally recognized professional trade or appraiser organization that regularly offers education in that property type, or an employer apprenticeship or educational program substantially similar to either | A designation awarded by a generally recognized professional appraiser organization *on the basis of demonstrated competency* |
| Experience proof | Two years valuing that type of property — not two years in the industry, and not two years valuing adjacent property | Implied by the designation; the appraiser still declares qualification under (b)(4) |
| Common failure | Coursework in a different property category; experience that is really market analysis rather than appraisal work | A certificate issued by a training vendor rather than an appraiser organization; a designation in an unrelated discipline |

The coursework pathway is the one most digital asset appraisers will actually rely on, because the designation marketplace has not produced a settled, field-recognized digital asset appraisal designation in the way it has for real property, machinery, fine art, or closely held businesses. That absence is not a loophole. It means the coursework-plus-experience route carries the weight, and it means the *type of property* question in Part Three becomes the whole case.

The regulation also constrains where the coursework can come from. Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(ii) requires that it be obtained from a professional or college-level educational organization, a generally recognized professional trade or appraiser organization that regularly offers educational programs in valuing that type of property, or an employer's apprenticeship or educational program substantially similar to those. A weekend webinar from a software vendor, standing alone, does not obviously satisfy any of the three.

Then there is the point that separates practitioners who have read the preamble from those who have read only the regulation. Commenters on the 2018 proposed regulations asked Treasury to accept a simple statement that the appraiser is qualified under the regulation and prepared the report in accordance with the substance and principles of USPAP. Treasury declined. The preamble to the final regulations, published at 83 Fed. Reg. 36410, explains that a general statement of qualification does not demonstrate, as Section 170(f)(11)(E)(iii)(I) requires, that the appraiser has verifiable education and experience qualifying the appraiser to prepare the appraisal *for that type of property*.

That refusal is the reason Section 1.170A-17(b)(4) reads the way it does. Verifiability is not satisfied by an assertion of status. It is satisfied by a recitation of specific education and specific experience, followed by a declaration that, because of that education and experience, the appraiser is qualified to appraise that type of property.

## Part Three: Type of property is the question digital assets make hard

Section 1.170A-17(b)(3)(i) defines the type of property as "the category of property customary in the appraisal field for an appraiser to value." The definition is deliberately circular, and the three examples in (b)(3)(ii) are the only guidance on how it operates.

| Example | Facts | Outcome | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|---|
| (1) Coursework | Few professional courses exist in widget appraising; personal property appraisers customarily appraise widgets; Appraiser A has professional-level coursework in valuing personal property generally but none in widgets | Coursework is for the type of property | Where the field does not subdivide, the broader category is the type |
| (2) Experience | Antique appraisers customarily appraise antique widgets; Appraiser B has two years valuing antiques generally | Experience is for the type of property | Custom in the field, not the asset's label, defines the boundary |
| (3) No experience | It is not customary for antique appraisers to appraise *new* widgets; Appraiser C has antique experience but none with new widgets | Not experience in the type of property | Subdivision within a field is real when the field itself treats it as real |

Applied to digital assets, the examples force a question that most engagement conversations skip. Is "digital assets" a type of property in the sense the regulation means, or is it an industry containing several types? The appraisal field's own custom is the test. Business valuation practice, personal property practice, and intangible asset practice each have established bodies of method and each credential their practitioners separately. A bitcoin holding valued by market approach on a deep, continuously quoted market is methodologically close to a publicly traded security. A generative-art NFT collection valued on trait rarity and realized sales is closer to personal property or collectibles. A DeFi liquidity position with impermanent loss exposure and an on-chain redemption mechanism is closer to a partnership interest or a derivative, and valuing it credibly requires modeling that a collectibles appraiser does not do.

The practical consequence is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: an appraiser whose demonstrated experience is in fungible, continuously quoted tokens has not thereby demonstrated experience in NFT collections, and the reverse is also true. Section 1.170A-17(b)(4) requires the appraiser to declare qualification for *the type of property being valued* in each report. A firm that signs both without a documented basis for both is making a representation it may not be able to support, and the declaration it signed is exactly what an examiner will read first.

## Part Four: What "verifiable" requires in the document

Section 1.170A-17(b)(4) makes education and experience verifiable only if two things happen inside the appraisal: the appraiser specifies the education and experience in valuing the type of property, and the appraiser makes a declaration that, because of that education and experience, the appraiser is qualified to make appraisals of that type of property.

That requirement is reinforced by the contents rules in Section 1.170A-17(a)(3)(iv), which require the report to state the appraiser's name, address, and taxpayer identification number; the appraiser's qualifications to value the type of property, including education and experience; and, where the appraiser is acting as a partner in a partnership, an employee, or an independent contractor engaged by someone other than the donor, the name, address, and taxpayer identification number of that firm or person. The regulation wants the signature traceable to an entity with a filing obligation.

There is also the declaration required by Section 1.170A-17(a)(3)(vi), which must appear in the report in substance. It states that the appraiser understands the appraisal will be used in connection with a return or claim for refund, that a substantial or gross valuation misstatement based on the appraisal may subject the appraiser to a penalty under Section 6695A as well as other penalties, and that the appraiser affirms not to have been barred from presenting evidence or testimony before the Treasury Department or the Service under 31 U.S.C. Section 330(c) at any time in the three-year period ending on the date of the appraisal.

That declaration is not boilerplate, and it should not be signed as boilerplate. It is the appraiser's acknowledgement of a specific statutory exposure and a specific disciplinary history, and Part Six explains how literally the Service reads it.

## Part Five: The six exclusions

Section 1.170A-17(b)(5) lists individuals who are not qualified appraisers for the appraised property, without regard to their credentials.

| Cite | Excluded individual | Note |
|---|---|---|
| (b)(5)(i) | Anyone receiving a fee prohibited by Section 1.170A-17(a)(9) | A value-based fee disqualifies the appraiser, not merely the fee |
| (b)(5)(ii) | The donor | — |
| (b)(5)(iii) | A party to the transaction in which the donor acquired the property, including an agent for the transferor or the donor | Narrow exception: property contributed within two months of acquisition and appraised at no more than the acquisition price |
| (b)(5)(iv) | The donee | Includes the charity receiving the gift |
| (b)(5)(v) | Anyone related within Section 267(b) to, or an employee of, an excluded party; married to such a person; or an independent contractor regularly used as an appraiser by such a person who does not perform a majority of appraisals for others in the taxable year | The majority-of-appraisals test is the one that catches captive arrangements |
| (b)(5)(vi) | Anyone prohibited from practicing before the Service under 31 U.S.C. Section 330(c) at any time in the three years ending on the signature date | Three-year lookback, not a current-status test |

Two of these deserve emphasis. The first is the fee rule in Section 1.170A-17(a)(9), which provides that the fee for a qualified appraisal cannot be based to any extent on the appraised value of the property, and specifies that a fee is treated as value-based if any part of it depends on the amount the Service allows after examination. The structure matters: the regulation does not say a contingent fee is unethical. It says the person who charges one is not a qualified appraiser for that property. Contingency destroys status.

The second is the three-year disciplinary lookback. Section 1.170A-17(a)(11) addresses the consequence of a 31 U.S.C. Section 330(c) bar: an appraisal prepared by a barred appraiser is disregarded as to value, but may still be a qualified appraisal if the other requirements are met and the donor had no knowledge that the signature, date, or declaration was false. That is a narrow life raft for an innocent donor and no help at all to the appraiser, who signed a declaration affirming the opposite.

## Part Six: Section 6695A reaches the person who signed

IRC Section 6695A, enacted by Section 1219 of the Pension Protection Act of 2006 and effective for appraisals prepared for returns and submissions filed after August 17, 2006, provides that a person who prepares an appraisal of the value of property, who knows or reasonably should have known that the appraisal would be used in connection with a return or a claim for refund, and where the claimed value on that return or claim results in a substantial valuation misstatement under Section 6662(e), a substantial estate or gift tax valuation understatement under Section 6662(g), or a gross valuation misstatement under Section 6662(h), shall pay a penalty.

Four elements, then, and each is doing independent work. The person must have *prepared an appraisal of the value of property* — a market commentary, an exchange export, or a portfolio printout is not that. The person must have known or reasonably should have known the intended tax use, which is why the Section 1.170A-17(a)(3)(vi) declaration is self-defeating as a defense: the appraiser affirmatively acknowledged the use. The claimed value must appear on a return or claim for refund. And that claimed value must cross one of the Section 6662 thresholds.

Those thresholds are worth setting out because the direction of the error differs by tax type and practitioners routinely conflate them.

| Provision | Tax type | Substantial threshold | Gross threshold | Underpayment floor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Section 6662(e), (h)(2)(A) | Income tax, chapter 1 — value or adjusted basis claimed | 150% or more of correct value | 200% or more of correct value | Portion of underpayment exceeds $5,000 ($10,000 for most corporations) |
| Section 6662(g), (h)(2)(C) | Estate and gift tax, subtitle B — value claimed | 65% or less of correct value | 40% or less of correct value | Portion of underpayment exceeds $5,000 |

The asymmetry is not an accident. An overstated value on an income tax return enlarges a deduction and understates tax; an understated value on an estate or gift tax return shrinks the base and understates tax. Section 6662 measures each in the direction that produces the underpayment. Regulation Section 1.6662-5(f) confirms that the determination is made property by property rather than by aggregating claimed values, and Section 1.6662-5(g) provides that where the correct value is zero, any claimed value is treated as 400 percent or more of the correct amount — a gross valuation misstatement by operation of arithmetic.

One drafting wrinkle is worth noting for anyone briefing an examiner. Regulation Section 1.6662-5(e) still recites 200 percent and 400 percent as the substantial and gross thresholds for chapter 1 property, language that predates the current statute. The operative thresholds are those in Section 6662(e) and (h) as amended, and the Service's own penalty manual states them as 150 percent and 200 percent. Where a regulation and a statute diverge on a number the statute controls, but the divergence is a reason to cite the statute rather than the regulation in any written submission.

## Part Seven: The arithmetic, and the defense the appraiser owns

Section 6695A(b) computes the penalty as the *lesser* of two amounts: the greater of 10 percent of the underpayment attributable to the misstatement or $1,000; or 125 percent of the gross income the person received from preparing the appraisal.

The 125 percent cap is the provision that determines most outcomes in practice, and it is the provision least often discussed. Where a fee is modest relative to the value at issue, the cap is the operative number and the 10 percent calculation is academic.

| | Example A — charitable deduction | Example B — estate inclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Claimed value | $2,000,000 | $450,000 |
| Correct value | $1,000,000 | $900,000 |
| Ratio | 200% of correct — gross misstatement, Section 6662(h) | 50% of correct — substantial understatement under Section 6662(g), not gross because the ratio exceeds 40% |
| Underpayment attributable to the misstatement | $370,000 (37% of the $1,000,000 disallowed deduction) | $180,000 (40% of the $450,000 omitted from the estate) |
| 10% of underpayment | $37,000 | $18,000 |
| Greater of that and $1,000 | $37,000 | $18,000 |
| Gross income from the appraisal | $25,000 | $12,000 |
| 125% of gross income | $31,250 | $15,000 |
| Section 6695A penalty — the lesser of the two | $31,250 | $15,000 |

Both examples land on the cap. That is the honest reading of the statute and it cuts in an uncomfortable direction: a low-fee appraisal carries a low ceiling, which means the penalty's deterrent force is weakest exactly where the fee is smallest relative to the value at stake. Section 6695A is not the only consequence an appraiser faces for a bad number, and any analysis that treats it as the whole exposure is incomplete.

Section 6695A(c) supplies the defense, and its structure is unusual. No penalty is imposed if the person establishes to the satisfaction of the Secretary that the value established in the appraisal was *more likely than not the proper value*. The burden sits on the appraiser. The appraiser is not defending the client's return and is not arguing that the taxpayer had reasonable cause. The appraiser is proving, on its own record, that its number was probably right.

That is why the workfile is the defense and why timeline discipline matters. The contemporaneous market data, the comparable transactions considered and rejected, the liquidity analysis, the modeling assumptions, and the reasoning that connects them are the evidence that a later value conclusion was wrong rather than unsupported. An appraiser whose file consists of a final report and a pricing screenshot has nothing to establish the exception with, and it will be asked to.

## Part Eight: A separate case on a separate clock

The Service's penalty manual, IRM 20.1.12 (revised August 10, 2026), describes how a Section 6695A case actually proceeds, and the sequence is materially different from a taxpayer examination.

The case is opened as a separate and distinct matter from the related tax examination. Facts supporting the penalty are developed during the tax examination and documented in the workpapers, but discussions with the taxpayer, return preparer, or representative are limited to fact development, and the penalty is *not* proposed in the presence of the taxpayer. Generally the appraiser penalty is not proposed until the related examination is closed at the group level — closed agreed, closed with no response after default, in Appeals, or in Tax Court. The appraiser can therefore receive a letter years after the return was filed and long after the client's matter appeared resolved.

On the procedural side, the examiner prepares Form 5345-D with group manager approval, controls the penalty investigation on ERCS using activity code 552, and the case carries an MFT of PB under the appraiser's identifying number. Written supervisory approval under IRC Section 6751(b) must be secured and documented before the first communication of the penalty to the appraiser, and the manual is explicit that group manager approval of the Form 5345-D does not itself satisfy Section 6751(b). That distinction is a real litigation point rather than a technicality. The appraiser is then contacted with Letter 4477, an appraiser appointment letter, and the stated purpose of the appointment is to gather facts on whether the appraiser can establish the more-likely-than-not exception in Section 6695A(c) — the Service may issue an information document request directed at exactly that question. If the appraiser cannot establish the exception, the penalty is proposed; if the examiner concludes no penalty is warranted, Letter 4478 issues. A proposed penalty is communicated by notice — generally a CP 15 — explaining the assessment and appeal rights, and assessment runs through Form 8278. A representative participating in the penalty matter needs a Form 2848 specifying "Civil Penalties," and a Form 8821 must designate "Civil Penalty."

The statute of limitations is the part practitioners most often get wrong. The limitations period on assessment of a Section 6695A penalty expires three years from the date the return was filed, or three years from the date a claim for refund was filed where the appraisal supported a claim. It can be extended using Form 872-AP, a consent specific to the Section 6695A penalty. Extending the statute on the related tax case does *not* extend the appraiser's period. Two clocks run, and one of them belongs to someone who is not a party to the taxpayer's engagement.

## Part Nine: Two gates, not one

Professional practice treats "qualified" as a single idea. It is at least two.

The first gate is USPAP. The Competency Rule requires an appraiser to have the knowledge and experience to complete the assignment competently, and where the appraiser lacks it, prescribes three steps: disclose the deficiency to the client before accepting the assignment, take all steps necessary or appropriate to complete the assignment competently, and describe both the deficiency and the corrective steps in the report. Competency can be acquired through personal study, association with an appraiser reasonably believed to have the necessary knowledge, or retention of others who do. If the assignment cannot be completed competently, the appraiser must decline or withdraw. The Record Keeping Rule separately requires retention of the workfile for at least five years after preparation, or two years after final disposition of a proceeding in which the appraiser testified, whichever is later — and a willful or knowing failure to comply is itself an Ethics Rule violation.

The second gate is Section 1.170A-17(b). It asks a different question, in a different vocabulary, on a different timeline. It asks whether the individual holds qualifying education and experience *for the type of property* as of the signature date. USPAP's Competency Rule explicitly contemplates acquiring competency during the assignment and documenting the acquisition. Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(i) measures the credentials *as of the date the individual signs*. A firm that acquires competency mid-engagement satisfies USPAP and may still fail the regulation, because the regulation does not credit acquisition the way the standard does.

There is a third exposure layered on top for practitioners who are attorneys or CPAs. Circular 230, at 31 CFR Section 10.51(a)(13), treats giving a false opinion knowingly, recklessly, or through gross incompetence as disreputable conduct, and Section 10.50 authorizes censure, suspension, disbarment, or a monetary penalty. Section 10.51(a)(10) separately treats disbarment or suspension from practice as an attorney or CPA by any duly constituted authority as a disciplinary matter before the Service. The Office of Professional Responsibility publishes a searchable list of disciplined practitioners, and 31 U.S.C. Section 330(d) gives Treasury authority to regulate appraisers directly. A CPA who signs an appraisal outside their demonstrated competence is not merely exposed to Section 6695A; the signature can reach their ability to practice.

## Part Ten: A vetting protocol

For attorneys and CPAs retaining an appraiser, the regulation can be converted into questions to ask before the engagement letter is signed and documents to require with the deliverable.

1. **Identify the individual who will sign.** Not the firm. Get the name, and get the education and experience statement that will appear in the report.
2. **Ask which pathway applies.** Coursework plus two years for this type of property, or a designation. Ask for the specific course and the provider, and confirm the provider fits Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(ii).
3. **Ask what "type of property" the firm treats the subject as.** If the answer is "digital assets," press. The regulation's examples turn on custom in the field, and the field's practitioners do not treat a fungible token, a generative-art collection, and a lending position as one discipline.
4. **Match the report's qualification statement to the asset.** Section 1.170A-17(b)(4) requires qualification *for that type*, and a generic statement fails under the preamble's reasoning.
5. **Confirm no value-based fee.** Any component tied to appraised value — including the amount the Service allows — disqualifies the appraiser under Section 1.170A-17(a)(9) and (b)(5)(i).
6. **Run the independence screen.** Donor, donee, party to the acquisition, related persons, employees, and captive contractors are all excluded. Ask about the last three years of engagements for the donor.
7. **Require the (a)(3)(vi) declaration to be signed as written,** and read the education and experience statement rather than the cover page.
8. **Ask what the workfile will contain,** and specifically what will let the firm establish the Section 6695A(c) more-likely-than-not exception if the value is challenged three years later.
9. **Ask for the firm's retention statement.** Five years is the USPAP floor; a Section 6695A statute plus an appeal can outlast it.

A qualified appraisal is a document, but qualified-appraiser status is a fact about a person on a date. The distinction is the difference between a deduction that survives an examination and one that fails before the methodology is ever discussed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who qualifies as a qualified appraiser for an IRS digital asset appraisal?

An individual who satisfies IRC Section 170(f)(11)(E) and Treasury Regulation Section 1.170A-17(b): someone who has qualifying coursework in valuing that type of property plus two or more years of experience valuing it, or a recognized appraiser designation for that type, who regularly performs compensated appraisals, and who is not within any of the six exclusions in Section 1.170A-17(b)(5). The test applies to the individual who signs, as of the signature date.

### Does a digital asset appraiser need a professional designation?

No. Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(i) provides two alternative pathways, and a recognized appraiser designation is only one of them. The coursework-plus-two-years pathway is fully sufficient. What the regulation does require is that whichever pathway is used relate to the type of property being appraised, and that the appraiser state the specific education and experience in the report under Section 1.170A-17(b)(4).

### Can my CPA, my exchange, or the NFT marketplace sign the appraisal?

Generally no. An exchange or marketplace is a party to the acquisition transaction and is excluded under Section 1.170A-17(b)(5)(iii), and an exchange export is not an appraisal at all. A CPA may sign only if the CPA individually satisfies the credentialing test for that type of property and is not otherwise excluded; general tax expertise is not a substitute for experience valuing that category of property.

### What is the penalty if an appraiser overvalues digital assets?

Under IRC Section 6695A the appraiser pays the lesser of two amounts: the greater of 10 percent of the underpayment attributable to the misstatement or $1,000; or 125 percent of the gross income received from preparing the appraisal. It applies where the claimed value produces a substantial or gross valuation misstatement under Section 6662, and the appraiser knew or should have known the appraisal would be used with a return or claim for refund.

### How long does the IRS have to penalize the appraiser?

Three years from the date the relevant return was filed, or from the date a claim for refund was filed, under IRM 20.1.12.5. The period is extended only by Form 872-AP, a consent specific to the Section 6695A penalty. Extending the statute on the taxpayer's examination does not extend the appraiser's period, so the two matters can diverge in time.

### Can an appraiser charge a percentage of the appraised value?

No. Regulation Section 1.170A-17(a)(9) provides that the fee cannot be based to any extent on the appraised value, and specifies that a fee is treated as value-based if any part depends on the amount the Service allows after examination. Section 1.170A-17(b)(5)(i) then provides that an individual receiving such a fee is not a qualified appraiser for that property, which means the appraisal fails as a qualified appraisal regardless of its quality.

## Conclusion: The file the appraiser keeps

The regulatory architecture around digital asset appraisals is often described as a documentation burden placed on the taxpayer. It is better understood as a professional-standards regime with a penalty attached to the professional. Section 1.170A-17(b) defines who may sign. Section 1.170A-17(b)(4) defines what the signature must be able to point to. Section 1.170A-17(b)(5) removes six categories of people from eligibility. Section 6695A makes the appraiser's own number, and the appraiser's own file, the thing at issue in a separate proceeding with a separate clock.

For the attorney or CPA, the useful consequence is that appraiser selection is a substantive step rather than an administrative one. The question is not whether the firm has done crypto work. It is whether a specific individual has qualifying education and experience in the specific type of property, is free of the six disqualifications, will state that education and experience in the report, and will have the contemporaneous record needed to establish that its value was more likely than not correct if asked three years from now.

For the appraiser, the consequence is simpler and less forgiving. The declaration in the report is a representation about exposure and about disciplinary history, the more-likely-than-not exception is proved from the workfile or not at all, and the statute that enforces both does not depend on the client's conduct. Defensible data is built at the intake stage, in an appraisal prepared under USPAP, signed by an appraiser whose qualifications match the property in front of them.
