# Crypto in Trusts: Qualified Appraisal Requirements for Digital Assets Held in Revocable and Irrevocable Trusts

*Published: June 12, 2026 | By Qualified Digital Asset Valuations (QDAV)*

As significant cryptocurrency wealth has accumulated over the past decade, more high-net-worth individuals are turning to trust structures to manage, protect, and transfer their digital assets across generations. Whether funding an irrevocable trust with Bitcoin, designating a spouse as beneficiary of an NFT collection through a Spousal Lifetime Access Trust (SLAT), or administering a credit shelter trust containing Ethereum, one critical requirement consistently emerges: **the need for a qualified, USPAP-compliant appraisal of digital assets.**

This guide examines when and why qualified appraisals are required for digital assets held in trust structures, the specific IRS and USPAP standards that apply, and how proper valuation supports both compliance and fiduciary duty throughout the trust lifecycle.

---

## Why Trust Structures Need Qualified Appraisals for Digital Assets

The IRS has long treated cryptocurrency and other digital assets as **property** for federal tax purposes. IRS Notice 2014-21 established that general tax principles applicable to property transactions apply to virtual currency transactions. This classification has profound implications for trust funding, administration, and distribution.

When an individual transfers digital assets into a trust, that transfer is a taxable event — either a gift (for lifetime transfers to irrevocable trusts) or a transfer that may be included in the estate (for revocable trusts at death). Unlike publicly traded securities with established market prices that can be determined with relative certainty, digital assets present unique valuation challenges that require professional appraisal expertise:

1. **Price volatility** — Cryptocurrency values can fluctuate significantly within hours, making the selection of the correct valuation date critical
2. **Illiquidity** — NFTs and lesser-known altcoins may have thin trading volume, requiring methodology beyond simple exchange price quotation
3. **Trait-based valuation** — NFT collections require trait rarity analysis, comparable sales research, and market condition adjustments
4. **Custody considerations** — The storage method (hot wallet, cold wallet, exchange custody) affects legal classification and valuation approach
5. **Absence of beneficiary designations** — Unlike traditional brokerage accounts, most self-custodied crypto lacks built-in transfer-on-death mechanisms

These factors mean that exchange spot prices alone do **not** constitute a qualified appraisal. The Treasury Department and IRS have consistently held that a qualified appraisal must be performed by a qualified appraiser following USPAP standards — a standard that exchange price snapshots and crypto tax software reports cannot meet.

## Trust Funding: When a Qualified Appraisal Is Required

The most common trigger for a qualified appraisal in the trust context is the **initial funding of an irrevocable trust** with digital assets.

### Gift Tax Threshold and the Qualified Appraisal Mandate

Under IRC §170(f)(11)(E)(i) and Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17, any non-cash gift exceeding $5,000 in value requires a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser. While Section 170 literally addresses charitable contributions, the qualified appraisal requirement echoes through the gift tax framework: when a donor transfers digital assets worth more than $5,000 to an irrevocable trust for the benefit of others, the transfer constitutes a gift subject to Form 709 filing requirements, and the value must be substantiated by a qualified appraisal.

The annual gift tax exclusion for 2026 is $19,000 per donee. However, funding a trust often involves gifts that exceed this threshold — sometimes substantially. When a grantor transfers $500,000 in Bitcoin to an irrevocable trust, the value in excess of the annual exclusion consumes the grantor's lifetime gift and estate tax exemption (currently approximately $13.99 million per individual, though scheduled to sunset under TCJA provisions).

### What the IRS Requires

For a trust funding appraisal to satisfy IRS requirements, it must include:

- A detailed description of the digital asset (token type, blockchain, quantity, wallet address)
- The fair market value (FMV) on the date of transfer
- The specific valuation methodology applied
- The appraiser's USPAP certification and qualification documentation
- The date of the appraisal and the appraiser's signature
- Disclosure of any assumptions or limiting conditions

### Timing Considerations

The **valuation date** for trust funding purposes is the date the digital assets are actually transferred into the trust — not the date the trust document was signed, not the date the appraisal was commissioned. Given the volatility of cryptocurrency markets, an appraisal dated even a few days before or after the actual transfer date may be rejected by the IRS. This makes coordinated timing between the settlement counsel, the appraiser, and the custodian essential.

## Gift Tax (Form 709) and Trust Funding Appraisals

When a grantor funds a trust with digital assets, Form 709 (United States Gift Tax Return) must be filed in the year of the transfer if the value exceeds the annual exclusion. For gifts of property other than cash, **Section B of Form 8283** (Noncash Charitable Contributions) provides the framework for substantiating value — and while Form 8283 is technically attached to the donor's income tax return for charitable gifts, the substantiation requirements for non-charitable gifts to trusts face similar scrutiny.

### Adequate Disclosure Requirements

Treasury Regulation §301.6501(c)-1(f) governs the "adequate disclosure" standard for gift tax purposes. To start the statute of limitations running on a gift tax return, the taxpayer must:

- Describe the transferred property in sufficient detail
- Disclose the FMV and valuation methodology
- Attach a qualified appraisal for gifts of property exceeding $5,000

Without adequate disclosure, the IRS can assess additional gift tax at any time — there is no statute of limitations. This is particularly concerning for digital asset gifts because the IRS may later argue that the value at the time of transfer was higher than reported, triggering additional tax, interest, and potential penalties.

### Valuation Discounts and LLC Wrappers

Some estate planners use a limited liability company (LLC) structure to hold digital assets before transferring the LLC interests to a trust. This approach may allow valuation discounts for lack of marketability and lack of control — potentially reducing the taxable gift. However:

- Retaining too much control over the LLC may cause inclusion back in the estate under IRC §2036(a)
- The IRS closely scrutinizes valuation discounts for entities holding marketable assets
- A qualified appraisal specifically addressing discount factors is essential

## Estate Tax (Form 706) and Step-Up in Basis for Trust-Held Crypto

When the grantor of a trust dies, the digital assets held in certain trust structures become subject to estate tax reporting on Form 706 (United States Estate Tax Return).

### Revocable Living Trusts

For digital assets held in a revocable living trust (the most common trust structure), the assets are included in the decedent's gross estate under IRC §2033. The estate tax return requires:

- A date-of-death FMV appraisal of all digital assets
- A qualified appraisal sufficient to substantiate values reported on Schedule F (Other Miscellaneous Property)
- Documentation of the valuation methodology used

### The Step-Up in Basis

Non-IRA digital assets held in a revocable trust at death receive a **step-up in basis** to the FMV on the date of death (or alternate valuation date, if elected under IRC §2032). This means the pre-death appreciation escapes capital gains tax entirely — a powerful benefit that makes proper date-of-death valuation essential.

**Example:** A grantor purchased Bitcoin at $30,000 per coin. At death, Bitcoin trades at $120,000 per coin. With a properly dated qualified appraisal establishing $120,000 FMV as the date-of-death value, the trust beneficiaries inherit the Bitcoin with a basis of $120,000 — eliminating tax on $90,000 of appreciation per coin.

**Critical distinction:** Digital assets held in a traditional IRA at death do **not** receive a step-up in basis. The IRA itself receives the step-up only for required minimum distribution purposes under the SECURE Act framework, but the character of the assets within the IRA retains their ordinary income treatment.

### Irrevocable Trusts and Estate Inclusion

For most irrevocable trusts (IDGTs, SLATs, GRATs), the trust assets are designed to be **outside** the grantor's taxable estate. However, certain events can trigger inclusion:

- Retained interests (IRC §2036)
- Reversionary interests (IRC §2037)
- Powers to alter beneficial enjoyment (IRC §2038)
- Life insurance trust premium payments within three years of death (IRC §2035)

When estate inclusion occurs, the same qualified appraisal requirements apply — but the valuation date may be the date of death rather than the date of trust funding.

## Trustee Fiduciary Duties and Digital Asset Valuation

Trustees who administer trusts containing digital assets face heightened fiduciary duties under state law (typically the Uniform Prudent Investor Act and the Uniform Trust Code) and federal tax law.

### The Prudent Investor Standard

The Uniform Prudent Investor Act requires trustees to diversify trust investments and monitor asset values. For digital assets, this means:

- **Annual valuation** — Trustees must ascertain the FMV of trust-held crypto at least annually for accountings
- **Documentation** — Valuation methodology must be documented and defensible
- **Impartiality** — Trustees must balance interests between income beneficiaries and remainder beneficiaries, which requires accurate valuations for distribution decisions

### RUFADAA Compliance

The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA), adopted in 47 states, governs a fiduciary's authority to access digital assets. Critically:

- The trustee's authority to access digital accounts must be **explicitly granted** in the trust document
- Without explicit authorization, the fiduciary is presumed to have **no access rights**
- Even with authorization, the fiduciary must follow the custodian's terms of service

This has direct implications for valuation: a trustee who cannot access the digital wallet to verify holdings cannot fulfill the fiduciary duty to properly value trust assets.

### Reporting to Beneficiaries

Trustees must provide regular accountings to beneficiaries. For a trust holding $5 million in various cryptocurrencies and NFTs, a simple statement of "held crypto" is insufficient. Beneficiaries are entitled to:

- A listing of each digital asset held
- The FMV as of the accounting date
- The valuation methodology used
- Records of any transactions during the accounting period

## Types of Trusts Requiring Digital Asset Appraisals

### Intentionally Defective Grantor Trust (IDGT)

The IDGT is one of the most powerful tools for transferring digital asset wealth. The grantor sells or gifts assets to an irrevocable trust, but the trust is "defective" for income tax purposes — meaning the grantor pays income taxes on trust earnings, effectively making additional tax-free gifts to the trust.

**Appraisal requirement:** A qualified appraisal is required at:
1. **Funding** — To establish the value of gifted assets and report on Form 709
2. **Asset sales** — If the grantor sells assets to the trust, FMV on the sale date must be established to avoid prohibited transaction issues
3. **Annual administration** — To determine grantor income tax liability on trust earnings

**Risk:** If the IDGT holds highly volatile crypto and the value declines significantly, the grantor's lifetime exemption may be wasted on assets that ultimately lose value. Proper appraisal methodology helps manage this risk by documenting the valuation basis for potential "portability" claims.

### Spousal Lifetime Access Trust (SLAT)

A SLAT is an IDGT where the spouse is a permissible beneficiary. It allows the grantor to indirectly benefit from trust assets while removing them from the estate.

**Appraisal requirement:** Same as IDGT — qualified appraisal at funding, plus annual updates for administration. The SLAT's valuation rationale must be documented carefully because the spouse's status as beneficiary creates scrutiny under IRC §2036.

### Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT)

A GRAT is particularly well-suited for high-appreciation assets — a perfect description of cryptocurrency in a rising market. The grantor contributes assets to a trust and receives annuity payments for a fixed term. If the trust outperforms the IRS Section 7520 rate (currently 5.4%), the excess passes to beneficiaries free of gift and estate tax.

**Appraisal requirement:** Critical at:
1. **Funding** — GRAT success depends entirely on the starting FMV. An undervalued contribution means the annuity payments are smaller, reducing the amount that must be returned to the grantor and increasing the tax-free pass-through
2. **Annuity payments** — If paid in-kind (with crypto), each payment requires FMV determination on the payment date
3. **Termination** — Distribution of remaining assets to beneficiaries requires date-of-distribution valuation

**Best practice:** A rolling GRAT strategy — funding new 2-year GRATs with annuity payments — requires ongoing appraisal support for each new trust.

### Credit Shelter Trusts (Bypass Trusts)

For married couples, credit shelter trusts preserve each spouse's estate tax exemption. If the first spouse dies with significant crypto holdings, the trust must be funded with a date-of-death appraisal of the digital assets.

**Appraisal requirement:** Date-of-death FMV appraisal is essential for proper funding and to establish the surviving spouse's basis in trust assets.

### Charitable Remainder Trusts (CRTs) / Charitable Lead Trusts (CLTs)

These split-interest trusts provide income to beneficiaries with remaining assets passing to charity. With the June 9, 2026 introduction of H.R. 9173 (Charitable Deductions for Digital Asset Donations Act), digital asset donations to charitable trusts may see simplified appraisal requirements — but as of this writing, the bill has not been enacted, and the existing qualified appraisal requirements remain in full force for trust funding purposes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do I need a new appraisal every year for crypto held in my revocable living trust?**

A: While there is no federal statute requiring annual appraisals for revocable trusts during the grantor's lifetime, trustees have a fiduciary duty to maintain accurate asset values. For irrevocable trusts, annual appraisals are strongly recommended for proper administration, beneficiary reporting, and income tax computation.

**Q: Can my CPA provide the qualified appraisal for trust funding purposes?**

A: No. Under IRC §170(f)(11)(E), a qualified appraiser must be independent — not the taxpayer, the donee, or any party to the transaction. While your CPA can prepare the tax return, they cannot issue the qualified appraisal itself.

**Q: What happens if the IRS challenges the value I reported for a crypto-to-trust transfer?**

A: Without a qualified appraisal, the IRS may: (1) assess additional gift tax plus interest; (2) impose accuracy-related penalties under IRC §6662 of up to 20% (or 40% for gross valuation misstatements); and (3) extend the statute of limitations indefinitely if adequate disclosure was not made. A properly prepared qualified appraisal by a USPAP-certified appraiser provides robust audit defense.

**Q: Does H.R. 9173 eliminate the need for qualified appraisals of crypto in trusts?**

A: No. H.R. 9173 (introduced June 9, 2026) specifically addresses **charitable donations** of digital assets — not trust funding, gift tax, or estate tax valuations. Even if enacted, the qualified appraisal requirement remains for all non-charitable transfers of digital assets exceeding $5,000.

**Q: How should digital assets held in trust be valued for distribution to beneficiaries?**

A: Beneficiary distributions require FMV determination on the actual distribution date. The beneficiary's basis in the distributed assets generally carries over from the trust (with potential step-up adjustments depending on trust type and funding source). A qualified appraisal at distribution documents the FMV for both trust accounting and beneficiary tax reporting.

**Q: Do I need separate appraisals for each type of digital asset in my trust?**

A: A single appraisal engagement can cover multiple digital assets, provided the report identifies each asset, applies appropriate methodology to each category, and documents the aggregate FMV. However, assets with different characteristics — liquid crypto versus illiquid NFTs — require distinct valuation approaches within the same report.
