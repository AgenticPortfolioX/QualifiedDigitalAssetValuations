# The Marital Deduction and Digital Assets: QTIP Elections, Portability, and the Valuation Discipline Section 2056 Demands
*Published: October 20, 2026 | By QDAV*
---

The marital deduction is usually described as a drafting achievement. A well-built instrument passes property to a surviving spouse in a form the statute will accept, and the estate tax at the first death disappears. For most asset classes that description is adequate, because the property being deducted has a value that can be observed, documented, and defended with a brokerage statement.

Digital assets break that description. The deduction is a dollar amount, and the dollar amount is a claim about value on a specific date for property that trades continuously, may not produce income, may not be transferable to the entity that needs to hold it, and may be worth a materially different amount by the time the trust is funded. The drafting can be flawless and the deduction can still fail, because the number underneath it cannot be supported.

This analysis examines the marital deduction as a valuation problem. It works through the terminable-interest limitation that produced the QTIP regime, the income requirement as applied to assets that generate no income, the mechanics of partial and protective elections, the funding-formula question that volatility makes urgent, portability in the $15,000,000 era, the second-death consequences under Section 2044 and Section 2207A, and the evidence a qualified appraisal has to produce for the deduction to hold.

## Why the marital deduction is a valuation problem before it is a tax problem

IRC Section 2056(a) allows an estate to deduct, from the value of the gross estate, an amount equal to the value of any interest in property that passes from the decedent to the surviving spouse, but only to the extent that interest is included in determining the value of the gross estate. Two features of that sentence drive everything that follows. The deduction is measured by *value*, and it is capped by *inclusion*.

For a portfolio of publicly traded securities, both features are trivially satisfied. The value is a closing price, the inclusion is a statement balance, and the deduction follows arithmetically. For a digital asset position, both features require work. Inclusion requires establishing that the decedent actually controlled the wallets and accounts in question — a proposition that turns on key custody, exchange records, and sometimes on discovery that happens after the nine-month filing window has already begun to run. Value requires a determination of fair market value on the applicable valuation date, developed under a professional standard, for an asset whose price at 11:59 p.m. on that date may differ by double digits from its price at noon.

The estate tax consequences of getting this wrong are not symmetric. If the reported value is too high, the estate overpays and may recover through a claim for refund, subject to the period of limitations. If the reported value is too low and the deduction is overstated, the estate faces an accuracy-related penalty under Section 6662 on the underpayment, and the exposure can survive into the second estate because the same number propagates forward. That asymmetry is why the appraisal is not a closing document to be assembled after the return is drafted. It is the evidentiary foundation of the deduction.

## The two deduction paths and the terminable-interest wall

An estate has two ways to obtain a marital deduction for a digital asset position: an outright bequest to the surviving spouse, or a transfer in a form that satisfies the qualified terminable interest property rules.

Section 2056(b)(1) denies the deduction for a *terminable interest*. Where an interest passing to the surviving spouse will terminate or fail on the lapse of time or the occurrence of an event, no deduction is allowed if an interest in the same property passed from the decedent to someone else for less than adequate consideration, and that other person may possess or enjoy the property after the spouse's interest ends. A life estate with a remainder to the decedent's children from a first marriage is the paradigm case. The spouse receives everything for life; the children receive the property at the spouse's death; and because the children's interest is what remains, the deduction is denied. The surviving spouse would otherwise be able to consume the property and defeat the remainder, which is precisely the risk the limitation addresses.

The QTIP regime, enacted in 1981 and codified at Section 2056(b)(7), relieves that result on the executor's election. Where the requirements are met, the entire property is treated as passing to the surviving spouse for purposes of Section 2056(a), and no part is treated as passing to any other person for purposes of the terminable-interest limitation. The decedent controls the remainder. The estate gets the deduction. The price of that combination is that the property comes back into the surviving spouse's gross estate under Section 2044, so the deferral is real but the eventual inclusion is not avoidable.

| Path | Deduction at first death | Control of remainder | Included in survivor's estate | Appraisal exposure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outright bequest to spouse | Section 2056(a), unlimited | None — spouse owns outright | Yes, as the spouse's own property | Value at first death only |
| QTIP election under Section 2056(b)(7) | Full value of the elected property | Decedent's instrument governs | Yes, under Section 2044 | Value at first death and again at second |
| Credit shelter / bypass trust | None — no marital deduction claimed | Decedent's instrument governs | No | Value at first death; basis fixed under Section 1014 |
| Charitable remainder trust | Partial, under Section 2055 | Charity receives remainder | No | Value at funding and at termination |

The table carries the point that matters for digital assets. The QTIP election doubles the number of valuation events: the same position must be valued at the first death to support the deduction and again at the survivor's death under Section 2044. Two appraisal dates, two market conditions, one continuous evidentiary record.

## The appraisal is inside the statute, not beside it

Practitioners sometimes treat the appraisal as an IRS Section 170 concept — a charitable contribution requirement imported into estate work by analogy. That framing understates the estate tax rule. Section 2056(b)(4) directs that in determining the value of the interest passing to the surviving spouse, the effect of the estate tax and any estate, succession, legacy, or inheritance tax on the net value to the spouse must be taken into account, and that where the interest is encumbered or the spouse incurs an obligation imposed by the decedent, that encumbrance or obligation is treated as it would be in determining the amount of a gift.

The consequence is that the marital deduction is not the gross fair market value of the property in every case. It is the net value of what the spouse actually receives. Where the instrument directs that death taxes be paid from the marital share, the deduction shrinks accordingly. Where the digital asset position carries a liability — a loan against pledged collateral, an obligation to deliver tokens under a forward contract, an unsettled purchase — the encumbrance reduces the deductible amount. A qualified appraisal prepared for estate purposes must therefore state the interest appraised, identify encumbrances, and make the net-value computation possible rather than merely reporting a token count multiplied by a price.

That is also why the appraisal standard matters at the estate level even though no regulation uses the phrase "qualified appraisal" in Section 2056. The IRS examines the deduction by asking whether the reported value is the fair market value on the valuation date, supported by a contemporaneous record developed under a recognized professional standard. An appraisal developed and reported under USPAP Standards 9 and 10 answers that question in the form the Service expects. A screenshot and a spreadsheet do not, however accurate the arithmetic.

## What makes a digital asset interest qualified terminable interest property

Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(i) defines qualified terminable interest property as property that passes from the decedent, in which the surviving spouse has a qualifying income interest for life, and to which an election under the paragraph applies. Three elements, and the third is procedural. The first two are substantive and each presents a distinct problem when the property is a digital asset.

| Element | Statutory source | Digital asset problem |
|---|---|---|
| Property passes from the decedent | Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(i)(I) | Custody must be provable; assets on an exchange in the decedent's name pass; assets in a wallet whose keys are lost may not be transferable at all |
| Surviving spouse has a qualifying income interest for life | Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) | Most digital assets produce no income; the trust must be drafted to make the interest real |
| Election applies to the property | Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(i)(III) and (B)(v) | Partial elections must be fractional or percentage shares, and the fraction must be fixed by formula |

The first element is an evidentiary question that estate administrators underestimate. Property passes from the decedent if the decedent had the power to dispose of it. A position held at a custodial exchange in the decedent's account passes under the account agreement and the institution's estate procedures. A position in a self-custodied wallet passes only if the fiduciary can obtain control of the keys, which may require a seed phrase, a hardware device, a multisignature quorum, or a court order compelling a third party to cooperate. Where control cannot be established, the property may be a total loss rather than a deductible bequest, and the estate's problem becomes a Section 2054 casualty or theft deduction question rather than a marital deduction question.

## The income requirement and the unproductive-property problem

Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I) requires that the surviving spouse be entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals, or to a usufruct interest for life. Subclause (II) then requires that no person have a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse, a prohibition that expressly does not reach a power exercisable only at or after the spouse's death. Regulation Section 20.2056(b)-7(d)(1) confirms that the spouse is inside the prohibited class and that a trustee's power to adjust between income and principal, if it satisfies Regulation Section 1.643(b)-1, is not treated as a power of appointment. Regulation Section 20.2056(b)-7(d)(2) supplies the measurement rule: whether the spouse is entitled for life to all of the income from the property is determined under the principles of Regulation Section 20.2056(b)-5(f).

Now apply that to the asset class. Bitcoin held in a trust produces no dividend, no interest, and no distribution. It produces nothing. A trust funded entirely with a non-income-producing asset, whose instrument says the spouse receives "all the income," delivers a right to receive zero dollars per year. The spouse's income interest exists on paper and has no economic content, which puts the deduction at risk on the theory that the spouse is not in substance entitled to all of the income from the property.

The established solution is a spousal power to compel the trustee to make the property productive — to sell or convert unproductive assets and invest in income-producing property. Where the instrument gives the spouse that power, the income interest has content, because the spouse can require the trust to generate the income to which the instrument entitles them. The corollary is that the power must be drafted deliberately, because a trust that says nothing gives the spouse nothing to compel.

Staking changes the analysis but does not resolve it. A trust holding a proof-of-stake position that is actively validating generates rewards, and those rewards are income in the ordinary sense. Whether the trustee must distribute them net of validator commissions and slashing reserve, and whether the trust's allocation between income and principal follows Michigan's Uniform Principal and Income Act (MCL 555.501 et seq.), are questions the instrument should answer rather than leave to litigation. The same analysis applies to lending yield, to tokenized fund interests that distribute, and to NFT positions that generate no cash flow at all.

Three drafting consequences follow. First, a marital trust funded with digital assets needs an express power in the spouse to compel productivity, or an express unitrust conversion power, or both. Second, the instrument should define income to include staking and lending receipts net of direct costs, so the trustee is not left to characterize them under a principal-and-income statute written for timber and mineral interests. Third, the appraisal should state whether the appraised position produces income and, if it does, the basis on which the income stream was measured — because that statement is what allows the fiduciary's allocation decision to be defended later.

## Partial elections, protective elections, and the irrevocability trap

The election itself is made by the executor on the estate tax return and is irrevocable once made, subject to modification on a subsequent return filed on or before the due date including extensions, under Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(v) and Regulation Section 20.2056(b)-7(b)(4). Two mechanics deserve attention when the property is volatile.

The first is the partial election. Regulation Section 20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)(i) permits the election to relate to all or any part of qualifying property, provided that any partial election is made with respect to a fractional or percentage share of the property, so that the elected portion reflects its proportionate share of any increase or decrease in value for purposes of Section 2044 and Section 2519. The regulation adds that the fraction or percentage may be defined by formula.

That sentence is the most useful provision in the area for a volatile asset. An executor who elects a fractional share defined by formula can calibrate the deduction to the exemption without fixing a dollar amount that the market will invalidate before funding. An executor who elects a fixed dollar slice of a bitcoin position has created a number that will be wrong by the funding date, and the correction requires reformation or an amended return.

The second mechanic is the protective election. Regulation Section 20.2056(b)-7(c)(1) allows a protective election where, at the time the return is filed, the executor reasonably believes there is a bona fide issue concerning whether an asset is includible in the gross estate, or concerning the amount or nature of the property the surviving spouse is entitled to receive. The election must identify the specific asset, group of assets, or trust and the specific basis for it, and under Regulation Section 20.2056(b)-7(c)(2) it cannot be revoked once made.

Digital assets generate protective-election questions routinely. A wallet may be jointly controlled and its inclusion litigated. An exchange account may hold positions the decedent claimed beneficially but titled in a family entity. A trust may hold tokens subject to a vesting schedule that determines what the spouse is entitled to receive. In each case the executor faces a choice between claiming the deduction and risking an overstatement, or forgoing it and risking an understatement. A protective election is the instrument that preserves the deduction conditionally, and it is available only if the return identifies the issue with specificity. A generic reservation does not satisfy the regulation.

The timing constraint governs all of it. The return is due nine months after the date of death, extendable six months on Form 4768. For an estate that must locate wallets, obtain custodian cooperation, and commission an appraisal of an illiquid position, nine months is not a generous period, and the extension is not unlimited.

## The alternate valuation date, and why it often does not help

Section 2031 values the gross estate as of the date of death. Section 2032 allows the executor to elect an alternate valuation date six months later, but only where the election decreases both the value of the gross estate and the amount of the tax imposed by Section 2001. That dual requirement is where volatility and the marital deduction collide.

Consider an estate of $20,000,000, all of it bitcoin, all of it passing to a QTIP trust with a full election, in a year with a $15,000,000 basic exclusion. The marital deduction absorbs the entire gross estate, taxable estate is zero, and the tax is zero. Six months later the position has fallen by half. The executor would like to elect the alternate valuation date and report $10,000,000. Section 2032 does not permit it, because the election would reduce the gross estate without reducing the tax — the tax was already zero. The estate reports the date-of-death value, takes a deduction for the same amount, and the surviving spouse's trust holds an asset worth half of the number on the return.

That result is not a defect in the statute so much as a consequence of the deduction's mechanics, and it has a second-death implication. The QTIP property is included in the survivor's estate at its value on the survivor's death under Section 2044, which will be the depressed value. The first estate claimed a large deduction for property that no longer had that value; the second estate includes the smaller number. The net effect across two estates can be favorable, but only if the fiduciary understands the sequence at the time of the first filing.

| Funding formula | How the marital share is fixed | Volatility exposure | Gain on funding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pecuniary amount | A stated dollar figure | Share drifts; requires revaluation and topping up | Yes — satisfaction with appreciated property is a realization event under Kenan |
| Fractional share of residue | A fixed fraction of the residue | Fraction holds; dollar value floats with the market | No — the fraction passes as a share, not in satisfaction of a debt |
| Fractional share with formula | A fraction defined by a formula tied to the exemption | Fraction recalculates; dollar target preserved | No, if drafted as a true fractional share |

## Pecuniary versus fractional funding, and the Kenan problem

A pecuniary marital bequest states a dollar amount: "an amount equal to the maximum marital deduction." A fractional-share bequest states a fraction of the residue. The difference matters more for digital assets than for any other asset class, and the reason is a 1940 case that every estate planner knows and few apply to crypto.

In *Kenan v. Commissioner*, 114 F.2d 217 (2d Cir. 1940), trustees satisfied a pecuniary gift with appreciated securities rather than cash. The court held that the transfer was a realization event: the trustees had used property to discharge a fixed obligation, which is a sale or exchange in substance regardless of the donative intent behind the bequest. The gain is measured from the property's basis to its value at the time of satisfaction.

Now put a stepped-up bitcoin position inside that sequence. The decedent dies owning bitcoin with a basis of $400,000 and a date-of-death value of $8,000,000. Section 1014 steps the basis to $8,000,000. The will leaves a pecuniary marital bequest of $6,000,000, to be funded from the residue. By the time the trust is funded, the position is worth $12,000,000. Satisfying a $6,000,000 obligation with $12,000,000 of property that has a basis of $8,000,000 produces $4,000,000 of gain to the estate, because the estate is using an appreciated asset to discharge a fixed dollar debt. The estate has manufactured taxable income in an entity that is otherwise exempt from income tax on the transfer of appreciated property at death.

A fractional-share formula avoids the problem because the marital trust receives a stated fraction of the residue rather than a payment in satisfaction of an obligation. Nothing is sold; a share is allocated. Revenue Procedure 64-19 provides the drafting guidance practitioners rely on for formula marital bequests, and the fractional approach is the standard recommendation where the residue includes assets whose value moves.

For digital assets there is a further reason to prefer the fractional share. A pecuniary formula requires the fiduciary to value the residue at the funding date, which may be many months after the valuation date and after the market has moved. A fractional formula requires the same valuation but allocates rather than sells, so the estate is not forced into a characterization decision about whether a token transfer to the marital trust was a disposition.

## Portability in the $15,000,000 era

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, set the basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000 per person for decedents dying after December 31, 2025, made the amount permanent, and indexed it for inflation in later years. The top rate above the exclusion remains 40 percent. A married couple therefore has $30,000,000 of combined exclusion before any planning, and the long-anticipated sunset that drove a decade of gift strategies is gone.

Section 2010(c)(4) defines the deceased spousal unused exclusion amount, and Section 2010(c)(5)(A) requires the executor to elect portability on a timely filed estate tax return for the surviving spouse to use it. A QTIP election and a portability election are compatible and frequently used together: the QTIP election produces the deduction at the first death, the portability election preserves the first spouse's own exclusion for the survivor, and the QTIP property is included in the survivor's estate under Section 2044 where both the survivor's exclusion and the DSUE are available.

The mechanics create a specific valuation trap for digital asset estates. Regulation Section 20.2010-2(a)(7)(ii) provides that an executor who is not required to file under Section 6018(a) but files to elect portability is not required to report the value of property eligible for the marital or charitable deduction, provided the value is estimated and the estimate is included in the gross estate. The Form 706 instructions implement this with a table of estimated values, and they warn that the estimates must result from the executor's due diligence and are subject to penalties of perjury.

An estimate of a volatile digital asset position is a weak foundation for a number that will govern the survivor's exclusion for the rest of their life. An estate that estimates a $12,000,000 position at $11,000,000 to stay within the exclusion has created a DSUE computation built on a figure nobody verified, and the Service can revisit it. Where the position is material, the sound practice is to value it properly and let the arithmetic fall where it does.

Timing relief exists but should not be relied on as a plan. Revenue Procedure 2022-32, published in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2022-30, provides a simplified method for estates not otherwise required to file to obtain an extension of time under Regulation Section 301.9100-3 to elect portability on or before the fifth anniversary of the decedent's death. Five years is more time than the nine-month default. It is not a substitute for filing on time, and the relief is unavailable to estates that were required to file.

## Two-death coordination: Section 2044, Section 2207A, and consistent basis

The QTIP election defers tax; it does not eliminate it. Section 2044 includes the elected property in the surviving spouse's gross estate, and the inclusion is measured at the value on the survivor's death, not the value on the first death. That is the provision that makes the second appraisal necessary rather than optional.

Section 2207A supplies the remedy that makes the structure workable. Where QTIP property is included in the surviving spouse's estate, the estate is entitled to recover from the persons receiving the property the portion of the estate tax attributable to the inclusion, unless the instrument directs otherwise. Section 2207A(b) extends the same recovery to a lifetime disposition: if the surviving spouse disposes of the qualifying income interest during life, Section 2519 treats the disposition as a transfer of the entire property, and the recovery right follows.

For digital assets, the recovery right has a practical edge. A QTIP trust holding tokens may distribute them to remainder beneficiaries in kind, and the tax attributable to those tokens may exceed the liquid assets available to the estate. A trustee who distributes before the tax is computed can leave the estate with a recovery right against beneficiaries who no longer hold the asset. The instrument should address the sequencing, and the fiduciary should understand that the appraisal supporting the second-death value is also the document that fixes the recovery amount.

Basis follows the same path. Property included in the survivor's estate receives a basis adjustment under Section 1014, and Section 1014(f) requires that the basis reported by the recipient not exceed the value finally determined for estate tax purposes. Section 6035 enforces that consistency through Form 8971 and Schedule A, which the executor must furnish to beneficiaries and to the Service. A marital deduction built on an appraisal that the Service later adjusts produces a basis report that must be adjusted with it, and the penalties for an inconsistent basis report reach the beneficiary rather than the estate.

## What a digital asset appraisal must establish to carry the deduction

An appraisal supporting a marital deduction is not a price report. It is an evidentiary record of defensible data that answers a defined set of questions at a defined date. The following table sets out what a defensible workfile must hold, mapped to the issue each element addresses.

| Element | What the report must state | Issue it resolves |
|---|---|---|
| Valuation date and time | The applicable date under Section 2031 or Section 2032, and the time of day relied on | Price selection; intraday movement |
| Interest appraised | Whether the subject is the entire position, a fractional interest, or an encumbered interest | Section 2056(b)(4) net-value computation; Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) "specific portion" |
| Custody and control | How the appraiser verified the decedent's control; what was inspected versus represented | Inclusion under Section 2031; the pass-from-decedent element |
| Unit of account | Per token, per lot, or per block, and why the unit matches the obligation | Consistency with the deduction claimed and the funding formula |
| Market selection | Which venues, which pair, which reference rate, and the basis for the choice | Fair market value under the willing-buyer/willing-seller standard |
| Restriction and vesting | Lockups, vesting schedules, transfer restrictions, and the discount applied | Value of the interest that actually passes |
| Income character | Whether the position produces income, and the measurement basis if it does | Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I); trustee allocation |
| Encumbrances | Loans against collateral, delivery obligations, unsettled trades | Section 2056(b)(4) net value |
| Methodology | The approach selected, the approaches considered and rejected, and why | USPAP Standards 9 and 10 development and reporting |
| Certification | Signed certification, appraiser qualifications, and independence | Weight of the report on examination |

Two of these deserve emphasis. The **interest appraised** question is where digital asset appraisals most often fail to support the deduction they were commissioned for. A report that values 40 bitcoin as a single block, at a single price, does not answer the question a partial QTIP election poses, because the election must be a fractional or percentage share of the property under Regulation Section 20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)(i), and the appraisal must value the same interest the election identifies.

The **market selection** question carries the most audit risk in a thin market. A large position in a low-volume token has a quoted price and no depth at that price. An approach that takes the last trade without analyzing depth, spread, or time to liquidate will overstate a position that cannot be sold at the quote. The appraisal has to say what market it chose, why, and what a hypothetical willing buyer would pay for that quantity on that date.

## Fiduciary and professional exposure

The executor who signs the return and the trustee who administers the trust carry different exposures, and both are measured against the same appraisal.

The executor's exposure is computational and procedural. An overstated deduction produces an underpayment, and Section 6662 imposes an accuracy-related penalty on the portion attributable to a substantial valuation misstatement, against which a contemporaneous independent appraisal is the strongest reasonable-cause evidence available. The procedural exposure is the election itself: an election omitted from the return cannot be added after the due date except through the protective-election mechanism.

The trustee's exposure is temporal. Michigan imposes no state estate tax, so the federal analysis governs, but the trustee's duty of impartiality between income and remainder beneficiaries does not disappear because the tax does. A trustee holding a concentrated, non-income-producing position in a QTIP trust makes an allocation decision every year, and the principal-and-income framework is not written for staking rewards, token burns, or airdrops. The instrument, the appraisal, and the trustee's documented reasoning are the record examined if a remainder beneficiary objects.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can cryptocurrency be held in a QTIP trust?** Yes, provided the trust satisfies Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii): the surviving spouse must be entitled to all income from the property, payable annually or more often, and no person may hold a power to appoint any part of the property to anyone other than the spouse during the spouse's life. The asset class is not disqualified; the instrument has to do the work.

**Does bitcoin produce income for QTIP purposes?** No. Bitcoin held passively generates no dividend, interest, or distribution. A trust funded entirely with non-income-producing property needs an express power in the spouse to compel the trustee to make the property productive, or the income interest has no economic content and the deduction is exposed.

**Do I need a qualified appraisal to make a QTIP election?** The statute does not use that phrase for estate tax purposes, but Section 2056(b)(4) requires the value of the interest passing to be determined, and the deduction is examined against that determination. An appraisal developed and reported under USPAP Standards 9 and 10 is the form of evidence the Service expects; an exchange statement is not.

**What is the difference between a pecuniary and a fractional marital bequest?** A pecuniary bequest fixes a dollar amount and is funded by satisfying an obligation. A fractional bequest allocates a share of the residue. Under *Kenan v. Commissioner*, satisfying a pecuniary bequest with appreciated property is a realization event, which is why fractional formulas are preferred where the residue holds volatile assets.

**Can the estate elect the alternate valuation date if the marital deduction eliminates the tax?** Generally no. Section 2032 permits the election only where it decreases both the value of the gross estate and the amount of tax imposed by Section 2001. Where the marital deduction already reduces the tax to zero, the dual-decrease requirement is not met.

**Does portability still matter with a $15,000,000 exclusion?** Yes. Section 2010(c)(4) and (5) allow a surviving spouse to use the deceased spouse's unused exclusion only if the executor elects portability on a timely return. Combined with a QTIP election, portability preserves the first spouse's own exclusion for use at the survivor's death under Section 2044.

**What happens to the QTIP property when the surviving spouse dies?** Section 2044 includes it in the survivor's gross estate at its then-value, and Section 2207A gives the estate a right of recovery against the persons receiving the property for the tax attributable to the inclusion, unless the instrument provides otherwise.

**What does the appraisal have to contain to support a marital deduction?** The valuation date and time, the interest appraised, the custody and control evidence, the unit of account, the market selected and why, any restrictions or encumbrances, whether the position produces income, the methodology and approaches considered, and a signed certification with the appraiser's qualifications.

## The number is the planning

The marital deduction rewards instruments that satisfy a statute and estates that can prove a value. In most asset classes the second requirement is satisfied by a statement. In digital assets it is satisfied only by an appraisal that fixes an interest, a date, a market, and a method, and that survives the second death eighteen months or eighteen years later when the same property is valued again.

Estate attorneys and CPAs working with digital asset estates should treat the appraisal as part of the drafting sequence rather than the filing sequence. The instrument determines what interest must be valued. The election determines what fraction must be supported. The appraisal determines whether the deduction holds. Get the order wrong and the instrument is elegant, the election is timely, and the deduction is still an assertion.
