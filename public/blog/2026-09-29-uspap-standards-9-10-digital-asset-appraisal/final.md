# USPAP Standards 9 and 10: How the Business and Intangible Asset Standards Govern a Digital Asset Appraisal

*Published: September 29, 2026 | By Qualified Digital Asset Valuations (QDAV)*

---

## Introduction: The Question Before the Number

When a CPA, an estate attorney, or a trustee asks whether a digital asset appraisal is "qualified," the conversation almost always turns on credentials and figures. Is the appraiser competent? Is the number defensible? Was the right price used on the right date?

Those are the right questions, but they are not the first question. The first question is which standards govern the assignment at all. That question is usually skipped, and it is the one that quietly decides the rest.

The reason it gets skipped is that most professionals meet USPAP — the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice — in real estate. Real property appraisals are governed by Standards 1 and 2, and that is where the widespread familiarity with the framework begins and ends. But a cryptocurrency, a non-fungible token, or a decentralized-finance position is not real property. It is not tangible personal property either. It is an **intangible asset**, and intangible assets are governed by **Standards 9 and 10** — the development and reporting standards for business and intangible asset appraisals.

That classification is not a technicality. In tax work it is the whole mechanism. The Treasury Regulation that defines a qualified appraisal defines "generally accepted appraisal standards" as **the substance and principles of USPAP**. The regulation does not stand apart from the appraisal standards; it incorporates them. Compliance with Standards 9 and 10 is therefore not a parallel consideration to the qualified-appraisal requirement — it is functionally the same test, applied to the same report, in the same filing.

This is the analysis that follows: why a digital asset appraisal is an intangible asset appraisal, what Standards Rules 9-1 through 9-4 require in development, what Standards Rules 10-1 through 10-3 require in reporting, where the Restricted Appraisal Report becomes a trap, and how the Competency Rule and the profession's newest technology guidance bear on an assignment that reaches into a blockchain.

## Key Takeaways

- **Digital assets are intangible assets, so Standards 9 and 10 govern.** They are not real property (Standards 1–2) and not tangible personal property (Standards 7–8). A report developed under the wrong standard pair is defective before its substance is examined.
- **Treas. Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(2) defines generally accepted appraisal standards as the substance and principles of USPAP.** The qualified-appraisal requirement and the appraisal standards are one system, not two.
- **Standard 9 governs development.** The Standards Rules require recognized methods, problem identification, an accurate description of the interest valued, and analysis of restrictions, control, and marketability — the very factors that dominate digital asset facts.
- **Standard 10 governs reporting.** A business Appraisal Report under Standards Rule 10-2(a) is the report type a filing obligation requires.
- **A Restricted Appraisal Report under Standards Rule 10-2(b) is available only where the client is the sole intended user.** Any return, any Form 8283, and any fiduciary filing names other intended users. The restricted option is therefore unavailable, and the defect cannot be cured after the fact.
- **The Competency Rule is decided before the assignment is accepted.** General business-valuation or real-property credentials do not, by themselves, establish competency for blockchain assets.
- **Advisory Opinion 41 confirms that technology does not move responsibility.** A tool cannot comply with USPAP; only the appraiser can. Output remains information until the appraiser analyzes it.
- **A claimed value is not a valuation.** Exchange screenshots and price printouts are not appraisals, and under the Treasury Regulation they do not substitute for a qualified appraisal.

## Part One: Where Digital Assets Fall in the USPAP Architecture

USPAP is not a single document so much as an architecture. It opens with a Preamble and a set of Definitions, then applies five Rules to all appraisal practice, and finally sets out ten Standards. The Rules — Ethics, Record Keeping, Competency, Scope of Work, and Jurisdictional Exception — establish the environment in which every assignment is performed. The Standards establish the performance requirements specific to each discipline.

The five Rules and their function:

| Rule | What it requires |
|---|---|
| **Ethics Rule** | Impartiality, objectivity, independence, and ethical conduct; no acceptance of an assignment conditioned on a predetermined result or a direction in value. |
| **Record Keeping Rule** | A workfile for every assignment, retained for at least five years after preparation or two years after final disposition of any judicial proceeding in which the appraiser testified, whichever is later. |
| **Competency Rule** | A pre-assignment determination that the appraiser possesses the knowledge and experience to perform the assignment competently, with defined steps when competency is absent. |
| **Scope of Work Rule** | Correct identification of the problem to be solved, and the research and analysis necessary to produce credible assignment results. |
| **Jurisdictional Exception Rule** | A saving clause preserving the balance of USPAP where a portion is contrary to the law or public policy of a jurisdiction. |

The ten Standards then divide by asset class and by function:

| Standards | Discipline | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1 and 2 | Real property | Development and reporting |
| 3 and 4 | Appraisal review | Development and reporting |
| 5 and 6 | Mass appraisal | Development and reporting |
| 7 and 8 | Personal property | Development and reporting |
| **9 and 10** | **Business or intangible asset appraisal** | **Development and reporting** |

### Why digital assets are intangible assets

USPAP's asset-class divisions are categorical. Real property means interests in land. Personal property, in the Standards 7 and 8 sense, means tangible movable property — art, antiques, equipment, collectibles. A token, a coin balance, or a smart-contract position has no physical form. What exists is a set of rights recorded on a distributed ledger and enforceable through protocol rules and contract. That is an intangible asset, and intangible assets are valued under Standards 9 and 10.

The same conclusion follows from the other direction. Many digital asset holdings are not held directly at all. A family limited partnership holding a concentrated token position, an LLC formed to hold an NFT collection, a trust holding a diversified wallet — in each case the property being appraised is an entity interest, the classic subject of a business appraisal. Standalone cryptocurrency and NFTs are intangibles; entity-held digital assets are business interests. Both roads lead to Standards 9 and 10.

### The threshold question: is it an appraisal at all?

There is a second classification that matters more than most practitioners realize. Under USPAP, not every estimate of value is an appraisal. A **calculation engagement** — where the client and the analyst agree in advance on the approaches and methods to be used, and the product is a narrower "calculated value" rather than the analyst's independent conclusion — is not an appraisal, and Standards 9 and 10 do not apply to it. The Appraisal Foundation draws the line at the appraiser's independent judgment: where the analyst is free to select the approaches the facts require and reaches an independent conclusion of value, the assignment is an appraisal and Standards 9 and 10 govern.

This distinction is cold comfort to a taxpayer. A calculation engagement, a crypto tax-software export, or an exchange cost-basis report may all be useful documents. None of them is a qualified appraisal, and none of them is developed or reported under the standards that a filing obligation demands. The threshold question — *is this an appraisal, and if so, under which standards was it developed and reported?* — is the first screen any reviewer should apply to a digital asset valuation.

## Part Two: Standard 9 — Development

Standard 9 establishes the requirements for developing a business or intangible asset appraisal. Its Standards Rules are written in the language of enterprise and ownership interests, which is precisely why they map so cleanly onto digital asset facts once the classification in Part One is accepted.

### Standards Rule 9-1 — the general requirement

Standards Rule 9-1 sets the baseline. An appraiser must be aware of, understand, and correctly employ those recognized methods and techniques necessary to produce a credible appraisal. The Rule then prohibits two failures: committing a substantial error of omission or commission that significantly affects the appraisal, and rendering appraisal services in a careless or negligent manner — including a series of individually minor errors that, in the aggregate, undermine the credibility of the results.

For digital assets this Rule is where the exchange screenshot fails. A quoted price applied to a wallet balance is not a recognized method applied by a competent appraiser; it is an input. The question Standard 9 asks is whether the appraiser selected and correctly applied an accepted approach to the specific interest owned. When the interest is a restricted, vesting, or thinly traded position, a spot price does not describe it.

### Standards Rule 9-2 — problem identification and the characteristics of the interest

Standards Rule 9-2 requires the appraiser to identify, from sources reasonably believed to be reliable, the characteristics of the subject property relevant to the standard of value, the definition of value, and the intended use. Among the characteristics the Rule enumerates:

- the subject business enterprise or intangible asset;
- the interest, equity, asset, or liability to be valued, and the attributes of that interest, including the rights and benefits of ownership;
- buy-sell and option agreements, investment-letter restrictions, restrictive charter or partnership clauses, and similar features that may influence value;
- the extent to which the interest contains elements of ownership control; and
- the extent to which the interest is marketable or liquid.

Every clause in that list has a digital asset counterpart. The "interest" may be a token, an NFT, a fractionalized position, a SAFT, or a membership interest in an entity that holds tokens. The "rights and benefits of ownership" may include governance rights, fee or revenue shares, staking rewards, or redemption rights — or none of these. The "agreements and restrictions" are the lockup schedules, cliff-and-vesting terms, transfer restrictions, and smart-contract mechanics that travel with the asset. The "extent of control" and "extent of marketability" are the questions that separate a freely traded position from a concentrated or restricted one.

An appraisal that fails to identify the interest correctly produces a defensible number for the wrong asset. This is the failure mode most common in digital asset valuation: a competent market analysis of a token, where the actual subject was a *restricted* token, or where the actual subject was an entity interest holding tokens. The number is internally sound and answers a question nobody asked.

### Standards Rule 9-3 — liquidation

Standards Rule 9-3 addresses equity interests with the ability to cause liquidation of the enterprise, requiring the appraiser to investigate whether the business enterprise may have a higher value through liquidation than as a going concern, and — if liquidation is the indicated premise — to value any real or personal property to be liquidated under the appropriate standard.

The Rule is narrow on its face, but the reasoning it embodies recurs in digital asset work. Where the holding is an entity interest, the analyst must consider whether the enterprise is worth more operated or wound up. Where a protocol's treasury controls held tokens, or where governance can trigger redemption, the same inquiry is engaged. The Rule is a reminder that the premise of value is a conclusion to be reached, not an assumption to be inherited.

### Standards Rule 9-4 — methods, data, and the analyses that matter

Standards Rule 9-4 is the development engine. It requires the appraiser to consider all appropriate valuation methods and procedures, to collect and analyze relevant data — including the prices, terms, and conditions of past sales and transfers of similar ownership interests — and, when necessary for credible results, to analyze the effect on value of agreements, restrictions, and the elements of control and marketability. That last obligation is specific: the appraiser must analyze factors such as holding period, interim benefits, and the difficulty of marketing the subject interest.

| Standards Rule | Requirement | Digital asset application |
|---|---|---|
| **9-1** | Employ recognized methods; no substantial error; no negligence | A defensible valuation developed through accepted approaches — not a spot price applied to a balance |
| **9-2** | Identify the interest and its characteristics | Which asset, held how, with what rights, subject to what restrictions, with what control and liquidity |
| **9-3** | Investigate liquidation where the interest can compel it | Entity premises, treasury holdings, redemption rights |
| **9-4** | Consider all appropriate methods; collect and analyze data; analyze restrictions, control, and marketability | The three approaches applied to the interest; on-chain and venue data; lockups, vesting, position size |

The three recognized approaches that Standard 9's method requirement invokes are the asset-based approach (also called the cost approach), the market approach, and the income approach. Digital asset appraisers apply all three, and the selection among them is itself a documented judgment:

- The **market approach** draws on comparable sales and market data, adjusted for the specific interest. For a listed token the raw data is abundant, but the analysis must address free float, trading concentration, and the difference between the marginal trade and the interest held.
- The **income approach** is appropriate where the asset produces identifiable cash flow or where a discounted-cash-flow or capitalization analysis is the best available evidence of value — protocol tokens with fee or revenue participation, staking positions, and certain NFT income streams among them.
- The **asset-based approach** underlies entity-held positions and pre-launch or developmental assets, where value is best reconstructed from the underlying holdings or the expenditures required to reproduce them.

The standard of value for tax work is **Fair Market Value** — the price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither under any compulsion, both having reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts. Where the subject is an enterprise interest, Revenue Ruling 59-60 supplies the eight factors the Service expects to be considered: the nature and history of the business; general economic and industry conditions; book value and financial condition; earning capacity; dividend-paying capacity; goodwill and other intangible value; prior sales of the interest and the size of the block to be valued; and the market prices of comparable publicly traded companies. Translating those factors to a holding entity is part of the development work, and it is documented in the report.

## Part Three: Standard 10 — Reporting

Standard 9 governs how the appraisal is developed. Standard 10 governs how it is communicated, and it is here that the most consequential defect in digital asset appraisals actually occurs.

### Standards Rule 10-1 — the general requirement

Standards Rule 10-1 requires that the report be clear, accurate, and not misleading. USPAP does not prescribe a form, format, or style; it prescribes content. The appraiser may present the work in any structure the assignment warrants, so long as the content required by the Rules is present and the result is not misleading to the intended users.

### Standards Rule 10-2 — the two report types

Standards Rule 10-2 divides reporting into two options:

- **Subsection (a) — the Appraisal Report.** The complete report type. It must address the assignment fully, providing the information required by the Rule in a manner that conveys the appraiser's analysis and reasoning.
- **Subsection (b) — the Restricted Appraisal Report.** A narrower report that may be used **only where the client is the sole intended user** — that is, where there are no intended users in addition to the client.

A business Appraisal Report carries the elements the discipline requires: identification of the client and the other intended users; the intended use; the interest appraised; the standard and definition of value; the valuation effective date and the report date; the scope of work performed; the approaches and methods applied and the reason for omitting any; the analyses and reasoning supporting the conclusion; all assumptions and limiting conditions; any extraordinary assumptions or hypothetical conditions; and a signed certification.

| Element | Appraisal Report — SR 10-2(a) | Restricted Appraisal Report — SR 10-2(b) |
|---|---|---|
| Permitted use | Any assignment, including filings and multi-user reliance | Only where the client is the **sole** intended user |
| Identification of intended users | Required — all of them | Client only |
| Presentation of scope of work | Full description | State the scope of work |
| Supporting data and reasoning | Presented in the report | May be retained in the workfile |
| Extraordinary assumptions / hypothetical conditions | Must be stated and conspicuous | Must be stated and conspicuous |
| Certification | Required | Required |
| Workfile | Must support the report | Must be sufficient to produce a full Appraisal Report |

### The restricted-report trap

An appraiser who delivers a Restricted Appraisal Report for a tax filing has made an error that cannot be repaired by attaching more pages. Standard 10 authorizes the restricted form only where the client is the sole intended user. In tax work the client is almost never the sole intended user. A gift-tax return is filed with the Service; a Form 8283 Section B appraisal accompanies a return that the Service will examine; an estate-tax appraisal may be relied upon by the personal representative, the beneficiaries, and the examining agent; an annual SDIRA attestation is relied upon by the custodian and, in substance, by the plan. Each of those is an intended user.

The consequence is categorical. Where intended users exist beyond the client, the report must be a full Appraisal Report. A Restricted Appraisal Report issued for a filing obligation is not a shortened qualified appraisal; it is a report delivered under a form the assignment did not permit. Reviewers look for precisely this, because it is objective on the face of the document and it does not depend on the value conclusion being wrong.

### Standards Rule 10-3 — certification

Standards Rule 10-3 requires the appraiser to sign the report, or authorize the use of a signature, to certify recognition and acceptance of USPAP responsibilities. The certification is not a formality: it carries the appraiser's representations as to independence, the absence of a contingent fee, the accuracy of the factual statements, and the appraiser's personal conduct of the assignment. For digital asset work, the certification is also where the appraiser's competency representation lives — which connects directly to Part Five.

The Record Keeping Rule operates alongside Standard 10. The appraiser must retain the workfile — data, analyses, and the basis for the conclusion — for at least five years after preparation, or two years after final disposition of any judicial proceeding in which the appraiser testified about the assignment, whichever period expires later. In a digital asset engagement the workfile is where the on-chain evidence, the venue data, the free-float analysis, and the discount support reside.

## Part Four: The Regulatory Bridge

The reason Standards 9 and 10 matter to a tax filing is not a matter of professional custom. It is written into the regulation.

Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17(a)(1) defines a qualified appraisal as an appraisal document prepared by a qualified appraiser in accordance with **generally accepted appraisal standards**. Paragraph (a)(2) then defines exactly what that term means: **the substance and principles of the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice**, as developed by the Appraisal Standards Board of the Appraisal Foundation.

That single definition collapses the distance between the profession's standards and the tax requirement. A qualified appraisal under IRS Section 170 is, by the regulation's own terms, an appraisal prepared in accordance with the substance and principles of USPAP. For a digital asset — an intangible asset — the standards whose substance and principles must be followed are Standards 9 and 10.

Paragraph (a)(3) then specifies what a qualified appraisal must contain, including a description of the property in sufficient detail that a person not generally familiar with the type of property can ascertain that the appraised property is the contributed property; the valuation effective date; the terms of any agreement; the appraiser's qualifications; and the prescribed appraiser declaration. The overlap with the content requirements of a Standard 10 Appraisal Report is not coincidental — it is the same architecture, one layer statutory and one layer professional.

| Standard 10 / §1.170A-17(a)(3) element | Where it lives |
|---|---|
| Description of the property sufficient to identify it | SR 10-2(a) report content; §1.170A-17(a)(3)(i)(A) |
| Valuation effective date | SR 10-2(a); §1.170A-17(a)(3)(i)(C); §1.170A-17(a)(5) |
| Standard and definition of value | SR 10-2(a); §1.170A-17(a)(3) (approach and method stated) |
| Appraiser's qualifications | SR 10-2(a); §1.170A-17(a)(3) and (b)(4) |
| Appraiser's declaration and signature | SR 10-3 certification; §1.170A-17(a)(3) declaration |
| Form 8283 Section B | The donor's reporting layer; must reconcile to the appraisal |

The parallel structure of the disqualification rules is equally instructive. Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17(b)(5) provides that certain persons are not qualified appraisers: an individual who receives a fee prohibited by paragraph (a)(9) — a fee based to any extent on the appraised value; the donor; a party to the transaction in which the donor acquired the property; the donee; related persons and employees; and any individual barred from practice before the Service under 31 U.S.C. §330(c) at any time during the three-year period ending on the date the appraisal is signed. The USPAP Ethics Rule independently bars compensation contingent on a predetermined result or a direction in value. Two systems, one principle.

Beyond the substantive regulation, the Service has proposed to extend the conformance requirement explicitly. On December 26, 2024, the Treasury Department and the Service published proposed amendments to Circular 230 that would create a new subpart D, including proposed §10.61, requiring appraisals submitted in an administrative proceeding before the Service to conform to the substance and principles of USPAP or of the International Valuation Standards. That rule is proposed and not final as of this writing, but the direction is clear: the standards of the appraisal profession are being drawn closer to the practice rules of the tax profession, not further apart.

Two further provisions complete the picture for digital assets. IRC Section 170(f)(11)(C) requires a qualified appraisal for a contribution of property — other than publicly traded securities — with a claimed value above $5,000. For digital assets, Chief Counsel Advice 202302012 concluded that cryptocurrency is not a publicly traded security for that purpose and that exchange-reported values do not substitute for a qualified appraisal. The advice may not be used or cited as precedent under IRC §6110(k)(3), but its reasoning aligns with the regulation. And IRC §6662 imposes an accuracy-related penalty of 20 percent on a substantial valuation misstatement and 40 percent on a gross valuation misstatement — with §6664's reasonable-cause and good-faith defense turning, in practice, on whether the valuation was developed and reported in a manner the profession recognizes. A Standards 9 and 10 report, with a contemporaneous workfile, is what that defense is built on.

## Part Five: The Competency Rule and Digital Assets

The Competency Rule sits above the Standards, and it is decided before the assignment is accepted. An appraiser must determine, prior to agreeing to perform an assignment, that he or she can perform it competently — meaning the technical skills, experience, and knowledge specific to the assignment. Where a deficiency exists, the Rule requires the appraiser to disclose it, take steps to acquire the necessary competency, associate with someone who has it, or decline or withdraw from the assignment.

Digital assets are a competency trap for the otherwise-credentialed. A certified general real property appraiser, or an experienced business valuer, may hold impeccable credentials and still lack the specific knowledge a blockchain assignment requires: how tokens are held and custodied; how protocol and smart-contract mechanics create transfer restrictions; how on-chain data is read and verified; how free float differs from reported circulating supply; how vesting schedules under grant agreements interact with §83; and how to value an NFT or an illiquid protocol token without a reliable comparable market.

The Rule's structure gives the practitioner a usable test. Competency is not a general reputation, it is a match between the assignment's requirements and the appraiser's specific knowledge. An engagement to value a concentrated, entity-held token position with voting rights and a lock-up requires a different competency than an engagement to value a liquid, freely transferable coin balance. The report's certification represents that the competency was present. The practical screen for evaluating that representation — credentials, methodology disclosure, and red flags — is the subject of a separate analysis in this series, and it is the right companion to this one.

## Part Six: Technology, AO-41, and Non-Delegable Judgment

Digital asset appraisal is inherently technology-mediated. On-chain analytics, venue data, order-book depth, automated valuation models, and increasingly generative tools all enter the analysis. On April 23, 2026, the Appraisal Standards Board adopted Advisory Opinion 41, *Use of Technology in an Appraisal or Appraisal Review Assignment*. AO-41 is guidance, not a new standard; it illustrates how existing USPAP obligations — the Ethics, Competency, Scope of Work, and Record Keeping Rules — apply when technology is used.

AO-41's central principle is that the appraiser remains in control. A tool cannot comply with USPAP and cannot be held liable for a violation; only the appraiser can. The Advisory Opinion sets out a four-step framework:

1. **Evaluate the tool** — its purpose, assumptions, and limitations, to the extent necessary for the assignment.
2. **Evaluate the data** — whether the inputs are reliable and appropriate.
3. **Perform the analysis independently** — the appraiser's own judgment, not the tool's output.
4. **Evaluate the credibility of the output** — whether the result is accurate, relevant, and appropriate for the assignment.

Two distinctions in AO-41 matter for digital asset work. The first is between **tool output and assignment results**. Output produced by an AVM, a regression model, or an AI system remains information until the appraiser analyzes it, determines its relevance, and decides whether to rely upon it. Assignment results are the appraiser's own opinions and conclusions. In a digital asset appraisal the raw material is heavily tool-derived, which makes the distinction decisive rather than academic.

The second is the **disclosure and workfile expectation**. USPAP does not require disclosure of every tool used merely because it was used; disclosure is required where omission would mislead intended users. But where a tool materially contributed to an analysis or a conclusion, the appraiser may need to explain how it was used and how its output was incorporated — and the workfile must contain sufficient documentation to support the appraiser's conclusions and demonstrate compliance. For digital assets, that means the on-chain evidence, data sources, model inputs, and the appraiser's analysis of them belong in the file.

AO-41 does not change the analysis in this post. It reinforces it. The obligations run from the Rules and from Standards 9 and 10; technology changes the inputs, not the accountability.

## Part Seven: The Reviewer's Screen

A CPA, attorney, fiduciary, or donor who receives a digital asset appraisal can run a short screen before relying on it. The seven questions below map directly to what Standards 9 and 10 and the qualified-appraisal regulation require.

1. **Is it an appraisal?** An independent conclusion of value, or a client-directed calculation? Only the former is developed under Standards 9 and 10, and only the former supports a qualified appraisal.
2. **Was the right interest identified?** The token, the entity interest, the restricted position — whatever actually transferred. The value must attach to the interest that changed hands.
3. **Is the development sound?** A recognized approach, correctly applied, with the data and reasoning disclosed — not a spot price multiplied by quantity.
4. **Are restrictions, control, and marketability analyzed?** Lockups, vesting, transfer restrictions, position size, and free float. Standards Rule 9-4 requires the analysis where it affects credibility.
5. **Which report type is it?** A filed report must be a full Appraisal Report. If the cover identifies it as a Restricted Appraisal Report, the assignment was reported in a form it did not permit.
6. **Is there a signed certification and a declared appraiser qualification?** Standards Rule 10-3 and §1.170A-17(b)(4) require both.
7. **Is the fee structure compliant?** A fee based to any extent on the appraised value disqualifies the appraiser for charitable work under §1.170A-17(a)(9) and (b)(5)(i), and offends the Ethics Rule everywhere else.

An appraisal that satisfies all seven is defensible data in the fullest sense — developed, reported, and documented in a form that survives examination. One that fails any of them has a problem that no amount of favorable market data will fix.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which USPAP standards apply to a digital asset appraisal?**
Standards 9 and 10. They establish the requirements for developing and reporting business or intangible asset appraisals, and digital assets are intangible assets. Real property appraisals are governed by Standards 1 and 2, and tangible personal property appraisals by Standards 7 and 8. Digital assets fall in neither category.

**Why are digital assets treated as intangible assets rather than personal property?**
Because they have no physical form. What exists is a set of rights recorded on a distributed ledger and defined by protocol and contract — governance rights, transfer rights, and claims on value. USPAP's personal property standards address tangible movable property such as art, equipment, and collectibles. Intangible rights, and entity interests that hold them, are appraised under Standards 9 and 10.

**What does Standard 9 require in developing a digital asset appraisal?**
It requires the appraiser to employ recognized methods and techniques, to avoid substantial errors and careless practice, to identify the characteristics of the interest being valued — including the rights of ownership, any agreements and restrictions, the extent of control, and the extent of marketability — and to consider all appropriate valuation methods while analyzing the effect of restrictions and liquidity on value.

**What is the difference between an Appraisal Report and a Restricted Appraisal Report?**
An Appraisal Report under Standards Rule 10-2(a) presents the full analysis, identifies the client and all intended users, and may be relied upon by parties other than the client. A Restricted Appraisal Report under Standards Rule 10-2(b) may be used only where the client is the sole intended user and omits supporting detail from the report, retaining it in the workfile.

**Can a crypto appraisal be delivered as a Restricted Appraisal Report?**
Not where the appraisal supports a tax filing or any other obligation that brings in additional intended users. A return filed with the Service, a Form 8283 Section B appraisal, an estate or gift filing, and an SDIRA attestation relied upon by a custodian each involve intended users beyond the client. In those settings the report must be a full Appraisal Report, and the restricted form is not available.

**How does USPAP relate to the IRS qualified appraisal requirement?**
Directly. Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17(a)(2) defines "generally accepted appraisal standards" — the standard a qualified appraisal must follow — as the substance and principles of USPAP. For a digital asset, that means the substance and principles of Standards 9 and 10. The qualified-appraisal requirement and the appraisal standards are one system.

**Does USPAP compliance guarantee that the Service will accept the value?**
No. USPAP compliance goes to the process — the credibility of how the value was developed and reported — not to the precision of the conclusion. A compliant appraisal can still be examined and challenged on its inputs. What compliance establishes is that the valuation was performed the way the profession recognizes, which is what the reasonable-cause and good-faith analysis under IRC §6664 turns on.

**What does Advisory Opinion 41 change?**
Nothing in the requirements, and everything in the record. AO-41, adopted April 23, 2026, confirms that using technology — spreadsheets, automated valuation models, machine learning, or generative tools — does not reduce the appraiser's responsibility. The appraiser must evaluate the tool and its data, perform the analysis independently, and assess the credibility of the output. Disclosure is required where omission would mislead, and the workfile must document the output relied upon.

## Conclusion: One Chain of Compliance

A digital asset appraisal is a chain: classification, development, reporting, and regulation. Each link depends on the one before it. The asset must be correctly classified as an intangible asset, which determines that Standards 9 and 10 govern. Standard 9 must be satisfied in development. Standard 10 must be satisfied in reporting — in the right report type, with a signed certification. And the whole chain must satisfy the Treasury Regulation that makes an appraisal qualified, which by its own terms requires the substance and principles of USPAP.

Break any link and the appraisal fails, regardless of how sound the resulting number looks. The most common breaks are also the least visible: a competent valuation of the wrong interest, a defensible analysis reported in the wrong form, a credential that does not match the assignment. None of them announce themselves on the face of the document, and all of them are avoidable when the standards are applied deliberately from the start.

For the attorney, CPA, fiduciary, or donor whose filing depends on the conclusion, the practical lesson is a single question to ask of any appraisal in hand: which standards governed this, and can the appraiser show it. For the digital asset holder, the lesson is that a qualified appraisal is not a document to be assembled after the fact. It is the product of an engagement scoped, developed, and reported under the standards that apply to the asset — which, for digital assets, means Standards 9 and 10.
