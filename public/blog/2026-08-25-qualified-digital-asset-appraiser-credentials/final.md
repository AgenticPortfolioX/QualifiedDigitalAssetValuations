# How to Verify a Qualified Appraiser for Digital Assets: IRS Credential Standards, USPAP Competency, and Red Flags

*Published: August 25, 2026 | By QDAV*

## The Question Every Advisor Is Now Asking

In the current compliance environment, a client's estate plan, charitable contribution, or self-directed retirement account can turn on a single document: the appraisal. And the appraisal turns on a single person: the appraiser who signs it. The IRS does not accept a valuation merely because a report exists. It accepts — and defends against challenge — a **Qualified Appraisal** prepared by a **qualified appraiser** who meets standards the law defines with considerable precision.

This is not a theoretical concern. Form 1099-DA broker reporting is now in its first full reporting cycle, the IRS has expanded its digital asset examination priorities, and valuation misstatements remain a reliable source of audit revenue. For estate attorneys, CPAs, trust officers, and high-net-worth individuals holding cryptocurrency, NFTs, or DeFi positions, the practical question is no longer "what is my crypto worth?" It is "who is qualified to tell the IRS what it is worth — and how do I verify that person's credentials before I rely on their work?"

This guide answers that question. It walks through the statutory definition of a qualified appraiser, the Treasury regulations that implement it, the USPAP Competency Rule that governs professional practice, the credential landscape, the individuals the law disqualifies, and the practical vetting checklist every advisor should run before engaging a digital asset appraiser. The goal is not to persuade you to hire any particular firm. It is to give you the standards by which you can evaluate every firm — including ours.

## Why the Appraiser's Identity Is Now a Compliance Issue

Ten years ago, a taxpayer holding Bitcoin could reasonably rely on an exchange statement or a price snapshot when reporting a gift, an estate, or a donation. The IRS has closed that door. In CCA 202302012, the IRS concluded that a charitable contribution of cryptocurrency exceeding $5,000 requires a qualified appraisal, and that the value reported by an exchange does not substitute for one. The same logic runs through the estate and gift provisions: Form 706 and Form 709 require the executor or donor to report **Fair Market Value** at a specific valuation date, and the IRS can — and does — challenge valuations that rest on unsupported price data.

The consequence is a compliance chain with three links:

1. **The asset** must be properly identified and documented (ownership, acquisition history, cost basis, transfers).
2. **The valuation** must be performed using professional methodology that yields a defensible Fair Market Value.
3. **The appraiser** must be qualified — by education, experience, and credential — to value that specific type of property, and must perform the work in accordance with generally accepted appraisal standards.

Most of the published guidance addresses links one and two. This post addresses link three, which is the one that is most often ignored and most often fatal. An appraisal prepared by an unqualified individual is not a qualified appraisal, no matter how thorough the analysis appears. The IRS can disregard it, the deduction or valuation can be denied, and penalties under IRC Section 6662 — 20 percent for a substantial valuation misstatement, 40 percent for a gross misstatement — can follow. The appraiser's identity is not paperwork. It is the foundation of the entire position.

## The Statutory Definition: IRC Section 170(f)(11)(E)

The starting point is the Internal Revenue Code itself. Section 170(f)(11)(E)(ii) defines a qualified appraiser as an individual who meets three requirements:

1. **Credential or education.** The individual has earned an appraisal designation from a recognized professional appraiser organization, or has otherwise met minimum education and experience requirements set forth in Treasury regulations.
2. **Regular practice.** The individual regularly performs appraisals for which the individual receives compensation.
3. **Other requirements.** The individual meets such other requirements as the Secretary prescribes in regulations or other guidance.

That is the baseline. Then the statute adds a second, stricter layer for the specific appraisal at issue. Under Section 170(f)(11)(E)(iii), an individual is **not** treated as a qualified appraiser with respect to any specific appraisal unless:

- **(I)** the individual demonstrates **verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property** subject to the appraisal; and
- **(II)** the individual has not been **prohibited from practicing before the IRS** under 31 U.S.C. Section 330(c) at any time during the three-year period ending on the date of the appraisal.

Two implications deserve emphasis. First, the statute contemplates that a credential alone is not enough: the appraiser must also demonstrate verifiable education and experience in the **type of property** being valued. Second, the statute operates at the level of the **individual**, not the firm. A firm may market "qualified appraisal services," but it is the individual who signs the appraisal who must satisfy the test. When you engage a firm, you must know exactly which individual will perform and sign the work.

### The "Type of Property" Problem

The phrase "type of property" is the hinge on which the whole digital asset analysis turns. The Treasury regulation defines it as "the category of property customary in the appraisal field for an appraiser to value" (26 CFR 1.170A-17(b)(3)(i)), and illustrates the concept with a telling example: an appraiser experienced in antiques is not automatically experienced in a different category of goods. The regulation is explicit that experience in one category does not transfer to another.

This is the single most important concept for anyone hiring a digital asset appraiser. A real estate appraiser's license, a business valuation credential, or a decades-long career appraising fine art does not, by itself, qualify an individual to value a cryptocurrency portfolio, an NFT collection, or a DeFi position. Those are distinct categories of property with distinct markets, distinct evidence standards, and distinct analytical challenges. The appraiser must show verifiable education and experience **in the digital asset category itself** — not in adjacent categories.

## The Regulatory Tests: 26 CFR 1.170A-17(b)

The Treasury regulations flesh out the statutory requirements. Under 26 CFR 1.170A-17(b)(1), a qualified appraiser is an individual with verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property for which the appraisal is performed. Paragraph (b)(2) then provides two alternative paths to satisfy that standard:

| Path | Requirement | Practical Meaning |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **A — Coursework + Experience** | Successfully completed professional or college-level coursework in valuing the type of property **and** two or more years of experience valuing the type of property | The education must be from a qualifying source (an educational organization described in Section 170(b)(1)(A)(ii), a generally recognized professional trade or appraiser organization, or an employer apprenticeship program), and the experience must be in the digital asset category, not a general valuation practice |
| **B — Recognized Designation** | Earned a recognized appraiser designation for the type of property | A "recognized appraiser designation" is a designation awarded by a generally recognized professional appraiser organization **on the basis of demonstrated competency** (26 CFR 1.170A-17(b)(2)(iii)) |

Two points about these paths are commonly misunderstood.

**First, the designation path is not a free pass.** The regulation requires that the designation be awarded "on the basis of demonstrated competency." A credential purchased through a weekend course or conferred without examination does not meet that standard, regardless of how official the certificate looks. The organization must be a generally recognized professional appraiser organization, and the designation must reflect demonstrated competency in the discipline.

**Second, the coursework-plus-experience path is demanding.** The coursework must be professional or college-level, from a qualifying educational source, and specifically in valuing the type of property. The experience requirement is two or more years **in the type of property** — which, for digital assets, is a genuinely short track record in an industry that only matured in the last decade. When you are evaluating an appraiser, ask directly: which path do you satisfy, and what specific coursework and what specific engagements support it?

### The Verifiability Requirement

Paragraph (b)(4) adds a documentation requirement that turns a private credential into a legally operative fact. Education and experience in valuing the type of property are "verifiable" only if the appraiser:

1. **specifies** in the appraisal the appraiser's education and experience in valuing the type of property, and
2. **declares** in the appraisal that, because of that education and experience, the appraiser is qualified to make appraisals of the type of property being valued.

This is why a one-page letter from a self-described "crypto valuation expert" is not a qualified appraisal. The report itself must contain the appraiser's education and experience, tailored to the digital asset category, plus the qualification declaration. When you review an appraisal, these elements should be present on the face of the document — not available on request.

## Qualified, Certified, or Licensed: The Credential Landscape

The vocabulary of appraiser credentials confuses even experienced advisors, so it is worth being precise.

**State licensing applies to real property.** In the United States, real property appraisers are licensed or certified by the states under a framework established by Title XI of the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989 (FIRREA). Titles such as Licensed Residential Appraiser, Certified Residential Appraiser, and Certified General Appraiser describe authority to appraise real estate for federally related transactions. None of these licenses authorize or qualify an individual to appraise digital assets. A state license is evidence of competence in real property valuation — and nothing more.

**Personal property and business valuation appraisers are credentialed through voluntary professional organizations.** The principal organizations include:

| Organization | Designations | Relevance to Digital Assets |
|--------------|--------------|------------------------------|
| American Society of Appraisers (ASA) | Accredited Senior Appraiser (ASA), Accredited Member (AM) | Personal property and business valuation disciplines; rigorous peer-reviewed designation process |
| AICPA | Accredited in Business Valuation (ABV) | Business valuation focus; relevant to crypto enterprises, not necessarily to individual tokens or NFTs |
| NACVA | Certified Valuation Analyst (CVA) | Business valuation focus |
| Appraisers Association of America (AAA) | Certified Appraiser of Personal Property | Personal property; relevant methodology parallels for collectibles and digital art |
| International Society of Appraisers (ISA) | ISA CAPP / ISA AM | Personal property |
| RICS | MRICS | International property/valuation credential |

**For digital assets, the operative question is not which letters follow a name — it is whether the individual satisfies the Section 170(f)(11)(E) and 1.170A-17(b) tests for the digital asset category.** A designation from a generally recognized professional appraiser organization is strong evidence under the regulation. But it must be paired with verifiable education and experience in valuing digital assets specifically, because that is the statutory requirement for the specific appraisal at issue.

A practical consequence: the digital asset appraisal field is young, and very few credentialed appraisers have both a recognized designation **and** a deep digital asset practice. That scarcity is exactly why the vetting process matters. The advisor who assumes every firm advertising "crypto appraisal" employs individuals who satisfy the statutory test is making a dangerous assumption.

## The USPAP Competency Rule: The Professional Standard Behind the Statute

The statutory phrase "generally accepted appraisal standards" is defined in the regulation to mean "the substance and principles of the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, as developed by the Appraisal Standards Board of the Appraisal Foundation" (26 CFR 1.170A-17(a)(2)). In plain terms: a qualified appraisal is a USPAP-compliant appraisal. That makes USPAP's rules the professional law of the engagement.

The most directly relevant rule is the **Competency Rule**. USPAP requires that an appraiser be competent to perform the assignment. Competency means the appraiser has the knowledge and experience to:

- identify the problem to be solved;
- determine the appropriate scope of work; and
- complete the assignment correctly.

Critically, the Competency Rule requires the appraiser to determine that they are competent **before accepting the assignment**. If the appraiser lacks the necessary knowledge or experience, the rule permits the appraiser to obtain it through research, education, or experience — but only if the appraiser discloses the lack of competency to the client **before** accepting the assignment, and takes the steps necessary to become competent **before completing** the assignment.

The Competency Rule has direct consequences for digital asset work:

- **Asset-class competence.** An appraiser competent in Bitcoin does not automatically satisfy the rule for an NFT collection, a DeFi liquidity position, or a token with no active market. Each is a distinct property type with distinct evidence and analytical requirements.
- **Market competence.** Appraising digital assets requires understanding market microstructure: trading volumes, liquidity, bid-ask spreads, wash trading, collection floors, trait rarity, and the difference between exchange price discovery and a defensible opinion of value.
- **Approach competence.** The market approach, income approach, and cost approach each require specialized knowledge in the digital asset context — which the appraiser must demonstrate, not merely claim.

The current USPAP edition in effect is the 2026–2027 edition, effective January 1, 2026. Appraisers are required to comply with the edition in effect as of the assignment. When you engage a digital asset appraiser, you should confirm, in writing, that the engagement will be performed in accordance with USPAP — and that the individual performing the work satisfies the Competency Rule for the specific assets involved.

### USPAP Advisory Opinion 41 and Technology-Assisted Appraisal

USPAP Advisory Opinion 41 (AO-41), issued by the Appraisal Standards Board, addresses the use of technology in appraisal practice — including the use of automated valuation tools, algorithmic analysis, and blockchain-sourced data. For digital asset appraisal, AO-41 is directly relevant: it confirms that technology can and should inform the analysis, but it does not displace the appraiser's professional judgment or the appraiser's responsibility for the opinion. An appraisal built on automated pricing data is still an appraisal that must be performed by a qualified appraiser, in accordance with USPAP, with a documented methodology and a signed certification. The tool does not make the appraiser; the appraiser makes the appraisal.

## Who Cannot Be Your Appraiser: Disqualification and Prohibited Fees

The law is as specific about who cannot serve as a qualified appraiser as it is about who can. Under 26 CFR 1.170A-17(b)(5), the following individuals are **not** qualified appraisers for the appraised property:

1. **The donor** of the property.
2. **A party to the transaction** in which the donor acquired the property — for example, the individual who sold, exchanged, or gave the property to the donor, or any agent of the transferor or donor (with a narrow exception for property contributed within two months of acquisition whose appraised value does not exceed its acquisition price).
3. **The donee** of the property — including, by extension, a charity that "values" the donated crypto to issue a receipt. As tax practitioners have noted, a charity's valuation for its own records does not make the charity a qualified appraiser for the donor's deduction.
4. **Related individuals** — anyone related within the meaning of Section 267(b) to the donor, the transferor, or the donee; employees of those individuals; spouses of related individuals; and regular independent-contractor appraisers who do not perform a majority of their appraisals for others during the taxable year.
5. **Barred practitioners** — anyone prohibited from practicing before the IRS under 31 U.S.C. Section 330(c) at any time during the three-year period ending on the date the appraisal is signed.

In addition, under paragraph (a)(9), the fee for a qualified appraisal **cannot be based, to any extent, on the appraised value** of the property. A fee arrangement that rewards the appraiser for hitting a target value is prohibited — and it voids the appraisal. This is the "contingent fee" prohibition, and it exists to protect the independence of the valuation. When you see a firm advertising "we maximize your donation deduction," that is not a qualified appraisal firm; that is a red flag wearing a marketing hat.

The penalty structure reinforces these rules. Under IRC Section 6695A, an appraiser who prepares an appraisal that results in a substantial or gross valuation misstatement is subject to a penalty — the greater of 10 percent of the understatement attributable to the misstatement, or a fixed amount ($1,000 for substantial, $5,000 for gross, indexed), capped at 125 percent of the gross income derived from the appraisal. The qualified appraisal must include a declaration referencing this exposure. An appraiser who understands this exposure treats the engagement with the seriousness it deserves.

## The Vetting Checklist: Ten Questions Before You Engage

For an estate attorney, CPA, trust officer, or HNW individual, the following checklist is the practical screen. Run every prospective digital asset appraiser through it before engagement — and document the answers in your file.

1. **Who is the individual performing and signing the appraisal?** Confirm the actual individual, not just the firm. The statute applies to the individual.
2. **Which qualification path does that individual satisfy?** Ask specifically: recognized appraiser designation (and from which organization, awarded on demonstrated competency), or professional coursework plus two or more years of experience valuing digital assets? Request the details.
3. **Is the education and experience in the digital asset category?** Probe for the specific asset types handled: cryptocurrencies, NFTs, DeFi positions, stablecoins, wrapped assets, tokens. "General business valuation" experience is not the same.
4. **Will the appraisal be performed in accordance with USPAP?** Confirm in the engagement letter. Verify the appraiser's familiarity with the current USPAP edition and, where relevant, AO-41.
5. **What methodology will be used?** A qualified appraisal must state the method of valuation and the specific basis for the valuation — comparable sales, income data, or cost/reproduction analysis, adapted to the digital asset. Ask for the approach before you pay.
6. **What evidence will the appraisal rely on?** Primary evidence (on-chain data tied to verified wallet addresses, exchange statements, custody records) versus screenshots. The answer reveals the quality of the work product.
7. **Will the report contain the required contents?** Property description, valuation effective date, Fair Market Value, appraiser name/address/TIN and qualifications, signature and date, the Section 6695A declaration, the income-tax-purpose statement, the method of valuation, and the specific basis for valuation. These are not optional extras.
8. **Is the fee structure compliant?** A flat or hourly fee for the engagement — never contingent on the appraised value. Confirm in writing.
9. **What is the firm's workfile and retention practice?** USPAP's Record Keeping Rule requires a workfile that supports the appraisal, retained for at least five years. Ask who holds the workfile and for how long.
10. **Can the appraiser defend the work?** Does the firm have experience supporting appraisals in IRS examinations, and the capacity to provide expert testimony if needed? An appraisal is only as strong as the appraiser's ability to stand behind it.

## Red Flags: When to Walk Away

Some signals are so fundamental that they should end the conversation:

- **The "appraiser" is a data vendor.** A firm that sells price reports, portfolio snapshots, or tax-loss harvesting data is not performing appraisals. A qualified appraisal is a professional opinion of Fair Market Value, not a data export.
- **Refusal to identify the signing appraiser.** If a firm will not tell you which individual performs and signs the work, the work will not satisfy the statute.
- **No USPAP compliance statement.** If the firm cannot confirm USPAP compliance in writing, the report will not satisfy the "generally accepted appraisal standards" requirement.
- **Fee contingent on value.** Any fee tied to the appraised value or the accepted deduction is prohibited — and the appraisal is void.
- **Valuation from screenshots or exchange spot prices.** A defensible digital asset appraisal verifies holdings through primary evidence. Screenshots have no evidentiary chain.
- **No methodology documentation.** A report that states a number without explaining the approach, the comparables, the adjustments, and the basis is not a qualified appraisal.
- **Marketing that sounds like speculation.** Appraisers who talk about "the next big token," price predictions, or guaranteed outcomes are not practicing professional valuation.
- **The charity provides the "appraisal."** A donee's receipt is not a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser, regardless of the charity's good intentions.
- **The CPA or the software vendor provides the "appraisal."** Crypto tax software generates transaction reports and tax summaries; it does not perform USPAP-compliant appraisals, and its outputs do not include an appraiser's education, experience, qualification declaration, or signature.
- **No defensible data.** If the firm cannot articulate what evidence supports the opinion, the opinion will not survive scrutiny.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What makes someone a qualified appraiser for digital assets under IRS rules?**
Under IRC Section 170(f)(11)(E), a qualified appraiser is an individual who has earned an appraisal designation from a recognized professional appraiser organization (or met minimum education and experience requirements), regularly performs appraisals for compensation, demonstrates verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property at issue, and has not been barred from IRS practice in the prior three years. For digital assets, the education and experience must be in the digital asset category itself.

**What is the USPAP Competency Rule and why does it matter for crypto appraisals?**
The USPAP Competency Rule requires an appraiser to have the knowledge and experience to complete an assignment correctly, and to determine competency before accepting the engagement. For crypto appraisals, it means the appraiser must be competent in the specific asset class — cryptocurrencies, NFTs, DeFi positions — not merely in valuation generally. A credential in another field does not satisfy the rule.

**What credentials should a digital asset appraiser hold?**
A recognized designation from a generally recognized professional appraiser organization (such as ASA, ABV, CVA, AAA, or ISA credentials) is strong evidence of competence. But the statutory test also requires verifiable education and experience in valuing digital assets specifically. The combination matters: a recognized designation plus demonstrated digital asset expertise.

**How do I verify an appraiser's education and experience in valuing digital assets?**
Ask directly which statutory path the individual satisfies, request details of coursework and engagements in the digital asset category, and confirm the report will contain the required specification of education and experience plus the qualification declaration. The appraisal document itself must make these verifiable.

**What is the difference between a qualified appraiser and a certified real estate appraiser?**
A state-certified real estate appraiser is licensed to value real property under FIRREA-based state regimes. A qualified appraiser under Section 170(f)(11)(E) is an individual who meets the statutory and regulatory tests for the type of property being valued. A real estate license does not qualify an appraiser to value digital assets.

**Who cannot be a qualified appraiser for a crypto donation?**
The donor, the donee, a party to the transaction in which the donor acquired the property, related individuals, and anyone barred from practicing before the IRS in the prior three years. The charity itself cannot serve as the qualified appraiser for its donor's deduction, and an appraiser whose fee is contingent on value is disqualified.

**Can a CPA or tax software vendor provide a qualified appraisal?**
No. Crypto tax software generates transaction reports and tax summaries; it does not perform USPAP-compliant appraisals and its outputs lack the appraiser's education, experience, qualification declaration, and signature. A CPA's expertise in tax generally does not extend to blockchain-specific valuation methodology. A qualified appraisal must be performed and signed by an individual who meets the Section 170(f)(11)(E) tests for the digital asset category.

**What red flags indicate an appraiser is not qualified to value digital assets?**
Refusing to identify the signing appraiser, no USPAP compliance statement, fees contingent on appraised value, valuation based on screenshots or exchange spot prices, no methodology documentation, marketing that sounds like speculation, or positioning by a charity, data vendor, or software platform as the "appraiser." Any of these means the appraisal will not survive scrutiny.
