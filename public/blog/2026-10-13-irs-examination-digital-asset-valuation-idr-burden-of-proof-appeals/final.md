# Inside an IRS Examination of a Digital Asset Valuation: Information Document Requests, the Burden of Proof Under IRC Section 7491, the Thirty-Day Letter, and How a Defensible Appraisal Performs at Appeals
*Published: October 13, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

## The Examination Is Where the Appraisal Is Tested

A Qualified Appraisal is prepared in the ordinary course, filed with a return, and then filed away. Most of the time nothing further happens to it. The entire justification for commissioning a formal digital asset valuation, however, rests on the possibility that something does: that a revenue agent opens the return, asks what the number is and how it was reached, and expects an answer that will survive being checked.

When that happens, the appraisal ceases to be a document and becomes evidence. It acquires an author who may be interviewed, a workfile that may be requested, a methodology that may be referred for technical review, and a conclusion that may be tested against the government's own appraiser. Drafting decisions made months earlier — whether the report stated its intended use, whether it identified the assets with enough specificity to be tied to on-chain records, whether it disclosed the discounts applied, whether the appraiser signed a certification — determine what happens next.

The subject throughout is constant: Defensible Data, established before the question is asked.

## How a Digital Asset Valuation Reaches an Examiner's Desk

Digital asset cases do not arrive at examination at random. Four selection pathways account for most of them.

**Information-report matching.** With Forms 1099-DA now in the reporting ecosystem, the Service has substantially more visibility into reported transactions than in prior filing years. Discrepancies between what a broker reports and what a taxpayer reports are mechanical to detect, and mechanical discrepancies are the cheapest source of exam inventory. A valuation that departs from a broker-reported figure without a documented explanation is exposed to this pathway.

**The digital asset question.** A negative answer to the digital asset question on Form 1040, where the Service holds contrary records, is itself a selection signal. So is a positive answer with an apparently thin supporting schedule.

**Estate and gift examination.** Where Form 706 or Form 709 reports digital assets, the reported value is a line item the examiner is entitled to test — the tax is computed on the value, so value is the issue.

**Targeted campaigns.** Large Business & International and the operating divisions maintain issue-focused examination work, and digital asset activity has been drawn into that architecture as volumes have grown. Practitioner observation of 2026 exam activity describes a consistent pattern: an initial request that includes general crypto questions, followed by a narrower, crypto-specific request once the examiner understands the shape of the holdings.

The practical consequence is that a digital asset valuation is rarely the only issue under examination; it is usually one question in a case that also involves completeness of reporting and substantiation of basis.

## The Three Forms of Examination

| Examination type | Typical conduct | Digital asset valuation posture |
|---|---|---|
| Correspondence examination | Conducted by mail from a campus function; narrow, document-driven, usually a single issue | Rarely reaches valuation methodology; more often resolves a reporting mismatch. A complete contemporaneous appraisal often resolves the item at this stage without further development. |
| Office examination | Conducted at an IRS office or by telephone and correspondence with a revenue agent; broader scope | The common setting for a valuation dispute. The agent will request the appraisal, the appraiser's qualifications, and the underlying data, and may refer the value question for technical review. |
| Field examination | Conducted at the taxpayer's or representative's location; used for larger and more complex cases | Applied to substantial portfolios, closely held entities holding digital assets, and coordinated issues. Expect the deepest record demands and the earliest involvement of counsel. |

The distinction is not academic: a correspondence examination is a document test and a field examination is an investigation, and the resource a taxpayer commits should track the form of examination rather than the amount at issue alone.

## The Information Document Request: Form 4564

An examiner obtains records through an Information Document Request, issued on Form 4564. The IDR is the Service's standard tool for asking an examined taxpayer for documents: each request identifies the items sought, states the response date, identifies the requesting representative, and specifies how the material is to be delivered. The process is structured rather than informal, and a failure to respond can be met with an administrative summons.

For a valuation issue, the following IDR items are ordinary rather than exceptional:

| IDR category | What is being tested |
|---|---|
| The appraisal report itself, in full, with exhibits | Whether a complete report exists and what it asserts |
| The appraiser's qualifications and experience in valuing this asset class | Whether the preparer meets the regulatory definition of a qualified appraiser |
| The engagement letter and scope of work | What the appraiser was engaged to do, for whom, and for what intended use |
| The appraiser's workfile: market data, comparable transactions, on-chain evidence, analysis | Whether the conclusion is supported by documentation or merely asserted |
| Wallet ownership records and exchange account statements | Whether the assets valued are the taxpayer's, and whether the inventory is complete |
| The valuation effective date and the basis for its selection | Whether the value corresponds to the date the tax provision requires |
| Records of any restrictions, lockups, vesting, or discounts applied | Whether the value reflects enforceable limitations or an unsubstantiated haircut |
| Certifications and statements of independence | Whether the report carries the representations the regulations require |

The framework that answers nearly all of these is the requirement that a Qualified Appraisal be prepared by a qualified appraiser in accordance with generally accepted appraisal standards — defined in the regulations as the substance and principles of the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice. Under USPAP Standards Rule 10-2, a full appraisal report states the identity of the client and any intended users, the intended use, the purpose, the scope of work, the definition of value, the valuation date, the approaches and methods considered and applied, the reasoning, and a signed certification. That is not a bureaucratic overlay on the analysis. It is a ready-made index to the IDR, and a report drafted to the standard can be mapped to the examiner's request line by line.

### The Historical Platform and Wallet Form

Practitioners conducting digital asset examinations have reported receiving, inside an IDR, a two-page attachment titled "List of Digital Asset Platforms, Wallets, Services, and Products Used (Individual Taxpayers)." It is not an OMB-approved numbered form and is not published on irs.gov. It is an examination document, and it is unusually broad.

Part I presents a pre-printed list of more than one hundred exchanges and trading platforms, including major venues and a substantial number of defunct ones — institutions whose records are precisely what would be needed to reconstruct earliest basis. For each platform the taxpayer indicates whether it was used, the date of first use, and associated usernames or email addresses, with blank rows for additions. Part II moves to self-custody wallets and decentralized finance tools. The look-back period in the instance reported ran from the taxpayer's first digital asset activity through the year under audit, under a deadline measured in weeks and a signature under penalties of perjury.

Two implications follow for the appraisal.

First, the Service is attempting to establish *completeness* — the historical perimeter of a taxpayer's digital asset activity — before it engages the numbers. Second, the perimeter question is squarely a valuation question. An appraisal that values a portfolio without establishing its completeness, or without documenting how holdings were traced to the addresses asserted, is vulnerable on the threshold issue rather than the conclusion. Verification of holdings without taking custody, and documentation of the inventory reconstruction from exchanges, custodians, and addresses, is the part of the engagement that most directly answers the form.

## Completeness Before Accuracy

Practitioner observation of 2026 examination activity describes a consistent progression: establish completeness first, accuracy second. Before an examiner evaluates whether gains and losses were computed correctly, the examiner establishes that every exchange account, wallet, and on-chain address has been accounted for. Only then do transaction reconciliation, basis substantiation, wallet-by-wallet tracking, and value substantiation become the work.

The appraiser's inventory reconciliation is therefore the first artifact examined and the hardest to repair retrospectively. Where an inventory cannot be tied to primary records, the deficiency is not a matter of interpretation; it is a gap, and a gap invites the examiner to substitute an estimate — the outcome the engagement exists to prevent.

## The Technical Referral: When the Examiner Rejects the Number

If the examiner does not accept the taxpayer's value, the issue may be referred for technical review. The Service maintains in-house appraisal capability, and its examiners and appraisers include individuals qualified to testify as expert witnesses in the Tax Court. Where the Service lacks the required expertise, and where the taxpayer has retained a highly qualified appraiser, the Service's own training materials contemplate obtaining an independent, outside appraisal — and in docketed cases direct that counsel participate in selecting and approving the appraiser, because the appraiser may be used as a testifying witness if the case does not settle.

The Service's training material for Appeals officers — substantial classroom instruction designed for officers with little or no valuation experience — identifies four factors considered when choosing an appraiser: the complexity and impact of the case, the litigating value of the appraisal, budget, and time. That fourth-list item matters to a taxpayer at the examination stage, because it is explicitly a function of the appraiser's credentials, the quality of the appraisal itself, and how well the appraiser handles testimony.

The same training material offers a framing worth quoting in every engagement letter. It distinguishes the *definition* of value — which criterion governs — from the *determination* of value, classifying the definition as a question of law and the determination as a question of fact, and states the consequence plainly: no case is stronger than its weakest link, and if the wrong standard is applied, the conclusion is defective. An appraisal that applies the correct definition of Fair Market Value, with the correct treatment of restrictions and marketability, cannot be defeated by pointing to a different but inapposite standard. An appraisal that blends standards can be defeated on that ground alone.

## The Burden of Proof Under IRC Section 7491

### The Default Rule

The starting position in a Tax Court proceeding is that the taxpayer bears the burden of proving the Commissioner's determination incorrect. The notice of deficiency is presumed correct, and the taxpayer must overcome that presumption by a preponderance of the evidence. In a valuation dispute this means the taxpayer must affirmatively establish that the value claimed is the correct value — not merely that the Service's number is imperfect. Courts have said repeatedly that valuation is inherently imprecise, but imprecision in the government's figure does not relieve the taxpayer of proving a better one.

### The Section 7491(a) Shift and Its Three Failure Points

Section 7491(a) provides the principal statutory exception. If, in a court proceeding, the taxpayer introduces *credible* evidence with respect to any factual issue relevant to ascertaining liability for a tax imposed by subtitle A or B, the Secretary bears the burden of proof on that issue. In a valuation case the shift is powerful: it converts the taxpayer's task from proving the number to requiring the government to disprove it. But it is conditional, and the conditions are stated in the statute. It applies only if:

1. the taxpayer has complied with the requirements of the Code to **substantiate** the item;
2. the taxpayer has **maintained all records** required under the Code and has **cooperated with reasonable requests** by the Secretary for witnesses, information, documents, meetings, and interviews; and
3. in the case of a partnership, corporation, or trust, the taxpayer satisfies the net-worth condition of section 7430(c)(4)(A)(ii), subject to a carve-out for certain qualified revocable trusts for periods ending after the decedent's death and before the applicable date.

The second condition is where section 7491(a) most often fails, and it fails on conduct rather than documentation. A taxpayer with a thorough appraisal can still lose the shift by declining an interview, by producing documents piecemeal past the request dates, or by allowing the representative to appear without the client's cooperation. The record that establishes cooperation is built during the examination, and it consists of IDR responses that are timely, complete, and indexed to the request. If the shift is worth having — and it is — the file must be assembled as though the shift will be litigated, because the conduct that earns it happens long before any petition is filed.

### Section 7491(c): The Penalty Burden of Production

Section 7491(c) operates independently of subsection (a) and is, for many digital asset cases, the more valuable provision. It states that notwithstanding any other provision of the Code, the Secretary shall have the **burden of production** in any court proceeding with respect to the liability of *any individual* for any penalty, addition to tax, or additional amount imposed by the Code.

The burden of production is narrower than the burden of proof, but it is not decorative. It requires the government to come forward with evidence that the penalty is appropriate — for an accuracy-related penalty under IRC Section 6662, evidence that the underpayment was attributable to a valuation misstatement, negligence, or another enumerated ground, together with compliance with the supervisory-approval requirement. Where the government cannot carry that production burden, the penalty fails regardless of what the underlying valuation turns out to be.

The two subsections therefore perform different functions in the same case. Section 7491(a) shifts the burden on the *value*; section 7491(c) places the burden on the *penalty*. A taxpayer who cannot satisfy the subsection (a) conditions — because cooperation cannot be reconstructed after the fact — may still defeat the penalty on subsection (c) grounds. Conversely, a taxpayer who has the shift under subsection (a) is not immunized from the penalty; the reasonable-cause and good-faith analysis of section 6664(c) operates separately, and it is why a contemporaneous professional appraisal matters even where the value is ultimately adjusted.

### What Section 7491 Does Not Do

Section 7491 allocates burdens in a *court proceeding*. It does not oblige a revenue agent to concede, to accept the appraisal without review, or to stop developing the issue. The statute also contains a coordination rule: the subsection (a) shift does not apply to an issue if another provision supplies a specific burden of proof for that issue. Section 7491 is a litigation-position provision, and it should be described to clients as such — not as an examination-stage entitlement.

## Contemporaneous Appraisal Versus Litigation Support

The regulations draw a line that has nothing to do with the quality of the analysis and everything to do with timing. For a charitable contribution, a Qualified Appraisal must be signed and dated by the qualified appraiser no earlier than sixty days before the date of the contribution and no later than the due date, including extensions, of the return; separately, it must be *received by the donor* before that due date. The same architecture — an appraisal obtained in connection with the filing, not in response to a challenge — governs the estate and gift contexts in which QDAV engagements arise under IRS Section 170 and its analogues.

An appraisal commissioned *after* the examination opens therefore occupies a different category. It is litigation support: it may be excellent, and more thorough than the original, but it is not the contemporaneous document the statute contemplates, and a reviewing court may treat it as advocacy prepared under the pressure of a pending dispute rather than a valuation prepared in the ordinary course.

Two refinements are worth stating precisely. First, contemporaneity is a requirement of the *report*, not of the appraiser's underlying knowledge: an appraiser engaged later to defend a value may produce a report admitted as an expert opinion under the Federal Rules of Evidence, but it will not function as the timely Qualified Appraisal that satisfies the substantiation regime. Second, the courts do not treat professional-standards compliance as an all-or-nothing admissibility gate. In *Whitehouse Hotel LP v. Commissioner*, the Tax Court held that it would not supplant its responsibility under Federal Rule of Evidence 702 to assess an expert appraiser's reliability by accepting USPAP as the defining standard of reliability. Failure to adhere to USPAP may affect the weight the Court accords to an appraiser's testimony, but does not necessarily preclude its admission; compliance with USPAP is evidence that the appraiser applied generally accepted methods. In *Utilicorp United, Inc. v. Commissioner*, the Court admitted a valuation report prepared for income tax purposes by appraisers not licensed in the relevant state, because the licensing statute's consumer-protection purpose did not reach an income tax appraisal. The practical reading of these authorities is that a fully USPAP Compliant report is more persuasive, not merely more admissible, and that a report prepared to the standard is the one that holds its weight rather than merely entering the record.

## The Thirty-Day Letter and the Protest to Appeals

If the examination cannot be closed by agreement, the Service issues a letter transmitting the proposed changes together with an examination report and explains the taxpayer's options. That letter is commonly called the thirty-day letter because it affords a limited period within which to request review. The taxpayer may accept the proposed changes, request a conference with the examiner's supervisor, request review by the Independent Office of Appeals, or do nothing and await a notice of deficiency.

The Service's Publication 5 sets out the framework: the mission of Appeals is to resolve federal tax controversies without litigation, on a basis fair and impartial to both the government and the taxpayer, and the Taxpayer Bill of Rights guarantees the right to appeal in an independent forum and to receive a written response on the outcome. The publication also states a procedural fact that functions as a warning — if the taxpayer raises new information or new issues to Appeals that the Service has not previously considered, that information may first need to be considered by the examining function, which will likely delay resolution.

That warning is why the evidentiary record must be built *during* the examination. A valuation argument introduced for the first time in a protest may be remanded back to the examiner, and the taxpayer loses the practical benefit of the independent forum. The appraisal, the workfile, the inventory reconciliation, and the appraiser's qualifications should all be in the file before the case leaves examination.

### What a Formal Protest Must Contain

A formal protest is a substantive document, not a cover letter. It should contain, at minimum:

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Identification | Taxpayer name, address, and identifying numbers; the tax periods and the specific proposed adjustments disputed |
| Statement of the proposed changes | Each adjustment, with the taxpayer's position stated item by item |
| Statement of facts | The facts supporting the valuation, with references to the documents produced during examination |
| Statement of law and authority | The governing Code sections, regulations, and authorities, including the definition and criteria of Fair Market Value actually applied |
| Argument | The application of the law to the facts, including the treatment of restrictions, marketability, and any discount, and the response to the examiner's contrary position |
| Request for relief | The value the taxpayer contends is correct, and the basis for it |
| Declaration and signature | Signed under penalties of perjury by the taxpayer or an authorized representative, with a power of attorney where applicable |

### The Appeals Conference and the Hazards of Litigation

The credibility of the protest is a function of the credibility of the underlying appraisal, because Appeals settles on the basis of the hazards of litigation — the relative strengths and weaknesses each side would face if the matter were tried. For valuation issues, that culture is unusually favorable to a well-documented taxpayer, and the Service's own training reflects it. Appeals officers are taught that the courts have repeatedly admonished that valuation disputes are more properly suited to compromise than adjudication, and are given the language of the leading cases to that effect: that valuation is inherently imprecise and capable of resolution only by a Solomon-like pronouncement; that the issue is more properly suited for the give and take of the settlement process than adjudication. The same training acknowledges that a highly qualified taxpayer's appraiser may be difficult to rebut with an in-house valuation that a court might view as biased, and contemplates engaging external appraisers where the Service lacks the required expertise.

There is a structural caveat. The National Taxpayer Advocate has reported that Appeals officers' autonomy to assess the hazards of litigation can be constrained where an issue is coordinated, with settlement authority influenced by specialists rather than the officer alone, and has recommended that Appeals engage external experts to assess hazards on valuation issues. The practical inference is that a strong appraisal matters more, not less, when the Service's settlement authority is constrained by an internal position: it is the artifact that gives the officer something to weigh.

## Into the Tax Court: The Expert Report Standard

If the matter is not resolved administratively, the Service issues a notice of deficiency, and the taxpayer has a limited period to petition the Tax Court for redetermination before assessment and collection. Note also that the limitation period may be extended by written agreement, and that the Service is required to notify the taxpayer of the right to refuse such an extension, or to limit it to particular issues or a particular period — a point worth raising with clients whenever a consent is presented, because it is the client's decision, not the agent's.

In Tax Court, an appraiser's written report is the mechanism by which the opinion reaches the court. Under T.C. Rule 143(f), a party calling an expert witness must submit a written report setting forth the witness's qualifications, the witness's opinion, the data on which the opinion is based, and the detailed reasons for the witness's conclusions. Because the report is generally received as the expert's direct testimony once identified by the expert, it must satisfy the admissibility standards of the Federal Rules of Evidence. Failure to comply can result in exclusion of the report, in whole or in part, absent good cause and the absence of undue prejudice — and a failure that significantly impairs the opposing party's ability to cross-examine the expert is a recognized form of prejudice.

Three requirements in that standard deserve emphasis in advance of any engagement.

The expert must be legitimate under Rule 702, which asks whether the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data, whether it is the product of reliable principles and methods, and whether the witness applied those principles and methods reliably to the facts. The trial judge performs a gatekeeping function with respect to relevance and reliability. Compliance with professional standards is evidence of reliability, though not the sole measure of it.

The report must be the expert's own work. Statements by experts who do not testify may be hearsay, and the mere adoption of another's words does not establish that the testifying expert is familiar with the material matters in the report. For a digital asset engagement with multiple contributors, this has a direct operational consequence: the person who signs must have performed or meaningfully directed the analysis. And the signing expert must be available to testify; a report signed by individuals who are not produced as witnesses risks exclusion of the statements attributable to them.

## How Long the Valuation Stays Exposed Under Section 6501

The period during which the Service may assess additional tax — and therefore the period during which a valuation may be challenged — is governed by section 6501.

| Provision | Period | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| §6501(a) | 3 years after the return was filed | The general rule |
| §6501(e)(1) | 6 years | Omission from gross income exceeding 25 percent of the gross income stated in the return |
| §6501(e)(2) | 6 years | For estate and gift returns, omission of items exceeding 25 percent of the gross estate or total gifts stated in the return; items adequately disclosed in the return or an attached statement are not counted toward the threshold |
| §6501(c)(1) | Unlimited | False or fraudulent return with intent to evade tax |
| §6501(c)(3) | Unlimited | Failure to file a return |
| §6501(c)(4) | As agreed | Extension by written consent (Form 872); the taxpayer must be notified of the right to refuse or limit the extension |
| §6501(c)(9) | Unlimited unless adequately disclosed | Gift of property the value of which is required to be shown on a gift tax return and is not shown on the return |

The estate and gift provisions are where digital asset valuations sit, and two of them deserve particular attention. Section 6501(e)(2) contains its own disclosure carve-out: in determining the items omitted, an item omitted from the gross estate or from the total gifts stated in the return is not taken into account if it is disclosed in the return or an attached statement in a manner adequate to apprise the Secretary of the nature and amount of the item. Disclosure is therefore not merely a defensive nicety; it is a computational input to whether the six-year period applies at all.

### The Gift Tax Rule That Never Closes

Section 6501(c)(9) provides that if a gift of property, the value of which is required to be shown on a gift tax return, is not shown on that return, the tax on the gift may be assessed at any time. The clause that follows is the taxpayer's entire protection: the unlimited period does not apply to any item that is disclosed in the return, or in a statement attached to the return, in a manner adequate to apprise the Secretary of the nature of the item.

The regulations define what adequate disclosure means for a transfer of property, and the definition is demanding in exactly the dimension that digital asset valuations implicate. The return or attached statement must provide a detailed description of the method used to determine Fair Market Value, the financial data relied upon with explanations of adjustments, the restrictions on the transferred property that were considered, and a description of any discounts claimed — including discounts for blockage, minority or fractional interests, and lack of marketability.

For a digital asset gift, that means a one-line entry of a dollar amount accompanied by a portfolio printout does not constitute adequate disclosure. Where a discount for lack of marketability has been applied to a restricted token position, the discount must be described, and the method must be stated, or the item remains in the unlimited assessment period. The point is not that discounts are disfavored; it is that an undisclosed discount is what holds the statute open indefinitely. An appraisal report that states the definition of value applied, identifies the restrictions considered, and quantifies and explains the discount is simultaneously the substantiation of the value and the instrument of disclosure that starts the clock.

Two limits on these rules deserve note: the substantial-omission extension under section 6501(e)(2) operates on items omitted from the *current* return, and the reach of section 6501(c)(9) is directed at the gift that is not shown, not at intervening years.

## A Response Protocol for Counsel and the Appraiser

The following sequence reflects the authorities above and is a working checklist for receipt of an examination notice concerning a digital asset valuation.

| Stage | Action | Authority or rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Receipt | Calendar every IDR response date; identify the form of examination and the scope of the request | Form 4564 states the due date and delivery method |
| Preservation | Issue a litigation hold; preserve the appraisal workfile, engagement letter, wallet and exchange records, and all source data | Completeness is the first thing tested; retrospective reconstruction is the weakest position |
| Counsel | Engage tax controversy counsel; establish the scope of the representative's authority on Form 2848 | Privilege and strategy are counsel's domain; the appraiser is a valuation professional and not counsel |
| Appraiser | Confirm whether the appraisal is a contemporaneous Qualified Appraisal or litigation support, and disclose which to the client | The regulatory timeliness window determines which |
| Response design | Map each IDR line item to a specific document, with an indexing cover that shows the response is complete | Builds the cooperation record that section 7491(a) requires |
| Cooperation record | Respond timely to every request for documents, information, meetings, and interviews; document each response | Section 7491(a)(2)(B) conditions the burden shift on cooperation |
| Technical review | Prepare the appraiser to explain the definition of value, the approaches considered, the treatment of restrictions, and any discount | Referral turns on whether the correct standard was applied |
| Penalty posture | Preserve the contemporaneous-appraisal record and any reliance on professional advice | Reasonable cause for purposes of section 6664(c); note the government's production burden under section 7491(c) |
| Protest | If unagreed, file a formal protest containing every required element, with the appraisal appended | Publication 5; new matters may be remanded to examination |
| Appeals | Prepare the appraiser to discuss the hazards of litigation, not merely the conclusion | Appeals settles on hazards; valuation is a favored subject of compromise |
| Pre-trial | If docketed, ensure the expert report satisfies T.C. Rule 143(f) in every element and that the signer will testify | Report is received as direct testimony; exclusion is possible |
| Limitation | Review the assessment period against section 6501, and treat any consent to extend as the client's decision | Section 6501(c)(4) notice requirement |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is an information document request, and must I respond to it?**

An Information Document Request is the Service's standard mechanism for asking an examined taxpayer to produce records, issued on Form 4564. Each request identifies the items sought, sets a response date, names the requesting representative, and specifies the delivery method. The Service follows structured IDR procedures, and a failure to respond can be met with an administrative summons. Respond on time, in an indexed and complete form, with professional guidance.

**What does the IRS ask for in a digital asset valuation examination?**

Ordinarily: the full appraisal report with exhibits; the appraiser's qualifications in that asset class; the engagement letter and scope of work; the appraiser's workfile, including comparable data and on-chain evidence; wallet and exchange records establishing ownership and completeness of inventory; the valuation effective date and the reason for it; records of any restrictions or discounts applied; and the report's certifications and statements of independence. Examiners have also used an examination attachment requesting a historical list of every platform, wallet, and service used by the taxpayer.

**Who has the burden of proof in a digital asset valuation dispute?**

The taxpayer ordinarily bears the burden of proving the Commissioner's determination incorrect in Tax Court. Under IRC section 7491(a), the burden shifts to the Secretary on any factual issue for which the taxpayer introduces credible evidence — but only if the taxpayer has complied with the substantiation requirements and has maintained all required records and cooperated with reasonable requests for witnesses, information, documents, meetings, and interviews. That cooperation condition is where the shift most often fails.

**What is the significance of section 7491(c)?**

Section 7491(c) provides that for an individual, the Secretary bears the burden of production in any court proceeding with respect to liability for any penalty, addition to tax, or additional amount imposed by the Code. It requires the government to come forward with evidence supporting the penalty, independently of the burden on the underlying value. A taxpayer who cannot earn the section 7491(a) shift may still defeat a penalty on section 7491(c) grounds.

**Can I obtain an appraisal after the audit has already begun?**

You can, and the analysis may be sound, but the report occupies a different category. For a charitable contribution, a Qualified Appraisal must be signed and dated no earlier than sixty days before the contribution and no later than the due date, including extensions, of the return, and must be received by the donor before that date. A valuation prepared once the examination is underway is litigation support: admissible as expert opinion, perhaps, but not the contemporaneous Qualified Appraisal the substantiation regime contemplates.

**What happens after the thirty-day letter if we cannot agree?**

The taxpayer may request a conference with the examiner's supervisor, request review by the Independent Office of Appeals, or take no action and await a notice of deficiency. A protest must state the proposed changes, the facts, the law and authority, the argument, and the relief sought, signed under penalties of perjury. Because Appeals generally will not develop a valuation argument never presented to the examining function, the appraisal and its supporting data should be in the record before the case leaves examination.

**What must an appraiser's report contain to be usable in Tax Court?**

Under T.C. Rule 143(f), a written report must set forth the witness's qualifications, the opinion, the data on which the opinion is based, and the detailed reasons for the conclusions. It is generally received as the expert's direct testimony, so it must satisfy the admissibility standards of the Federal Rules of Evidence, including Rule 702's reliability requirements, reflect the expert's own work, and be supported by a signing expert who will testify.

**How long can the IRS challenge a digital asset valuation?**

Generally three years from the filing of the return under section 6501(a). The period extends to six years where omissions exceed twenty-five percent of the gross income, gross estate, or total gifts stated in the return, under sections 6501(e)(1) and 6501(e)(2). It is unlimited in the case of a false or fraudulent return, a failure to file, or — under section 6501(c)(9) — a gift of property required to be shown on a gift tax return that is not shown, unless the item is adequately disclosed. Adequate disclosure for this purpose requires a description of the valuation method, the data relied upon, the restrictions considered, and any discounts claimed.

## The Standard Is Contemporaneous Defensibility

Every element of the examination sequence rewards the same behavior. The IDR rewards a report prepared to a recognized standard, because the standard supplies the index to the request. The technical referral rewards the correct definition of value, because the wrong standard makes the conclusion defective regardless of the arithmetic. The burden rules reward cooperation conducted contemporaneously, because coercive conditions cannot be satisfied retroactively. The timeliness rule rewards an appraisal obtained in connection with the filing, because a later one is litigation support. The limitation rules reward disclosure, because an undisclosed discount is what holds the statute open.

None of this requires anticipating a specific examiner's question. It requires a valuation that is defensible on its face — a Qualified Appraisal, prepared under USPAP by a qualified appraiser, applying the correct definition of Fair Market Value to a complete and verified inventory, with restrictions and discounts analyzed, disclosed, and explained, and with a workfile that can be produced on request.
