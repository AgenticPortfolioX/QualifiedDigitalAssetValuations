# What Constitutes a Qualified Appraisal for Digital Assets Under IRS Rules: Statutory Requirements, USPAP Standards, and Why Crypto Tax Software Reports Don't Meet the Threshold

*Published: June 16, 2026 | By Qualified Digital Asset Valuations (QDAV)*

The Internal Revenue Code draws a sharp and consequential distinction between an **exchange-reported price** and a **qualified appraisal** — a distinction that many cryptocurrency investors, estate attorneys, tax professionals, and even some CPA firms do not fully appreciate. When a taxpayer holds $500,000 in Bitcoin, an NFT collection valued at $250,000, or a portfolio of staked Ether across multiple DeFi protocols, the question of what constitutes a qualified appraisal has direct implications for audit defense, penalty exposure, and the validity of the underlying tax position being reported.

This guide provides a comprehensive examination of the qualified appraisal requirement as it applies to digital assets under federal tax law. We analyze the statutory framework of IRC §170(f)(11), the definitional requirements for both a qualified appraisal and a qualified appraiser, the role of USPAP (Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice) in digital asset valuation, and — critically — why the output of crypto tax software platforms does not satisfy the legal standard for a qualified appraisal.

## Part I: The Statutory Framework — IRC §170(f)(11) and Its Application to Digital Assets

### The Foundation: Digital Assets as Property

The IRS has consistently treated cryptocurrency and other digital assets as **property** for federal tax purposes since the issuance of Notice 2014-21. This classification has profound implications for valuation requirements. Under the property framework, the valuation of digital assets is governed not by securities pricing conventions but by the appraisal standards applicable to noncash property — specifically, IRC §170(f)(11) and the Treasury Regulations promulgated thereunder.

IRS Chief Counsel Advice Memorandum 202302012 (January 2023) definitively confirmed that **cryptocurrency is not excepted property** under §170(f)(11)(A)(ii)(I). The excepted categories — cash, publicly traded securities, inventory, intellectual property, and certain vehicles — do not include digital assets. The memorandum explicitly rejected the argument that exchange-quoted prices render an appraisal unnecessary, stating that "[t]he exchange-reported value of digital assets does not substitute for the qualified appraisal required for a charitable contribution deduction."

### The $5,000 Threshold

Under IRC §170(f)(11)(C), a qualified appraisal is required whenever a taxpayer claims a deduction exceeding **$5,000** for a contribution of property. While the literal language of §170 addresses charitable contributions, the qualified appraisal framework extends to the full range of federal tax reporting contexts where property value must be substantiated:

| Context | Code Section | Threshold | Appraisal Requirement |
|---------|-------------|-----------|----------------------|
| Charitable contributions | IRC §170(f)(11)(C) | >$5,000 | Qualified appraisal + Form 8283 Section B |
| Gift tax (trust funding) | IRC §2512; Treas. Reg. §301.6501(c)-1(f) | >$5,000 | Qualified appraisal for adequate disclosure |
| Estate tax (Form 706) | IRC §2031; Treas. Reg. §20.2031-1(b) | Any value | Qualified appraisal recommended for all noncash assets |
| IRA annual valuation | IRC §408; Prohibited Transaction rules | Any value | USPAP-compliant valuation for audit defense |

The table above illustrates a critical point: while the statutory requirement for a qualified appraisal explicitly applies to charitable deductions under §170, the **adequate disclosure** standard for gift tax purposes (Treas. Reg. §301.6501(c)-1(f)) and the **substantiating documentation** standard for estate tax returns effectively require the same level of documentation for any significant digital asset position.

### Aggregation Rules

A common misunderstanding involves the aggregation requirement of IRC §170(f)(11)(F): "property and all similar items of property donated to 1 or more donees shall be treated as 1 property." For digital assets, this means that multiple cryptocurrency donations during a tax year — even to different charities — are aggregated to determine whether the $5,000 threshold is met. The same aggregation principle applies in the estate and gift tax context: multiple transfers of similar digital assets are aggregated for threshold purposes.

## Part II: Defining the "Qualified Appraisal"

### Statutory Definition

IRC §170(f)(11)(E)(i) establishes a two-part test for a qualified appraisal:

> The term "qualified appraisal" means, with respect to any property, an appraisal of such property which—
> (I) is treated for purposes of this paragraph as a qualified appraisal under regulations or other guidance prescribed by the Secretary, and
> (II) is conducted by a qualified appraiser in accordance with generally accepted appraisal standards and any regulations or other guidance prescribed under subclause (I).

### Treasury Regulation Requirements

Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17 elaborates on the statutory framework, requiring that a qualified appraisal must:

1. **Be made no earlier than 60 days before the date of contribution** (or the relevant valuation date) and no later than the date the return is filed.
2. **Include a detailed description of the property** — for digital assets, this includes the asset type (cryptocurrency, NFT, tokenized asset), blockchain identifier, quantity, and wallet address.
3. **State the fair market value (FMV) on the applicable valuation date** and the specific valuation methodology employed.
4. **Include the appraiser's qualifications**, including education, professional designations, and experience in the relevant asset class.
5. **Contain the appraiser's declaration** that the appraisal is prepared for federal tax purposes, that the appraiser has not been prohibited from practicing before the IRS under Circular 230, and that the appraiser is independent of the taxpayer and the transaction.
6. **Be signed and dated by the qualified appraiser.**
7. **Include a description of the fee arrangement** — appraisals based on a percentage of the reported value are prohibited.

### What a Qualified Appraisal Is NOT

Equally important is understanding what does **not** constitute a qualified appraisal:

- ❌ **A screenshot of an exchange price feed** (CoinMarketCap, CoinGecko, exchange order book)
- ❌ **A crypto tax software report** (CoinTracker, CoinLedger, Koinly, TaxBit output)
- ❌ **A portfolio valuation from a DeFi dashboard or wallet aggregator**
- ❌ **An exchange-issued transaction history or trade confirmation**
- ❌ **A valuation prepared by the taxpayer, the donor, or the donee**
- ❌ **An appraisal whose fee is contingent upon the reported value**

These items may serve as **inputs** to a qualified appraisal — they provide market data that the appraiser considers — but they do not themselves satisfy the statutory requirements. This distinction has been central to IRS enforcement actions, as illustrated by the Tax Court's observation in *Pankratz v. Commissioner*, T.C. Memo. 2021-26, that "four mentions of 'appraisal,' 'appraiser,' or 'appraised' on one page of one form is pretty good notice that substantial noncash donations need to be backed up by an appraisal."

## Part III: The Qualified Appraiser — Who Qualifies?

### Statutory Requirements

IRC §170(f)(11)(E)(ii) defines a "qualified appraiser" as an individual who meets three criteria:

1. **Professional Designation or Minimum Standards:** The appraiser must have "earned an appraisal designation from a recognized professional appraiser organization or has otherwise met minimum education and experience requirements set forth in regulations prescribed by the Secretary."

2. **Regular Practice:** The appraiser must "regularly perform[] appraisals for which the individual receives compensation."

3. **Additional Requirements:** The appraiser must meet "such other requirements as may be prescribed by the Secretary in regulations or other guidance."

### Specific Appraisal Restrictions

IRC §170(f)(11)(E)(iii) imposes additional requirements for each specific appraisal:

> An individual shall not be treated as a qualified appraiser with respect to any specific appraisal unless—
> (I) the individual demonstrates verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property subject to the appraisal, and
> (II) the individual has not been prohibited from practicing before the Internal Revenue Service by the Secretary under section 330(c) of title 31, United States Code, at any time during the 3-year period ending on the date of the appraisal.

### What This Means for Digital Asset Appraisers

The "verifiable education and experience" requirement under clause (I) is the most consequential for digital asset appraisal. Valuing cryptocurrency requires not only general appraisal training but **domain-specific expertise** in:

- **Blockchain technology** — Understanding how transactions are recorded, confirmed, and timestamped on distributed ledgers.
- **Market microstructure** — Recognizing the implications of thin order books, wash trading, and exchange-specific pricing anomalies on FMV determination.
- **Trait-based valuation** — For NFTs, understanding rarity scoring, trait correlations, and collection-level market dynamics.
- **DeFi and staking positions** — Valuing assets subject to lock-up periods, slashing risks, and variable yield rates.
- **Custody and control analysis** — Assessing whether assets in lost wallets, compromised accounts, or multisig arrangements have the same FMV as freely transferable assets.

A general personal property appraiser without specific digital asset experience **does not** satisfy the qualified appraiser standard for cryptocurrency or NFT valuations, even if they hold USPAP certification. Conversely, a blockchain engineer with deep technical knowledge but no USPAP training or appraisal designation **also does not** qualify. The qualified appraiser for digital assets must possess both credentials: USPAP-compliant appraisal methodology and verifiable digital asset expertise.

### Circular 230 Considerations

The reference to 31 U.S.C. §330(c) (Circular 230) in §170(f)(11)(E)(iii)(II) introduces an additional layer of regulation. Appraisers who have been disbarred or suspended from practice before the IRS are ineligible to serve as qualified appraisers for a three-year period following the prohibition. Proposed regulations published December 26, 2024 (89 Fed. Reg. 105,090) would expand Circular 230's scope to explicitly cover appraisal professionals, imposing conduct standards and disciplinary mechanisms specifically for appraisers who prepare valuations for federal tax purposes. While these proposed regulations have not been finalized as of June 2026, they signal the IRS's increasing focus on appraisal quality and appraiser accountability.

## Part IV: USPAP Standards and Digital Asset Valuation

### The Role of USPAP

IRC §170(f)(11)(E)(i)(II) requires that a qualified appraisal be "conducted by a qualified appraiser in accordance with generally accepted appraisal standards." The Treasury Regulations explicitly identify USPAP — the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, promulgated by The Appraisal Foundation — as the generally accepted appraisal standards for purposes of the qualified appraisal requirement.

### Applicable USPAP Standards

For digital asset appraisals, the relevant USPAP standards depend on the classification of the digital asset:

| Asset Type | USPAP Standard | Approach |
|-----------|---------------|----------|
| Cryptocurrency (Bitcoin, ETH, etc.) | Standard 7 — Personal Property Appraisal | Market approach via exchange data |
| NFTs (art, collectibles, in-game assets) | Standard 7 — Personal Property Appraisal | Market approach with trait analysis |
| Tokenized securities | Standard 9 — Business Appraisal | Income or market approach |
| DeFi positions (liquidity pools, staking) | Standard 7 or 9 | Hybrid (income + market) |

**USPAP Standard 7** (Personal Property Appraisal, Development) governs the development of opinions of value for personal property, which is the appropriate classification for most digital assets. Standard 7 requires the appraiser to:

- Identify the client and intended users of the appraisal
- Identify the intended use of the appraisal
- Identify the specific property being appraised (with sufficient specificity to distinguish it from other property)
- Identify the real or personal property interest being appraised
- Identify the effective date of the appraisal and the date of the report
- Identify the scope of work necessary to produce credible results
- Identify and reconcile any extraordinary assumptions or hypothetical conditions
- Develop an opinion of value using appropriate valuation approaches

**USPAP Standard 8** (Personal Property Appraisal, Reporting) governs the communication of appraisal results, requiring the report to contain sufficient information to enable the intended user to understand the appraisal and the appraiser's analysis.

### USPAP Advisory Opinion 41 (2026)

The Appraisal Standards Board adopted Advisory Opinion 41 (AO-41) on April 23, 2026, superseding AO-18 and AO-37. AO-41 provides modernized guidance on technology use in appraisals — directly relevant to digital asset valuation. Key provisions include:

1. **Appraiser Responsibility:** The appraiser bears ultimate responsibility for the valuation conclusion, regardless of which technological tools (blockchain explorers, DeFi analytics platforms, trading APIs) were used in the analysis.

2. **Competency:** Appraisers must demonstrate competency in the technological tools they employ. For digital asset appraisers, this means proficiency with blockchain data tools and market analytics platforms.

3. **Confidentiality:** When cloud-based appraisal tools or third-party APIs are used, the appraiser must ensure that confidential client information — including wallet addresses and portfolio composition — remains protected.

4. **Workfile Documentation:** All data inputs and analytical steps must be documented in the appraisal workfile, including the specific sources of exchange pricing data, the timestamps of data collection, and any adjustments made to raw market data.

AO-41 reinforces a principle that is fundamental to digital asset appraisal: the appraiser's professional judgment, not the raw output of a software tool, is what creates value in the appraisal. This principle is directly relevant to the distinction between crypto tax software and qualified appraisals discussed in Part VI.

## Part V: The Penalty Framework — Why Compliance Matters

### Accuracy-Related Penalties Under IRC §6662

The consequences of failing to obtain a qualified appraisal extend well beyond a denied deduction. IRC §6662 imposes accuracy-related penalties on underpayments attributable to:

| Violation | Penalty Rate | Applicability |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Negligence or disregard of rules | 20% | Failure to obtain required appraisal |
| Substantial understatement of income tax | 20% | Understatement >10% of correct tax (or $5,000) |
| Substantial valuation misstatement | 20% | Claimed value >150% of correct value |
| Gross valuation misstatement | **40%** | Claimed value >200% of correct value |

For digital asset valuations, the **gross valuation misstatement** penalty is a particular concern. When a taxpayer reports the FMV of cryptocurrency based on an exchange price on a single date, without the methodological rigor of a qualified appraisal, the IRS may later determine that the correct FMV was materially different — particularly for illiquid assets or during periods of high volatility. A 40% penalty on a $100,000 underpayment means $40,000 in penalties alone, before interest.

### The Reasonable Cause Defense

IRC §170(f)(11)(A)(ii)(II) provides a reasonable cause exception that may preserve a deduction even without a qualified appraisal. However, the Tax Court and IRS have construed this exception narrowly. In CCA 202302012, the IRS Chief Counsel explicitly stated:

> "the reasonable cause exception was not intended to provide taxpayers with the choice of whether to obtain a qualified appraisal, but to provide relief where an unsuccessful attempt was made in good faith to comply with the requirements of section 170."

The Tax Court's decision in *Schweizer v. Commissioner*, T.C. Memo. 2022-102, reinforces this interpretation: a taxpayer who simply chooses not to obtain a qualified appraisal — believing exchange prices to be sufficient — has not exercised "ordinary business care and prudence" and cannot invoke the reasonable cause defense.

### Statute of Limitations Concerns

Under Treas. Reg. §301.6501(c)-1(f), the statute of limitations for assessing additional tax does **not begin to run** unless the taxpayer makes "adequate disclosure" of the gift or transaction. For gift tax purposes, a qualified appraisal is a cornerstone of adequate disclosure. Without it:

- The IRS can assess additional gift tax **at any time** — there is no statute of limitations.
- Interest accrues from the original due date of the return.
- Penalties compound on the underlying underpayment.

This open-ended exposure is particularly dangerous for digital asset transfers, where valuation disputes may arise years after the transaction and the relevant market data may be more difficult to reconstruct.

## Part VI: Crypto Tax Software vs. Qualified Appraisal — A Systematic Comparison

This is perhaps the most consequential distinction for digital asset holders. The proliferation of crypto tax software platforms — CoinTracker, CoinLedger, Koinly, TaxBit, ZenLedger, and others — has created widespread confusion about what constitutes a qualified appraisal. Below is a systematic comparison:

| Attribute | Crypto Tax Software Report | Qualified Appraisal (QDAV) |
|-----------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Legal Status** | Tax preparation support tool | IRS-recognized valuation document |
| **Valuation Source** | Single exchange price feed | Multi-source data aggregation with reconciliation |
| **Methodology** | Automated — price at time of transaction | Professional — USPAP-compliant Standards 7 & 9 |
| **Asset Coverage** | Liquid exchange-traded coins only | All digital assets (crypto, NFTs, DeFi, staked assets) |
| **Illiquid Asset Handling** | Not supported — no trait analysis | Trait rarity analysis, comparable sales, market adjustment |
| **Appraiser Analysis** | None — automated algorithm | Professional appraiser judgment and reconciliation |
| **USPAP Compliance** | No — no USPAP procedures followed | Yes — fully USPAP-compliant development and reporting |
| **Audit Defense Value** | Minimal — "machine output, no human judgment" | High — signed certification with appraiser credentials |
| **Penalty Protection** | None — does not establish good faith | Strong — demonstrates due diligence and professional standards |
| **Form 8283 Compatibility** | Does not satisfy | Fully compatible with Section B requirements |
| **Cost** | $0-$499 (tax software subscription) | Professional appraisal fee (commensurate with asset complexity) |
| **IRS Recognition** | Unrecognized as appraisal | Explicitly recognized under §170(f)(11)(E)(i) |

### Why Software Cannot Produce a Qualified Appraisal

The fundamental reason crypto tax software cannot produce a qualified appraisal is structural: **an algorithm cannot be a qualified appraiser.** The statutory requirements under §170(f)(11)(E) — professional designation, regular practice, verifiable expertise, Circular 230 eligibility — are attributes of a human professional, not a software application.

Software output can be a **data input** to a qualified appraisal — the appraiser may use exchange pricing data, transaction histories, and portfolio snapshots as raw material — but it cannot substitute for the professional analysis, methodology selection, market reconciliation, and certification that constitute a qualified appraisal.

This principle was affirmed in the Tax Court's reasoning in *Pankratz* and *Schweizer*, where reliance on incomplete or automated documentation was rejected as a substitute for proper appraisal compliance. The court's skepticism toward "software did it" defenses is only likely to intensify as crypto tax software becomes more prevalent and the IRS becomes more sophisticated in identifying returns that lack proper appraisal substantiation.

### Practical Implications

For a taxpayer holding $2 million in a diversified digital asset portfolio:

- **Crypto tax software** provides cost basis tracking and gain/loss calculations for annual income tax reporting. This is useful and often necessary for compliance with Form 1099-DA reconciliation.
- **A qualified appraisal** provides the FMV determination required for:
  - Charitable donation substantiation on Form 8283
  - Gift tax reporting on Form 709
  - Estate tax reporting on Form 706
  - Trust funding and administration
  - IRA annual valuation for SDIRA compliance
  - Audit defense documentation

The two serve complementary but distinct functions. Using crypto tax software in lieu of a qualified appraisal for substantiation purposes is analogous to using a stock ticker as a substitute for a formal business valuation — the ticker provides a data point, but it does not constitute a professionally developed opinion of value.

## Part VII: Form 8283 and Documentation Requirements

### Completing Section B of Form 8283

When a qualified appraisal is obtained in the charitable donation context, the taxpayer must attach Form 8283 (Noncash Charitable Contributions) to their tax return. Section B of the form requires:

1. **Donee information:** Name, address, and taxpayer identification number of the charitable organization.
2. **Donor declaration:** Signature attesting to the accuracy of the information provided.
3. **Detailed property description:** For digital assets, this should include the type of asset, blockchain identifier, quantity, and any identifying characteristics (such as token ID for NFTs).
4. **Date of contribution:** The actual date the digital assets were transferred to the charity.
5. **Date acquired by donor:** For held-for-investment assets, the original acquisition date.
6. **How acquired:** Typically "purchase" or "exchange."
7. **Donor's cost or adjusted basis:** The original acquisition cost.
8. **Fair market value:** The FMV on the date of contribution, as determined by the qualified appraisal.
9. **Appraiser's signature and credentials:** Including the appraiser's USPAP certification, professional designations, and taxpayer identification number.

The appraiser must sign Part IV of Form 8283, certifying under penalties of perjury that:
- The appraiser is a qualified appraiser under §170(f)(11)(E)
- The appraisal fee is not based on a percentage of the appraised value
- The appraiser has not been prohibited from practicing before the IRS

### Substantiation for Contributions Over $500,000

For property contributions exceeding $500,000 in claimed value, IRC §170(f)(11)(D) requires that the qualified appraisal itself be attached to the return — not merely summarized on Form 8283. For a high-value digital asset donation (a Bored Ape Yacht Club NFT valued at $600,000, for example), the full USPAP-compliant appraisal report must accompany the tax return.

## Part VIII: Practical Guidance for Digital Asset Holders

### When to Obtain a Qualified Appraisal

Digital asset holders should obtain a qualified appraisal in the following circumstances:

1. **Charitable donations** of digital assets exceeding $5,000 in value (per IRC §170(f)(11)(C)).
2. **Trust funding** with digital assets where the transfer exceeds the annual gift tax exclusion ($19,000 per donee in 2026).
3. **Estate planning** — date-of-death valuations for Form 706 reporting.
4. **Annual SDIRA valuations** for self-directed IRAs holding digital assets.
5. **Divorce proceedings** where digital assets constitute a significant portion of marital property.
6. **Business dissolution or partnership separation** involving digital asset holdings.

### How to Verify a Qualified Appraisal

Taxpayers, attorneys, and CPAs should verify that any appraisal submitted for tax purposes meets the following criteria:

- ✅ The appraiser holds a professional designation from a recognized appraisal organization (ASA, ISA, AAA, or equivalent).
- ✅ The appraiser demonstrates verifiable experience in the specific digital asset class being valued.
- ✅ The appraisal follows USPAP Standards 7 and 8 for personal property appraisal.
- ✅ The appraisal report includes the appraiser's certification and qualifications statement.
- ✅ The appraisal fee is not contingent on the reported value.
- ✅ The appraiser has not been prohibited from practice before the IRS under Circular 230.
- ✅ The appraisal is performed independently — the appraiser is not the taxpayer, the donee, or a party to the transaction.

### Red Flags: What to Avoid

- ⚠️ Appraisal fees calculated as a percentage of the reported value (prohibited by Treasury Regulations).
- ⚠️ "Appraisals" issued by crypto tax software platforms or exchanges.
- ⚠️ Reports that rely on a single data source for pricing without reconciliation.
- ⚠️ Appraisers who lack specific digital asset experience, even if they hold general appraisal credentials.
- ⚠️ Reports that do not reference USPAP standards in the methodology section.
- ⚠️ Valuations prepared after the tax return filing date (must be completed before the return is filed).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is a qualified appraisal for digital assets under IRS rules?**

A: A qualified appraisal is a formal valuation of digital assets conducted by a qualified appraiser in accordance with generally accepted appraisal standards (USPAP), as defined by IRC §170(f)(11)(E)(i). It requires a detailed property description, FMV determination with documented methodology, appraiser certification and qualification disclosure, and independence from the taxpayer and transaction.

**Q: Does crypto tax software produce a qualified appraisal?**

A: No. Crypto tax software platforms (CoinTracker, CoinLedger, Koinly, TaxBit, and others) produce tax preparation reports for cost basis and gain/loss calculations, but they do not produce qualified appraisals. An algorithm cannot satisfy the qualified appraiser requirements of IRC §170(f)(11)(E), and automated price feeds do not constitute USPAP-compliant valuation methodology.

**Q: What qualifications must a digital asset appraiser have?**

A: Under IRC §170(f)(11)(E)(ii)-(iii), a qualified appraiser must: (1) hold a professional designation from a recognized appraisal organization or meet minimum education and experience requirements; (2) regularly perform appraisals for compensation; (3) demonstrate verifiable education and experience in valuing the specific type of property (digital assets); and (4) not have been prohibited from IRS practice under Circular 230 within the prior three years.

**Q: What is the $5,000 threshold for qualified appraisals?**

A: IRC §170(f)(11)(C) requires a qualified appraisal for any property contribution for which a deduction exceeding $5,000 is claimed. Under aggregation rules (§170(f)(11)(F)), similar items of property donated to one or more donees are treated as a single property — meaning multiple digital asset donations in a tax year are aggregated for threshold purposes.

**Q: What penalties apply for not obtaining a qualified appraisal?**

A: Without a qualified appraisal, the taxpayer risks: (1) denial of the charitable deduction; (2) accuracy-related penalties of 20% under IRC §6662 for negligence or substantial valuation misstatement; (3) penalties of 40% for gross valuation misstatements (claimed value >200% of correct value); (4) indefinite statute of limitations for gift tax purposes under Treas. Reg. §301.6501(c)-1(f); and (5) interest on all underpayments from the original due date.

**Q: Can I use an exchange price as my crypto valuation for IRS purposes?**

A: For routine cost basis and gain/loss reporting on Form 1099-DA, exchange transaction prices are generally acceptable. However, for any purpose requiring substantiation of FMV — charitable donations, gift tax reporting, estate tax filings, trust funding, IRA valuations — an exchange price alone does not constitute a qualified appraisal. The IRS has explicitly stated in CCA 202302012 that exchange-reported values do not substitute for qualified appraisals.

**Q: What is USPAP and why does it matter for crypto appraisals?**

A: USPAP (Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice) is the generally accepted appraisal standards recognized by the Treasury Department under IRC §170(f)(11)(E)(i)(II). For digital assets, USPAP Standard 7 governs the development of personal property appraisals and Standard 8 governs their reporting. USDA, the certifying organization, mandates that appraisers complete annual USPAP continuing education to maintain their credentials.

**Q: How is Form 8283 used for digital asset donations?**

A: Form 8283 (Noncash Charitable Contributions) is the IRS form used to report charitable donations of property other than cash. Section B of the form must be completed when the claimed deduction exceeds $5,000 and requires: detailed property description, date of contribution, FMV, appraiser signature and credentials, donee acknowledgment, and donor declaration. The qualified appraisal must be completed before Form 8283 is signed.

**Q: What is the difference between a qualified appraisal and a crypto tax report?**

A: A crypto tax report calculates cost basis and capital gains/losses for income tax purposes, using transaction-level data and historical pricing. A qualified appraisal provides a professional opinion of fair market value for substantiation purposes, following USPAP methodology, signed by a qualified appraiser under IRC §170(f)(11)(E). They serve complementary but distinct functions: the tax report supports income tax compliance; the qualified appraisal supports FMV substantiation for deductions, gifts, and estate reporting.

**Q: When do I need a qualified appraisal vs. just reporting cost basis?**

A: You need a qualified appraisal whenever you must **substantiate the FMV** of digital assets for a purpose beyond routine income tax reporting. This includes: charitable donations >$5,000; gift tax reporting on Form 709; estate tax reporting on Form 706; trust funding and administration; SDIRA annual valuations; and any context where the valuation may be scrutinized by the IRS. For routine sales and exchanges, cost basis reporting via crypto tax software is sufficient.
