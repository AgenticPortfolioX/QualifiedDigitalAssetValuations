# Form 8971 and Schedule A for Digital Assets: The Executor's Basis-Reporting Duty Under IRC Section 6035, the Consistent-Basis Rule of Section 1014(f), the Section 6662 Penalties That Reach Heirs, and Where a Qualified Appraisal Becomes the Value Everyone Must Use

*Published: October 6, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

An estate administration can be run cleanly for a year and still fail at a single line. The line is the value an executor puts on an inherited asset in the report that goes to the Internal Revenue Service and, separately, to every beneficiary who receives that asset. For cash, the line is trivial. For real estate, it is supported by an appraisal the estate already expects to obtain. For digital assets — bitcoin, ether, stablecoins, NFTs, tokens held in DeFi positions, and the staking or lending balances that sit inside them — the line is where estates quietly come apart.

The reporting instrument is **Form 8971** and its attached **Schedule A**. The governing statutes are **IRC section 6035**, which compels the report, and **IRC section 1014(f)**, which makes the reported value the ceiling on the beneficiary's tax basis. Together they convert a routine estate-tax filing into a fiduciary duty with two audiences: the government, and the heirs. And because the value that must be reported is the *fair market value* of the digital assets as finally determined for estate tax purposes, the duty ends where the appraisal begins.

This analysis is written for executors, personal representatives, estate attorneys, CPAs, and trust officers who administer estates that hold digital assets. It sets out who must file, what must be reported, the deadline, the value that goes in the box, the consequences of getting that value wrong — for the estate and for the heir — and the point at which a USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal stops being optional and becomes the number everyone must use.

## The Two Statutes That Sit Behind the Form

Section 1014(a) is the familiar rule: the basis of property acquired from a decedent is its fair market value at the date of death. That is the "step-up," and it is why heirs of a long-held bitcoin position can generally sell without the capital gain the decedent would have recognized. Section 1014(f), added in 2015, is the check on that benefit. It provides that the basis of certain property acquired from a decedent **cannot exceed** the property's *final value* for federal estate tax purposes or, if no final value has yet been determined, the value **reported on the required statement**. The statement is the Schedule A. The ceiling is the estate's own number.

Section 6035 supplies the machinery. The executor of any estate *required* to file an estate tax return under section 6018 must furnish to the Secretary of the Treasury and to each person acquiring an interest in property included in the decedent's gross estate a statement identifying the value of each interest as reported on the return. Practitioners know this statement as Schedule A to Form 8971. The consistent-basis requirement of section 1014(f) and the reporting requirement of section 6035 were designed as a pair: the Service learns the values the estate has adopted, and each heir is told, in writing, the basis the estate's own reporting has fixed.

Two refinements matter before anything else. First, only property whose value *increases the estate tax payable* — after allowable credits — is "consistent basis property" within the meaning of section 1014(f). Property that qualifies for the marital deduction or the charitable deduction must still be *reported*, but it is not consistent basis property, because it does not raise the tax. Second, the requirement attaches to the estate's *obligation* to file, not to the fact of filing: an estate that files only to elect portability, to allocate generation-skipping transfer tax exemption, or as a protective filing is **not** subject to section 6035 reporting or to the section 1014(f) cap.

## Who Must File Form 8971 — and Who Is Exempt

Form 8971 is required of the executor, personal representative, or administrator of an estate that must file Form 706 or Form 706-NA under section 6018(a) or 6018(b). Where no executor has been appointed, or where one executor cannot assemble complete information, every person in actual or constructive possession of the decedent's property can be treated as an executor, and more than one Form 8971 may be required for the same estate.

The obligation turns on the section 6018 filing threshold. For decedents dying in **2026**, the basic exclusion amount is **$15,000,000** per person. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, set that figure by statute, made it permanent, and indexed it for inflation beginning in 2027; it replaced the $13,990,000 amount that applied for 2025 and eliminated the scheduled 2026 sunset. An estate whose gross estate, adjusted taxable gifts, and specific exemption together fall below the applicable basic exclusion amount is not required to file a return — and therefore has no Form 8971 obligation and no section 1014(f) ceiling on its beneficiaries' basis.

Three categories of filing do **not** create a Form 8971 duty, even though a return is filed: returns filed solely to allocate or elect with respect to the generation-skipping transfer tax; returns filed solely to elect portability of the deceased spousal exclusion amount; and protective filings made to avoid a penalty or to satisfy a state law requirement. The practical consequence is a clean dividing line. If the estate **must** file a Form 706, the executor inherits a penalty-backed duty to value and report the digital assets. If the estate is under the threshold, or is filing only for portability or protection, the duty does not arise — but, as discussed below, the absence of a Schedule A is not a gift to the heirs. It simply moves the burden of proving the step-up onto them.

## The Deadline, the Filing Address, and the Schedule A

The due date is unforgiving because it is drafted to run against whichever event comes first. Form 8971 must be filed with the IRS, and each required Schedule A furnished to the beneficiary, **no later than the earlier of** (i) 30 days after the date the Form 706 is required to be filed, *including extensions*, or (ii) 30 days after the date the Form 706 is actually filed. An executor who extends the estate tax return and files at the extended due date has 30 days from that date. An executor who files early compresses the deadline and must furnish Schedules A within 30 days of filing. If the due date lands on a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday, filing on the next business day is permitted.

Form 8971 is filed with the IRS at Mail Stop 824G, 7940 Kentucky Drive, Florence, KY 41042, separately from every other return the estate files. A complete submission includes a copy of each Schedule A furnished or required to be furnished. The executor must furnish the Schedule A to each individual beneficiary, each trustee of a beneficiary trust, and each executor of a beneficiary estate — in person, by email, by U.S. mail to the last known address, or by a designated private delivery service. The executor then certifies on Form 8971, Part II, column (d), the date the Schedule A was provided, and retains proof of mailing or delivery.

One rule is stated in the instructions in capital letters of intent, if not type: furnish to a beneficiary **only** that beneficiary's Schedule A. The Form 8971 itself is never given to a beneficiary. It identifies all the heirs and their property; it is a return, not a disclosure to the family. An executor must also file the Form 8971 even when every item subject to reporting is excepted property — in that case the Form reports that the property is excepted, and no Schedule A need be furnished for it.

## Why Digital Assets Are the Hardest Line on the Form

The property subject to reporting is *included property* — property whose value is included in the decedent's gross estate — together with any property the basis of which is determined by reference to included property, such as property acquired in a like-kind exchange or an involuntary conversion. Digital assets held personally by the decedent are included property. Bitcoin in a self-custody wallet, ether on an exchange account, a blue-chip NFT, a tokenized position in a DeFi lending pool: all of it is part of the gross estate and, where a Form 706 is required, all of it is subject to reporting.

The exception that swallows so much of the analysis is the definition of *excepted property* in Treasury Regulation section 1.6035-1(f)(2). Excepted property is subject only to limited reporting — the executor notes on the Form 8971 that the property is excepted and does not itemize it on a Schedule A. The fourteen categories are, with few surprises, dollar-denominated or basis-disconnected: U.S. dollars; U.S. dollar-denominated demand deposits; U.S. dollar certificates of deposit; U.S. dollar cash collateral held by a third party; U.S. dollar money-market funds; life insurance proceeds payable in a lump sum in U.S. dollars; federal, state, and local tax refunds payable in U.S. dollars; notes forgiven in full at death; household and personal effects for which no appraisal is required; property completely sold, exchanged, or otherwise disposed of in a recognition event before distribution; property whose initial basis is not derived from estate-tax fair market value (annuities, income in respect of a decedent, installment obligations, certain appreciated property acquired within one year of death, passive foreign investment company stock, and U.S. dollar retirement-plan interests); bonds redeemed for U.S. dollars before distribution; property included in the gross estate of a beneficiary who predeceased the filing deadline; and any property later designated by published guidance.

**Digital assets are not on that list.** A cryptocurrency, an NFT, or a DeFi position is not a U.S. dollar, is not a demand deposit, and is not a basis-disconnected interest. Unless the executor disposes of it before distribution — the "liquidation trap" examined below — it is reportable property that must be valued and placed on the beneficiary's Schedule A.

That is precisely why digital assets are the hardest line. The estate must arrive at a defensible **fair market value** for property that has no closing bell, trades continuously across venues and borders, and may include positions whose "price" is an abstraction — the floor of a collection, the mark on a lending pool, a governance token with limited liquidity. The estate tax value that goes on the Form 706, and is carried forward onto the Schedule A, is a *determination* that must be supported the way any other appraisal is: by a reasoned methodology, a market analysis, and a workfile of defensible data.

## What Value Goes in the Box: Estate Tax Value and Final Value

The instructions define two terms that govern the number. The **estate tax value** is the value of the property as reported on the Form 706 (or Form 706-NA, or a supplement to it) until the property's final value is determined; once final value is determined, the estate tax value *becomes* the final value. The **final value** is the value as finally determined for purposes of the section 1014(f) consistent-basis rule. It is final when one of four things happens: the value reported on the return stands after the period of limitations on assessment expires without a timely adjustment; an IRS-determined value stands after that period expires without a timely contest by the executor; a value is fixed by a written agreement with the Service that binds all parties; or a value is fixed by a court whose determination can no longer be appealed.

The practical reading is that the Schedule A value is a *provisional* number at first and a *binding* number later. Until final value is determined, the estate reports the Form 706 value, and the beneficiary's basis cannot exceed that reported value under section 1014(f). If the value is later adjusted — on examination, by agreement, or by a court — the executor's duty to supplement, discussed below, brings the Schedule A into line, and the beneficiary's ceiling moves with it.

For digital assets, the reported value is the product of the appraisal work, and the quality of that work is the quality of the ceiling. An estate that reports a value higher than fair market value overstates the estate tax and understates nothing on the income side — the heir gets a high basis but the estate paid tax it did not owe. An estate that reports a value lower than fair market value may pay less estate tax but hands the heir a low ceiling, and the heir pays the difference as capital gain on a later sale, often at a rate that dwarfs the estate tax saved. The line is not rhetorical: **the appraisal is the number, and the number runs in both directions**.

### Exchange statements are not appraisals

A recurring objection is that the decedent's exchange already reports a value. It does not report the right one. A custodial statement reflects an account balance, not a date-of-death fair market value determined under professional standards, and it carries no appraiser signature, no methodology, and no reconciliation across wallets, venues, and positions. Where fair market value is the standard, the work product the Service and the heir receive is a **qualified appraisal** built on a workfile of **defensible data**, not a screenshot.

## The Consistent-Basis Rule: Why the Schedule A Number Becomes the Heir's Basis

Section 1014(f) is where the estate's valuation decision reaches the heir who did nothing wrong. The rule is one-directional: the beneficiary **cannot use a value higher** than the value reported on the Schedule A as the beneficiary's initial basis in the property. The estate's number is the ceiling. If the estate reports $4,000,000 of bitcoin, the heir's basis is capped at $4,000,000 even if the heir's own accounting suggests a higher date-of-death value, and even if the asset is later sold at a higher price. The appreciation above the reported value is the heir's gain.

That is the case for taking the valuation seriously on the estate side. But section 1014(f) does not operate alone, and it is important to be accurate about what it does *not* do. The final regulations under T.D. 9991, published September 17, 2024, and codified in Treasury Regulation section 1.1014-10, **eliminated the "zero basis" rule** that appeared in the proposed regulations and **eliminated the subsequent-transfer reporting requirement** for all beneficiaries other than trustees. Under the rules now governing returns filed after September 17, 2024, the failure to furnish a Schedule A does not automatically reduce an heir's basis to zero. The real exposures are the information-return penalties discussed below and the operation of the section 1014(f) ceiling itself.

What remains, and what should drive the engagement, is the ceiling. The estate's reported value binds the heir whether it is too high or too low, and the executor — as a fiduciary — is the person who selects it.

## The Liquidation Trap: Sell Before Distribution, and the Asset Drops Off the Form

The excepted-property list contains a category that does double duty: property that, before distribution from the estate or the decedent's revocable trust, is **completely sold, exchanged, or otherwise disposed of in one or more transactions that are recognition events** for federal income tax purposes. If the executor liquidates the digital assets and distributes cash, the property never reaches a Schedule A. The reporting duty evaporates. So, however, does much of the benefit the heir was entitled to.

| Executor's choice | Schedule A treatment | Income tax consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Distribute digital assets in kind | Reportable on Schedule A; value = estate tax value; heir's basis capped at that value under §1014(f) | Step-up preserved in the heir's hands; the heir owns the future appreciation and the future sale |
| Sell before distribution, distribute cash | Excepted property under Reg. §1.6035-1(f)(2)(x); no Schedule A item | The **estate** recognizes post-death gain or loss on the sale; the heir's step-up is limited to the cash received |

The choice is a valuation and tax decision, not an administrative convenience. Liquidating a large digital-asset position inside the estate converts what would have been the heir's capital gain into the estate's taxable gain — often taxed at compressed estate and trust rates — and it strips the heir of a stepped-up basis in the underlying asset. Where the estate is administratively able to distribute in kind, doing so preserves the step-up *and* creates the Schedule A duty. Both consequences flow from the same decision, and both should be made deliberately, with the appraisal in hand.

## The Penalty Architecture: Sections 6721, 6722, and the Trap for Heirs

Two information-return penalties attach to the executor, and one accuracy-related penalty attaches to the heir. Together they explain why the valuation work cannot be deferred to the day the return is signed.

**Section 6721 — failure to file a correct Form 8971 by the due date.** The penalty applies to a failure to file timely, a failure to include all required information, a failure to include correct information, or a failure to supplement timely. The base amount is **$250 per Form 8971**, adjusted annually for inflation. For returns due in calendar year 2026, the tiered rates under Rev. Proc. 2024-40 run **$60** (corrected within 30 days of the due date), **$130** (corrected after 30 days but on or before August 1), and **$340** (corrected after August 1), subject to annual caps, with a higher amount for intentional disregard. Only one penalty applies to each Form 8971, but the initial filing and each supplement are separate filings.

**Section 6722 — failure to furnish a correct Schedule A to a beneficiary by the due date.** The same structure applies, per Schedule A and per supplement, with the same base amount and the same inflation-adjusted tiers. A late or incorrect Schedule A can be penalized even where the estate tax return itself was correct — and even where no estate tax was due.

Both penalties yield to a **reasonable-cause** exception: the executor must show that the failure was due to an event beyond the executor's control or to significant mitigating factors, that the executor acted responsibly before and after the failure, and that steps were taken to avoid it. Two protective rules also apply. An **inconsequential** error or omission is excused — but the instructions warn that errors relating to a taxpayer identification number, a beneficiary's surname, and, critically, **the value of the asset the beneficiary is receiving are never inconsequential**. A **de minimis** safe harbor disregards an inadvertent dollar error of not more than **$100**.

**Section 6662 — the penalty that reaches the heir.** A beneficiary who reports a basis in property that is inconsistent with the amount on the Schedule A may be liable for a **20 percent** accuracy-related penalty under section 6662. A beneficiary who reports a basis that is **200 percent or more** of the correct amount may instead face the **40 percent** gross-valuation-misstatement penalty under section 6662(h). This is the sharp edge of the regime, and it is the reason the estate's figure is not merely the estate's problem: the Schedule A the executor furnishes becomes the benchmark against which the heir's own return is judged, and an heir who cannot reconcile to it, or who claims a basis the estate never supported, inherits the exposure. A **qualified appraisal** that establishes the value, documents the methodology, and survives into the heir's file is the single most effective defense against both the executor's penalties and the heir's.

## The Obligation That Never Ends: Supplementing Form 8971

The duty to report is not discharged by the initial filing. The executor must supplement the Form 8971 and furnish a supplemental Schedule A when a beneficiary acquires reportable property not previously reported, or when a change to previously reported information makes it incorrect or incomplete.

Three deadlines govern the supplements:

- **Property acquired after the due date** is reported, and the supplement filed, **on or before January 31** of the year following the beneficiary's acquisition.
- **A change to the information** — a corrected value, a newly discovered asset, a corrected beneficiary — is reported within **30 days** after the date the information becomes available to the executor. For a change arising from supplementing the estate tax return, that date is the filing date of the supplemental information; for a change arising from a determination of final value, it is the date the value becomes final.
- **The duty continues** until final value has been determined for all reportable property or, if later, until all reportable property has been acquired by a beneficiary.

For digital assets this ongoing duty is not theoretical. A wallet discovered after the return is filed, an airdrop received by the estate, a token whose value is adjusted on examination, a DeFi position unwound during administration: each can trigger a supplement. The executor who engaged a valuation early, and who keeps an inventory of the estate's holdings current, meets the supplement deadlines as a matter of record-keeping. The executor who did not is exposed to a running section 6721 and section 6722 problem.

## The Threshold Inversion: When No Form 706 Is Filed, the Heir Stands Alone

Because the 2026 basic exclusion is $15,000,000, a far larger share of estates will never file a Form 706 — and those estates have no section 6035 duty and no section 1014(f) ceiling on their beneficiaries. It is tempting to read that as the problem disappearing. It does not. It relocates.

An estate under the threshold still passes digital assets to heirs who receive a stepped-up basis under section 1014(a). What those heirs do not receive is a Schedule A — a professionally prepared, IRS-filed statement of the date-of-death value. If the heir later sells the assets and reports a basis, the heir must substantiate that basis from the estate's records. Where the only records are exchange exports and portfolio screenshots, the heir bears the entire burden of proving the date-of-death **fair market value** in an audit of the heir's own return, years after the fact, without the estate's valuation work behind it.

Here the calculus inverts in the appraisal's favor. For the filing estate, the qualified appraisal is compelled by the reporting duty. For the non-filing estate, it is voluntary — and it is the only durable record of the step-up. A contemporaneous, USPAP-compliant appraisal obtained at the date of death gives the heir a **defensible data** foundation and the executor a clean record of fiduciary conduct, whether or not a Form 8971 is ever required.

## Metro Detroit and Michigan: The Fiduciary Standard at Home

Michigan imposes no state estate tax, so the entire reporting obligation for a Metro Detroit estate that crosses the federal threshold runs through the federal regime. That does not simplify the fiduciary's work; it concentrates it. A Michigan personal representative probating an estate under the Estates and Protected Individuals Code files the inventory required by MCL 700.3706 and the PC 577 inventory, and the digital-asset values entered there should be the same values the executor carries to the Form 706 and, where required, to the Schedule A. When those numbers diverge — because the inventory and the return were prepared from different sources — the executor creates an internal inconsistency that a beneficiary or an examiner can exploit.

For the estate attorneys, CPAs, and trust officers across Oakland, Wayne, and Genesee Counties who administer digital-asset estates, the durable practice is to fix the value once, at the correct date, with a USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal, and to use that number consistently across the inventory, the Form 706, and the Form 8971. An estate that does so has one value and one workfile. An estate that does not has several numbers and no defensible lineage between them.

## What Executors and Their Counsel Should Do Before the Value Is Set

The sequence is straightforward, and it should begin early in the administration rather than at the deadline.

First, determine whether the estate is **required** to file a Form 706 under section 6018, applying the $15,000,000 basic exclusion for 2026 and the statutory additions for adjusted taxable gifts and specific exemption. That single determination controls whether sections 6035 and 1014(f) apply at all.

Second, complete the inventory of digital assets before the value is fixed: exchange accounts, self-custody wallets, NFT holdings, DeFi positions, staking and lending balances, and any tokens acquired in the year of death. The inventory is the input to the appraisal, and a missing wallet produces a missing value.

Third, decide the in-kind-versus-liquidation question deliberately, understanding that liquidation moves the asset into the excepted-property category and shifts the post-death gain to the estate, while in-kind distribution preserves the heir's step-up and creates the Schedule A duty.

Fourth, engage the appraisal at the correct valuation date — the date of death, or the alternate valuation date if the estate elects it under section 2032 — and require a report developed under the substance and principles of USPAP, with the methodology, market evidence, and reconciliation the estate's own filing will depend on.

Fifth, calendar the Form 8971 deadline against whichever comes first — the extended due date of the return or the actual filing date — and the supplement triggers, including the January 31 date for late-acquired property.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Form 8971 apply to cryptocurrency and other digital assets?

Yes, provided the estate is required to file a Form 706. Cryptocurrency, NFTs, and DeFi positions are included property — part of the gross estate — and they are not among the excepted-property categories in Treasury Regulation section 1.6035-1(f)(2). If the estate distributes them in kind, they must be valued and reported on each beneficiary's Schedule A; if the executor sells them before distribution, they become excepted property.

### What value does the executor report for digital assets on Schedule A?

The estate tax value — the value as reported on the Form 706 until the property's final value is determined, and the final value after it is determined. For digital assets that value must be a defensible fair market value determined by a qualified appraisal at the applicable valuation date, not an exchange account balance or a portfolio screenshot.

### How long does an executor have to file Form 8971?

No later than 30 days after the earlier of (i) the date the Form 706 is required to be filed, including extensions, or (ii) the date the Form 706 is actually filed. Filing the return early shortens the window; if the due date falls on a weekend or holiday, the next business day is permitted.

### What happens to an heir's basis if the estate under-reports the value?

The heir's basis is capped at the value the estate reported on Schedule A. Section 1014(f) prevents the beneficiary from using a basis higher than the reported estate tax value, so an understated value hands the heir a lower step-up and a larger capital gain on a later sale.

### What is the penalty for reporting a basis inconsistent with Schedule A?

A beneficiary who reports a basis inconsistent with the Schedule A may be liable for a 20 percent accuracy-related penalty under section 6662. If the reported basis is 200 percent or more of the correct amount, the 40 percent gross-valuation-misstatement penalty under section 6662(h) may apply instead.

### Is the "zero basis" rule still a concern?

Not for estates whose returns are filed after September 17, 2024. The final regulations under T.D. 9991 eliminated the zero-basis rule that appeared in the proposed regulations and removed the subsequent-transfer reporting requirement for all beneficiaries other than trustees. The live exposures are the section 6721 and 6722 penalties and the section 1014(f) ceiling.

### Do I need Form 8971 if my estate is under the estate tax exclusion?

No. Form 8971 is required only when the estate must file a Form 706. An estate below the $15,000,000 basic exclusion for 2026 — or one filing only to elect portability or for protection — has no section 6035 duty and no section 1014(f) ceiling. Its heirs still receive a stepped-up basis but must substantiate it from their own records, which is why a contemporaneous qualified appraisal remains valuable.

### Is an exchange statement or price tracker enough to value inherited crypto?

No. An exchange statement or price screen is not a qualified appraisal. It carries no appraiser signature, no methodology, and no reconciliation across wallets, venues, and positions. Where the standard is fair market value — on a section 6035 filing, on a Form 706, and in any audit of the heir's basis — the defensible record is a USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal.

## Conclusion

Form 8971 is short, and it is easy to treat as clerical. It is not. The value an executor places on an inherited digital asset, and certifies to the IRS and to each beneficiary, becomes the estate tax value the Service can check, the section 1014(f) ceiling the heir cannot exceed, and the benchmark against which the heir's own return will be measured under section 6662. For cash and marketable securities the line is set by the market. For cryptocurrency, NFTs, and DeFi positions there is no such convenience: the line is set by the appraisal, and it holds only as well as the appraisal is built.

For a Metro Detroit estate, or any estate that holds digital assets and must file a Form 706, the disciplined path is the same. Determine the filing obligation. Inventory the holdings. Decide the liquidation question with the tax consequences in view. Fix the value at the correct date with a qualified appraisal. Carry one number — the appraised value — through the inventory, the estate tax return, and the Schedule A, and keep the workfile that makes it defensible. Do that, and the schedule the executor files becomes not a formality but the record that protects the estate and the heirs alike. The premise that governs a charitable bequest under IRS Section 170 is the same premise that governs the estate: the value must be supportable, and a **qualified appraisal** is how that support is built.
