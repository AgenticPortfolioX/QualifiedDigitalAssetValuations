# IRS Cost Basis Reporting for Digital Assets Under the New 2026 Rules: Wallet-by-Wallet Tracking, Specific Identification, and When a Qualified Appraisal Remains Essential for Compliance

*Published: July 28, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

## Introduction: The Landmark Shift in Digital Asset Tax Reporting

The 2026 tax year represents a watershed moment for digital asset taxation in the United States. For the first time, brokers are required to report **cost basis information** for digital asset transactions — a regulatory change that brings cryptocurrency, NFT, and other digital asset reporting closer to the standards that have governed traditional securities for decades.

Adopted through final regulations issued by the Treasury Department and the IRS in July 2024 (TD 9995), these rules went into effect on January 1, 2026, and are now shaping the first filing season in which taxpayers and their advisors must reconcile broker-reported cost basis with their own transaction records. The change is significant: the elimination of "universal wallet" accounting, the introduction of mandatory wallet-by-wallet cost basis tracking, and the implementation of specific identification requirements represent the most consequential regulatory development in digital asset taxation since IRS Notice 2014-21 first established that cryptocurrencies are treated as property for federal tax purposes.

For estate attorneys, CPAs, trust officers, and high-net-worth individuals who hold or advise on digital asset portfolios, understanding these rules is not optional — it is a fiduciary imperative. And critically, despite the enhanced reporting regime, a **qualified appraisal** remains indispensable for a wide range of digital asset transactions.

This guide provides a comprehensive, authority-supported analysis of the new cost basis reporting requirements, their practical implications, and the specific scenarios in which a USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal remains the only defensible documentation for IRS purposes.

## Section 1: The New Cost Basis Reporting Framework

### 1.1 What Changed on January 1, 2026

Effective for transactions occurring on or after January 1, 2026, brokers that take possession of digital assets being sold by their customers must report both **gross proceeds** and **adjusted cost basis** on Form 1099-DA. This is a phased expansion of the reporting requirements that began with the 2025 tax year, when brokers were required to report gross proceeds only.

The final regulations apply to:

- **Custodial digital asset trading platforms** (centralized exchanges such as Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini)
- **Hosted wallet providers** that control customer private keys
- **Digital asset kiosks** (crypto ATMs)
- **Certain processors of digital asset payments** (PDAPs)

The regulations explicitly do **not** apply to decentralized or non-custodial brokers that do not take possession of the digital assets being sold or exchanged. The Treasury Department has indicated that rules for these brokers may follow in a separate regulatory action — but for now, DeFi transactions remain outside the broker reporting framework, even though the transactions themselves remain fully taxable.

### 1.2 Covered vs. Noncovered Digital Assets

A critical distinction under the new rules is the classification of digital assets as **covered** or **noncovered**:

| Classification | Definition | Cost Basis Reporting |
|----------------|------------|---------------------|
| **Covered** | Digital assets acquired and held within the same broker account **on or after January 1, 2026** | Mandatory — broker must report both gross proceeds and adjusted cost basis |
| **Noncovered** | Digital assets acquired before January 1, 2026, or transferred into the broker account from another wallet or exchange | Optional — broker may report cost basis but is not required to do so |

This distinction creates a significant compliance challenge for taxpayers who hold digital assets acquired across multiple platforms, through different time periods, or via transfers between wallets. Assets moved into a broker account from a self-custody wallet remain noncovered, even if the broker otherwise reports cost basis for covered assets held within that account.

### 1.3 The Phased Implementation Timeline

Understanding the implementation timeline is essential for reconciling the forms that taxpayers are receiving in 2026:

| Tax Year | Forms Issued | Reporting Requirement |
|-----------|-------------|----------------------|
| 2025 | Early 2026 | Gross proceeds only (no cost basis required) |
| 2026 | Early 2027 | Gross proceeds + cost basis for covered assets |
| 2027+ | Early 2028+ | Full reporting (with expected system refinements) |

For the 2025 tax year — the forms currently being issued in 2026 — brokers were required to report gross proceeds only. Many taxpayers are receiving Form 1099-DA with gross proceeds filled in but cost basis fields left blank or marked as unavailable. This is expected and does not relieve the taxpayer of the obligation to accurately compute and report gain or loss.

Taxpayers should **not** assume that a blank cost basis field on a 2025 Form 1099-DA means the basis is zero. The broker simply was not required to report it. Taxpayers remain solely responsible for tracking and substantiating their own cost basis for all digital asset transactions, regardless of what appears on the form.

## Section 2: The End of the "Universal Wallet" — Mandatory Wallet-by-Wallet Tracking

### 2.1 What the Elimination of Universal Wallets Means

One of the most consequential changes introduced by the final regulations is the elimination of the "universal wallet" or pooled accounting method. Prior to these rules, some taxpayers and tax reporting platforms aggregated digital asset holdings across multiple wallets and accounts, applying a single cost basis method to the combined pool.

Under the final regulations, this approach is no longer permissible. Taxpayers must **physically trace** to a particular wallet or account the source of the digital assets being sold. Cost basis, holding period, and gain or loss must be determined separately for each wallet or account.

This requirement was reinforced by EY Principal Matthew Stevens during a December 2025 webinar, who noted that the final regulations "eliminated taxpayers' use of what was called 'universal wallets,'" requiring taxpayers to demonstrate the specific wallet from which a digital asset was acquired and later sold.

### 2.2 Practical Implications for Taxpayers

The wallet-by-wallet tracking requirement creates several concrete challenges:

**Challenge 1: Multi-Platform Holdings.** A taxpayer who acquired Bitcoin on Coinbase in 2020, transferred it to a self-custody hardware wallet in 2021, and then transferred it to Kraken in 2023 must trace the asset through all three locations. The cost basis established at acquisition carries through, but the wallet path must be documented.

**Challenge 2: Split Transactions.** When digital assets from multiple wallets are aggregated into a single transaction (e.g., transferring partial amounts from two wallets to fund a single purchase), each contributing wallet's basis must be separately tracked and reported.

**Challenge 3: Gifts and Inheritances.** Digital assets received as gifts carry the donor's carryover basis (for gain) — but that basis must be traced to the specific wallet from which the donor transferred the assets. For inherited digital assets, the basis is stepped up to fair market value at the date of death (or alternate valuation date), but the estate's executor must document which wallet held each asset at death.

**Challenge 4: Staking Rewards.** Cryptocurrency received as staking rewards is taxable as ordinary income at its fair market value when received. Each reward tranche establishes a new tax lot with its own cost basis (equal to the FMV at receipt) and holding period, and must be attributed to the specific wallet or protocol that generated it.

### 2.3 Revenue Procedure 2024-28: The Transitional Bridge

Recognizing the difficulty of transitioning from the prior accounting framework, the IRS issued Revenue Procedure 2024-28, which allows taxpayers to allocate units of **unused basis** to remaining digital asset units held within each wallet or account as of January 1, 2025.

This means that if a taxpayer held digital assets in multiple wallets before the transition date, they may allocate unused basis from prior transactions to the assets remaining in each wallet. This allocation must be made on a wallet-by-wallet basis and cannot be aggregated across wallets.

The practical effect is that taxpayers who properly document their wallet holdings as of January 1, 2025, have a clean starting point for the new tracking regime. Taxpayers who did not document wallet holdings by that date face a more complex reconstruction challenge.

## Section 3: Specific Identification — Choosing Which Units Are Sold

### 3.1 The Importance of Specific Identification

Under the final regulations, as with other forms of property, taxpayers may choose how to identify which units of a digital asset are being sold when executing a disposition. This choice has a material impact on the amount of taxable gain or loss reported.

The key identification methods available are:

**First-In, First-Out (FIFO):** The oldest units (by acquisition date) are deemed sold first. This is the default method if no specific identification is made. FIFO generally produces the highest taxable gain in a rising market because the oldest units typically have the lowest basis.

**Specific Identification:** The taxpayer specifically identifies which units (by acquisition date, wallet, and transaction hash) are being sold at the time of disposition. This allows for tax-efficient selection — for example, selling high-basis units to minimize gain, or low-basis units to realize losses.

**Last-In, First-Out (LIFO):** The most recently acquired units are deemed sold first. This may produce lower gains in a rising market but requires careful tracking.

### 3.2 Documentation Requirements

The IRS requires that specific identification be made by the date and time of sale and be supported by adequate records. For digital assets, this means:

- The taxpayer must identify the specific wallet or account from which the asset is being sold
- The identification must reference the acquisition transaction (purchase date, exchange, and amount)
- The records must be contemporaneous — retroactive identification after the sale date is not permitted

This is where the wallet-by-wallet tracking requirement intersects with specific identification. A taxpayer who holds Bitcoin in three wallets cannot simply say "I'm selling 1 BTC" and apply the highest-basis lot from any wallet. They must identify which wallet's Bitcoin is being sold and which specific tax lot within that wallet.

### 3.3 The Broker's Role in Lot Selection

Brokers are beginning to implement specific identification tools, but these systems may not be fully operational in 2026. EY's Tara Ferris has cautioned that "these systems may not be fully available right away," making it essential for taxpayers to maintain their own records rather than relying solely on broker-provided cost basis information.

Taxpayers should:

1. **Maintain a wallet-by-wallet tax lot ledger** documenting every acquisition, transfer, and disposition by wallet
2. **Record specific identification elections at the time of each sale** — not after the fact
3. **Reconcile broker-reported cost basis against personal records** and document any discrepancies
4. **Retain blockchain transaction hashes** for every acquisition and disposition

## Section 4: When Taxpayers Still Need a Qualified Appraisal

### 4.1 The Critical Misconception

One of the most dangerous assumptions emerging from the new cost basis reporting rules is that broker-reported cost basis eliminates the need for professional appraisals. This is **incorrect**.

Broker-reported cost basis serves a specific, limited function: it reports the acquisition cost of **covered assets** that were purchased and held within the same broker account. A qualified appraisal, by contrast, establishes the **fair market value** of digital assets for a specific date and purpose — typically a date that does not correspond to a purchase transaction — using USPAP-compliant methodology performed by a qualified appraiser.

The two serve fundamentally different purposes, and one does not substitute for the other.

### 4.2 Scenarios Requiring a Qualified Appraisal

#### 4.2.1 Charitable Contributions (IRC §170)

Under IRC Section 170(f)(11)(C), a qualified appraisal is required for any charitable contribution deduction exceeding $5,000. For deductions exceeding $500,000, the qualified appraisal must be attached to the tax return. IRS Chief Counsel Advice has specifically confirmed that cryptocurrency and digital asset donations fall within this requirement, and that reliance on exchange-reported value does not constitute a good-faith effort sufficient to invoke the reasonable-cause exception.

This means that a taxpayer donating $100,000 in Bitcoin to a donor-advised fund cannot rely on the exchange price at the time of transfer. A USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal, performed by a qualified appraiser meeting the standards of IRC Section 170(f)(11)(E), is required to substantiate the deduction.

Notably, legislation introduced in the House (H.R. 3633-related provisions and the broader PARITY Act discussions) has proposed exempting certain digital asset donations from the qualified appraisal requirement. As of July 2026, this proposal has not been enacted into law. The existing requirement remains in full force.

#### 4.2.2 Estate Tax Valuation (IRC §2031 and §2032)

For estates that include digital assets, the executor must report the fair market value of those assets on Form 706 (the federal estate tax return). The relevant valuation date is the date of death (IRC §2031) or the alternate valuation date (IRC §2032), if elected.

Broker-reported cost basis — which reflects what the decedent paid for the asset, not its value at death — is irrelevant for this purpose. The estate needs a **date-of-death appraisal** establishing fair market value as of the specific date the decedent died.

For estates with significant digital asset holdings, the difference between cost basis and date-of-death value can be substantial. A decedent who purchased Bitcoin at $10,000 and held it through a peak valuation at death requires a qualified appraisal establishing the date-of-death FMV, not the purchase price.

#### 4.2.3 Gift Tax Valuation (IRC §2512 and §2523)

Digital asset gifts must be reported on Form 709 at their fair market value on the date of the gift. While Form 709 does not require a qualified appraisal to be attached (unlike Form 8283 for charitable contributions), the failure to adequately disclose the valuation methodology prevents the statute of limitations from starting — leaving the donor exposed to an IRS valuation challenge indefinitely.

For gifts of:
- Liquid cryptocurrency exceeding the $19,000 annual exclusion (2025/2026)
- Illiquid digital assets such as NFTs or thinly traded tokens
- Digital assets with complex basis histories

A USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal provides the documentation necessary to start the statute of limitations and defend the reported value in the event of an audit.

#### 4.2.4 Self-Directed IRA Annual Valuation

Holders of self-directed IRAs (SDIRAs) that include digital assets are required to obtain an annual fair market value attestation for Form 5498 reporting. Exchange screenshots do not meet the standard for a qualified appraisal, and insufficient documentation creates SDIRA disqualification risk — a potentially catastrophic outcome that could trigger immediate taxation of the entire account balance.

#### 4.2.5 Noncovered Assets and Complex Portfolios

For noncovered digital assets — those acquired before January 1, 2026, or transferred between wallets — the broker may not report cost basis at all. Taxpayers holding assets across multiple platforms, wallets acquired through airdrops, hard forks, or other non-purchase events, or assets with complex basis histories face the most significant documentation challenges.

In these scenarios, a comprehensive portfolio valuation that reconstructs basis through transaction history, applies appropriate identification methodology, and documents fair market value at relevant dates provides the only complete defense against IRS scrutiny.

### 4.3 Decision Framework

| Scenario | Cost Basis Reporting Sufficient? | Qualified Appraisal Required? |
|----------|----------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Routine sale of covered asset within same broker account | Yes (for cost basis computation) | No |
| Charitable donation > $5,000 | No | Yes (IRC §170) |
| Estate tax filing (Form 706) | No | Yes (IRC §2031) |
| Gift of digital assets > annual exclusion | No | Strongly recommended |
| SDIRA annual valuation | No | Yes (custodian requirement) |
| Transfer of noncovered assets | Partial only | Recommended |
| Audit defense for prior-year filings | No | Yes |
| Trust funding with digital assets | No | Recommended |

## Section 5: The 2026 Filing Season — What Taxpayers and Their Advisors Are Experiencing

### 5.1 Form 1099-DA Reconciliation Realities

The 2026 filing season represents the first year of Form 1099-DA issuance, and early experience has revealed several practical challenges:

**Late Form Issuance.** The deadline for furnishing Form 1099-DA to taxpayers was February 17, 2026. Major exchanges including Coinbase and Kraken missed this deadline, with estimated issuer dates pushing into mid-March. The IRS confirmed it would not penalize exchanges for late filing in 2026 provided a good faith effort was made, but this left many taxpayers and their advisors preparing returns without complete information.

**Incomplete Basis Information.** Because cost basis reporting was not required for 2025 transactions, many Form 1099-DA forms show gross proceeds with blank or incomplete cost basis fields. Taxpayers who expect to see complete information may be surprised, and advisors must be prepared to explain that blank fields do not mean zero basis.

**Multiple Forms from Multiple Platforms.** A single taxpayer may receive multiple Form 1099-DA from different exchanges, and potentially multiple forms from the same exchange for different asset types or account segments. Consolidating this information into a comprehensive tax return requires careful reconciliation.

**Corrected Forms Expected.** EY's Tara Ferris has warned that corrected Form 1099-DA are "likely" as brokers refine their reporting processes in the early years of implementation. Taxpayers and advisors should expect to receive amended forms and should not treat initial filings as final without verification.

### 5.2 What Form 1099-DA Does Not Capture

It is equally important to understand what Form 1099-DA does **not** capture:

- **DeFi transactions:** Decentralized exchange activity, liquidity pool participation, and non-custodial protocol interactions are not reported on Form 1099-DA
- **Non-custodial wallet activity:** Transactions conducted from self-custody wallets are not captured
- **Wrapping and unwrapping transactions:** Temporarily excepted from reporting under Notice 2024-57
- **Staking through non-custodial protocols:** Not captured, though staking rewards remain taxable
- **Lending of digital assets:** Temporarily excepted from reporting under Notice 2024-57

Taxpayers engaging in any of these activities remain fully responsible for tracking and reporting the associated income and gain. The absence of a Form 1099-DA does not imply the absence of a tax obligation.

### 5.3 The Digital Asset Question on Form 1040

The IRS continues to require all taxpayers to answer the digital asset question on Form 1040 (and Forms 1041 and 1065). With the IRS now receiving Form 1099-DA data that can be matched against returns, answering "No" when reportable activity occurred carries significantly elevated audit risk.

For executors of estates and trustees administering trusts that hold digital assets, the digital asset question on Form 1041 must be addressed with particular care. The IRS has made clear that the question applies to all return types, not just individual returns.

## Section 6: Legislative Developments Affecting Digital Asset Taxation

### 6.1 The CLARITY Act (H.R. 3633)

The Digital Asset Market Clarity (CLARITY) Act passed the House of Representatives in July 2025 with strong bipartisan support and cleared the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs on May 14, 2026, by a vote of 15-9. As of July 2026, the bill remains on the Senate calendar awaiting floor consideration.

The CLARITY Act would allocate regulatory jurisdiction over digital assets between the SEC and the CFTC, granting the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction over spot markets in "digital commodities." While primarily a market structure bill rather than tax legislation, its classification of digital assets as commodities could influence their treatment under the Internal Revenue Code.

A field hearing held at Federal Hall National Memorial in New York City on July 17, 2026 — the one-year anniversary of the House's bipartisan passage — examined the CLARITY Act's role in promoting innovation, protecting consumers, and strengthening America's leadership in digital assets. Subcommittee Chairman Bryan Steil stated that the goal is to "replace regulation by enforcement with clear rules of the road for digital assets."

### 6.2 The PARITY Act (H.R. 8899)

The Digital Asset Protection, Accountability, Regulation, Innovation, Taxation, and Yields (PARITY) Act represents the most significant proposed tax-specific legislation for digital assets. The bill would:

- Create a new trading safe harbor for digital assets
- Allow dealers and active traders to make a mark-to-market election under IRC Section 475
- Extend securities-lending treatment under IRC Section 1058 to digital assets (making lending a digital asset a non-taxable event)
- Address wash sale rules for digital assets

The House Ways and Means Committee is preparing its own digital asset tax draft for markup, though the timing remains unclear. EY's Sean Shea has indicated that the PARITY Act has bipartisan support and can advance independently of the CLARITY Act.

### 6.3 The GENIUS Act

Signed into law in July 2025, the GENIUS Act established the first comprehensive federal regulatory framework for payment stablecoins. The law requires payment stablecoins to be backed 1-to-1 by U.S. dollars or other high-quality liquid assets, with monthly independent attestations. It definitively classifies payment stablecoins as neither securities nor commodities.

For tax purposes, the GENIUS Act does not change how stablecoin transactions are taxed — stablecoins remain property for federal tax purposes — but the expanded regulatory clarity has increased stablecoin adoption, meaning more taxpayers and more potentially taxable transactions.

### 6.4 International Developments: CARF and DAC8

The OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF) and the European Union's DAC8 directive entered operational implementation on January 1, 2026, even though formal information exchanges between tax authorities are not expected until 2027. Under CARF, crypto-asset service providers must collect user transaction data beginning in 2026, with the first cross-border exchanges expected in 2027.

The U.S. has not formally adopted CARF as domestic law, but the White House and IRS have indicated that proposed CARF regulations are under development. Taxpayers and advisors with international digital asset exposure should remain attentive to these developments, as the 2027 compliance landscape will include multilateral information sharing that does not exist today.

## Section 7: Audit Risk in the New Reporting Environment

### 7.1 Enhanced IRS Data Matching

The introduction of Form 1099-DA creates a data matching environment that did not previously exist for digital assets. The IRS now receives third-party reports on digital asset transactions and can cross-reference those reports against taxpayer returns. Underreporting that might have gone undetected in prior years is now significantly more likely to trigger an IRS inquiry.

The IRS has also announced increased enforcement funding and has indicated that digital asset compliance is a priority area. Taxpayers who fail to report digital asset transactions, who incorrectly classify transactions, or who use valuation methods that cannot withstand scrutiny face elevated audit risk.

### 7.2 Penalty Exposure

The potential penalties for noncompliance are substantial:

| Violation | Penalty | Code Section |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| Failure to report income | Tax due + interest + 20% accuracy-related penalty | IRC §6662 |
| Valuation misstatement (gross) | 40% penalty on underpayment | IRC §6662(h) |
| Failure to file Form 709 | 5% per month, up to 25% of tax due | IRC §6651 |
| Failure to file Form 706 | 5% per month, up to 25% of tax due | IRC §6651 |
| Failure to file informational return | $310 per return (2026) | IRC §6721 |
| SDIRA disqualification | Immediate taxation of entire account | IRC §408 |

For high-net-worth individuals with substantial digital asset portfolios, the combination of accuracy-related penalties and interest on a significant underpayment can result in liability far exceeding the cost of proper valuation and compliance in the first instance.

### 7.3 The Role of the Qualified Appraisal in Audit Defense

A qualified appraisal prepared by a qualified appraiser in compliance with USPAP standards serves as the most robust form of audit defense available. In the event of an IRS challenge:

- The appraiser's methodology and credentials establish a presumption of correctness
- The USPAP compliance framework provides a documented, standards-based approach that the IRS recognizes
- The appraiser's availability to provide expert testimony if the matter proceeds to Tax Court
- The contemporaneous nature of a qualified appraisal (prepared at the time of the reportable event) carries significantly more weight than a valuation prepared during an audit

Taxpayers who rely on exchange screenshots, self-calculated values, or broker reports for transactions requiring a qualified appraisal are effectively unarmed in an audit. The position that "the exchange said it was worth this much" has been specifically rejected by the IRS in Chief Counsel Advice.

## Section 8: Practical Recommendations for Taxpayers and Advisors

### 8.1 For High-Net-Worth Individuals

1. **Document wallet holdings as of key dates.** Maintain a comprehensive inventory of all digital asset wallets, the assets held in each, and the acquisition history for each asset by wallet. The January 1, 2025, transition date under Revenue Procedure 2024-28 is a critical baseline.

2. **Implement wallet-by-wallet tracking.** Do not rely on aggregated portfolio trackers that do not maintain wallet-level attribution. Ensure your recordkeeping system supports physical tracing of each asset to its source wallet.

3. **Schedule qualified appraisals for reportable events.** For charitable donations, estate planning, gift transfers, and SDIRA annual valuations, engage a USPAP-compliant qualified appraiser before the event occurs — not after.

4. **Reconcile all Form 1099-DA against personal records.** Do not assume broker-reported information is complete or accurate. Maintain independent records of cost basis and acquisition history.

5. **Engage tax professionals with digital asset experience.** The complexity of the new reporting rules, combined with the legislative developments in play, requires specialized expertise that many general practitioners do not possess.

### 8.2 For Estate Attorneys

1. **Include digital asset valuations in estate planning engagements.** Clients with substantial digital asset holdings need a qualified appraisal for their estate plan, not just for the Form 706 filing at death.

2. **Advise trust clients on wallet-by-wallet tracking.** Trusts that hold digital assets across multiple wallets or accounts must maintain wallet-level records to comply with the new cost basis rules.

3. **Coordinate with CPAs and appraisal firms.** The intersection of cost basis reporting, estate planning, and qualified appraisal requirements demands cross-disciplinary coordination.

4. **Update form language for digital asset provisions.** Trust agreements, powers of attorney, and estate planning documents should explicitly authorize wallet-by-wallet accounting and qualified appraisal engagement.

### 8.3 For CPAs and Tax Advisors

1. **Prepare clients for Form 1099-DA reconciliation.** Inform clients that they may receive multiple forms, that cost basis fields may be blank, and that corrected forms are likely.

2. **Do not assume broker-reported cost basis is correct.** Verify against client records and wallet tracking documentation.

3. **Engage qualified appraisers for client transactions.** For charitable donations, estates, and gifts, the qualified appraisal is not optional — it is a statutory requirement for deductions over $5,000.

4. **Stay current on legislative developments.** The CLARITY Act, PARITY Act, and House Ways and Means digital asset tax draft could significantly affect client planning strategies.

5. **Document the digital asset question response.** For clients who answer "No" to the digital asset question on Form 1040, maintain documentation supporting that response.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1: What is the new IRS cost basis reporting requirement for digital assets in 2026?**

The IRS now requires brokers to report both gross proceeds and adjusted cost basis for "covered" digital assets — those acquired and held within the same broker account on or after January 1, 2026. This reporting is made on Form 1099-DA and brings digital asset reporting closer to the standards applied to traditional securities.

**Q2: What does wallet-by-wallet tracking mean for my crypto taxes?**

The IRS has eliminated "universal wallet" accounting. You must track cost basis, holding periods, and gain or loss separately for each wallet or account. When selling digital assets, you must identify which specific wallet the assets came from and which acquisition lot within that wallet you are disposing of.

**Q3: Do I still need a qualified appraisal if my broker reports my cost basis?**

Yes. Broker-reported cost basis covers routine sales of covered assets but does not eliminate the need for qualified appraisals. You still need a qualified appraisal for: charitable donations exceeding $5,000 (IRC §170), estate tax filings (Form 706), gift tax valuation (Form 709), SDIRA annual valuation attestations, and any noncovered assets where the broker does not report basis.

**Q4: What is the difference between covered and noncovered digital assets?**

Covered digital assets are those acquired and held within the same broker account on or after January 1, 2026. Noncovered assets were acquired before January 1, 2026, or transferred in from another wallet or exchange. Brokers must report cost basis for covered assets but may (but are not required to) report it for noncovered assets.

**Q5: What happens if my Form 1099-DA shows incorrect or missing cost basis?**

You remain responsible for tracking and reporting accurate cost basis regardless of what your broker reports. Keep detailed records of acquisition dates, amounts, and wallet sources. Expect that corrected forms may be issued as brokers refine their reporting systems.

**Q6: What is Revenue Procedure 2024-28?**

Revenue Procedure 2024-28 is the IRS's transitional guidance that allows taxpayers to allocate unused basis to remaining digital asset units held in each wallet or account as of January 1, 2025. It provides a workable transition to the new wallet-by-wallet cost basis methodology.

**Q7: How does the CLARITY Act affect digital asset taxation?**

The CLARITY Act (H.R. 3633) primarily addresses regulatory jurisdiction — not tax rules specifically. However, its classification of digital assets as commodities under CFTC jurisdiction could influence future tax treatment. The PARITY Act (H.R. 8899) would more directly affect digital asset tax rules, including mark-to-market elections, wash sale treatment, and securities-lending rules.

**Q8: What is backup withholding for digital assets?**

If your broker lacks a valid Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN) for you, the IRS requires 24% backup withholding on digital asset sale proceeds. Brokers may need to liquidate a portion of your assets to satisfy this requirement. Ensure your broker has accurate W-9 information on file.

**Q9: How should I track my crypto cost basis across multiple wallets?**

Maintain a wallet-by-wallet tax lot ledger documenting every acquisition, transfer, and disposition. Record the wallet address, acquisition date, amount, cost basis, and transaction hash for each lot. When selling, record which wallet and which lot within that wallet are being disposed of.

**Q10: What are the penalties for incorrectly reporting digital asset cost basis?**

Penalties include the 20% accuracy-related penalty under IRC §6662 for underpayment of tax, which increases to 40% for gross valuation misstatements. Additional penalties apply for failure to file information returns. The IRS's enhanced data matching capabilities through Form 1099-DA make incorrect reporting more likely to be detected than in prior years.
