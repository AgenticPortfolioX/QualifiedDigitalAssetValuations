# Form 1099-DA and the 2026 Cost-Basis Phase-In: What Brokers Now Report, the Notice 2026-20 Identification Relief, the Self-Custody Gap, and Where a Qualified Appraisal Becomes the Taxpayer's Own Record

*Published: October 2, 2026 | By Qualified Digital Asset Valuations (QDAV)*

---

## Introduction: The Year the Form Grew a Basis Column

For as long as Form 1099-DA has existed, it has been a gross-proceeds form. It told the Internal Revenue Service what a client received when a digital asset was sold. It did not tell the Service what the client paid. In 2026 that changes — but not in the way most coverage suggests.

Beginning with sales effected after 2025, a broker must report basis information on Form 1099-DA. The obligation is real, and it is narrower than the headlines imply: basis is mandatory **only for a covered security**, which the 2026 instructions define as a digital asset acquired after 2025 for cash, stored-value cards, different digital assets, or property or services, in an account for which the broker provided custodial services — and only if the asset was acquired in such an account and held there until the broker effects the disposition. For a noncovered security, the broker is not required to report basis at all. It may do so voluntarily, and the form provides a box for that, but it is not obligated.

The practical result is easy to state and easy to underestimate. Most positions a high-net-worth client holds in 2026 were either acquired before 2026, transferred in from a self-custody wallet or another platform, or held somewhere a broker never took custody. Each of those is a noncovered security. For each, the taxpayer supplies the basis. And where a transaction falls outside the regulations altogether, there is no form, no payee statement, and the taxpayer's own records are the entire compliance record.

Two developments make this the right moment to examine the regime as a whole rather than instrument by instrument. The first is that the Service extended, for an additional year, the temporary relief that lets a taxpayer identify which units of a digital asset were sold on the taxpayer's own books and records rather than through the broker. That relief was first granted for 2025 by Notice 2025-7. It now runs through 2026 under Notice 2026-20, published in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-15. The second is what the extension notice says in passing — a sentence every preparer should read twice: for 2026 transactions, the acquisition date and basis a broker reports may not match the lot identification and basis on the taxpayer's books and records.

That mismatch is the subject of this article. It is a reporting problem, and it is also, in the right fact pattern, a valuation problem — because the same regime that now reports proceeds and sometimes basis still leaves the fair market value of a non-cash asset to be established by the taxpayer, with a USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal as the defensible standard.

## Section 1: What the 2026 Reporting Regime Actually Requires

### 1.1 The Statutory and Regulatory Frame

The broker-reporting requirement for digital assets is not an administrative invention. It results from amendments to Internal Revenue Code §6045 made by the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act in 2021, which expanded the categories of assets for which brokers must report and added basis reporting for digital assets. Treasury and the IRS implemented the change in final regulations, T.D. 10000, filed on June 28, 2024 and published at 89 FR 56480 on July 9, 2024. Those regulations require brokers to file information returns and furnish payee statements reporting gross proceeds — and, in certain circumstances, adjusted basis — on sales of digital assets effected for customers.

The regime was phased. Gross-proceeds reporting began with sales effected on or after January 1, 2025. Basis reporting begins with sales effected after 2025. The 2026 instructions are, in that sense, the operating manual for the first year in which the form carries a basis column for anything at all.

### 1.2 Proceeds, Basis, and the Line the Headlines Miss

The 2026 instructions state the rule in a single sentence: mandatory reporting of gross proceeds for **all** digital assets, mandatory reporting of basis for digital assets that are **covered securities**, and voluntary reporting of basis for digital assets that are **noncovered securities**. The word doing the work is "covered."

| Reporting year (sale effected) | Gross proceeds | Basis | Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 | Required (all digital assets) | Not required | T.D. 10000; Notice 2024-56 transition relief |
| 2026 and beyond | Required (all digital assets) | Required for covered securities; voluntary for noncovered | Form 1099-DA instructions (2026); §1.6045-1 |

A **covered security** is a digital asset acquired after 2025, for cash, stored-value cards, different digital assets, or property or services, in an account for which the broker provided custodial services — and it is covered only if it was acquired in that account and held there until the broker effects the disposition. Anything else is a **noncovered security**. The instructions list the noncovered categories with unusual clarity, and each one describes a position a real client holds:

| Noncovered category (per the 2026 instructions) | Typical fact pattern |
|---|---|
| Not acquired in a broker-custodial account | Coins bought on an exchange and withdrawn to self-custody |
| Acquired before 2026 | Nearly every legacy holding in a long-term portfolio |
| Transferred in to the broker | Assets moved from another platform or a hardware wallet |
| Acquired by an exempt recipient | Certain entity and intermediary holdings |
| Certain foreign-intermediary cases | §1.6045-1(g)(4)(vi)(E) holdings |

The consequence is that the first year of "basis reporting" reports basis on a small slice of a typical portfolio — the slice acquired in the custodian's account after 2025 and never moved. For everything else, the taxpayer is the source of truth.

### 1.3 Who Is a Broker — and Who Is Not

The regime reaches persons who, in the ordinary course of a trade or business, stand ready to effect sales of digital assets for others. In practice that means custodial trading platforms, certain hosted wallet providers, digital asset kiosks, and certain processors of digital asset payments. For 2026 it also reaches real estate reporting persons who have actual knowledge, or ordinarily would know, that digital assets were used by a buyer to make payment — for those closings, the reporting person reports the fair market value of the digital assets paid by the buyer and received by the seller.

The definition's edges matter as much as its center. The instructions exclude a person solely engaged in proof-of-work or proof-of-stake validation, and a person who only provides hardware or software permitting users to control private keys. And the final regulations themselves **do not include reporting requirements for decentralized or non-custodial brokers that do not take possession of the digital assets being sold or exchanged.** That exclusion is not a footnote. It is the boundary that pushes an entire category of activity — DeFi swaps, self-custody transfers, peer-to-peer settlement — outside the payee-statement system entirely, which is where the taxpayer's own records become the only record.

### 1.4 What the 2026 Form Shows

A preparer meeting the 2026 form for the first time should read the boxes as a map of the regime's limits. Box 1g is cost or other basis. Box 2 is a checkbox for whether basis was reported to the IRS — and it is unchecked on noncovered sales the broker chooses not to report voluntarily. Box 8 indicates that the broker relied on customer-provided acquisition information. Box 9 is the noncovered-security checkbox, and the instructions are explicit that reporting a noncovered sale without checking it subjects the broker to penalties it would otherwise avoid. Boxes 12a and 12b record the number of units transferred in and the transfer-in date — the fingerprints of a position whose basis the broker may not know.

Read together, the boxes describe a form that reports what a custodial platform can know and flags, in the open, what it cannot.

## Section 2: The Identification Relief, Extended by Notice 2026-20

### 2.1 "Adequate Identification" Under the Wallet-by-Wallet Rules

To understand the relief, start with the rule it relieves. The final regulations adopted wallet-by-wallet basis tracking and an ordering rule in Treas. Reg. §1.1012-1(j), which determines which units of the same digital asset are treated as sold when a taxpayer holds multiple units in the same wallet. For units held in the custody of a broker, §1.1012-1(j)(3)(ii) generally permits an **adequate identification**: the taxpayer specifies to the custodial broker, no later than the date and time of the sale, the particular units to be sold, by reference to an identifier the broker designates as sufficiently specific. A standing order or instruction communicated to the broker can serve the same purpose. In other words, the default rule requires the taxpayer to speak to the broker before the trade.

That is a workable rule when the broker's systems accept specific-identification instructions. It is a **defensible data** problem when they do not. Because not every platform built the plumbing to receive and honor a lot designation, the Service issued transition relief.

### 2.2 Notice 2025-7 and the 2025 Relief Period

**Notice 2025-7**, issued December 31, 2024, allowed eligible taxpayers, during calendar year 2025, to make an adequate identification of units of a digital asset held in the custody of a broker by identifying the particular units — or recording a standing order — **on the taxpayer's own books and records**, rather than communicating the identification to the broker. For the relief period, the taxpayer's records stood in for the broker conversation.

The notice was careful about its own limits. It did not apply to units not held in the custody of a broker. It did not apply for purposes of the §1.6045-1 information-reporting rules. And a taxpayer relying on the Rev. Proc. 2024-28 safe harbor — the transitional safe harbor that permits allocation of unattached basis to wallets and accounts as of January 1, 2025 — could use the relief only after the safe harbor's requirements were satisfied.

### 2.3 What Notice 2026-20 Changes — and What It Does Not

**Notice 2026-20**, published in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-15, extends the §4.02 relief of Notice 2025-7 **for an additional year**. The practical effect is that the same books-and-records identification method that was available for 2025 transactions is available for 2026 transactions. A taxpayer whose platform still cannot accept a lot designation is not, for another year, forced into a default ordering method the records do not support.

| Feature | Notice 2025-7 (2025) | Notice 2026-20 (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Relief period | Calendar year 2025 | Calendar year 2026 (one additional year) |
| Method permitted | Identify units on the taxpayer's books and records, or record a standing order, without communicating to the broker | Same |
| Applies to assets outside broker custody? | No | No |
| Applies for §1.6045-1 reporting purposes? | No | No |
| Condition for Rev. Proc. 2024-28 users | Safe harbor requirements must be satisfied first | Same |

What the extension does **not** change is as important as what it does. It is a relief from a timing requirement, not a substantive rule about what the units' basis is. It does not convert a noncovered asset into a covered one. It does not obligate a broker to accept or honor a designation. And it does not reach assets the taxpayer holds outside a broker's custody — for those, the operative rule is the unhosted-wallet standard discussed below.

### 2.4 The Mismatch the Notice Admits

Here is the sentence that should reshape how preparers approach the 2026 filing season. The extension notice states that, exactly as under Notice 2025-7, the relief does not apply for purposes of the §1.6045-1 information-reporting rules — and then draws the conclusion: **for 2026 transactions, the acquisition date and basis reported by a broker to a taxpayer may not match the lot identification and basis of that sale, disposition, or transfer on the taxpayer's books and records.**

The logic is straightforward once separated from the noise. The taxpayer is allowed to pick lots on the taxpayer's own books. The broker is not required to follow that pick in what it reports to the Service. The two records can therefore describe the same economic disposition with different acquisition dates and different bases — and no one has made an error. The form is not wrong; the books are not wrong; they are simply answering different questions, because the relief severs the taxpayer's identification from the broker's reporting.

The consequence for practice is that **reconciliation, not the receipt of the form, is the compliance event for the 2026 tax year.** A payer who files the Form 1099-DA as received, without mapping it to the taxpayer's lot records, will report a basis the regulations do not require the taxpayer to accept. A preparer who reconciles the two — and documents why they differ — is doing the work the regime now demands.

### 2.5 Self-Custody: The Unhosted-Wallet Rule

For assets outside a broker's custody, the relief never applied, and the standard is the one the Service added to its frequently asked questions on December 15, 2025. Under that guidance, a taxpayer who sells less than all of the units of the same digital asset held in a single unhosted wallet may identify the basis and holding period of the units sold — but only by satisfying two conditions. First, no later than the date and time of the sale, the taxpayer must identify the particular units **on the taxpayer's books and records**, using any identifier sufficient to identify them, such as purchase date and time or purchase price. Second, the taxpayer must maintain adequate records to establish that the units sold were removed from the unhosted wallet. When both conditions are met, the identification is treated as a specific identification.

The self-custody rule is stricter than the broker relief in the way that matters: there is no custodian to notify, so the entire evidentiary burden sits on the taxpayer's records. An unhosted-wallet holder who cannot produce a contemporaneous record of which units left the wallet has, in practice, defaulted to an ordering convention the records were never built to support.

## Section 3: What the Regime Does Not Capture

The 1099-DA regime is often described as if it reports digital asset activity. It reports a subset of it. The boundaries are set by three instruments, and each boundary is a place where the taxpayer's own substantiation becomes the only substantiation.

### 3.1 The Notice 2024-57 Deferred Transactions

**Notice 2024-57** provides that brokers are not required under §6045 to file information returns or furnish payee statements for a defined set of transactions, and that the Service will not assert §6721 or §6722 penalties for failing to do so, until further guidance is issued. The identified transactions are:

| Deferred transaction (Notice 2024-57) | What it describes |
|---|---|
| Wrapping and unwrapping | Exchanging a native asset for its wrapped representation and back |
| Liquidity-provider transactions | Depositing assets into an automated market maker pool and redeeming a pool interest |
| Staking transactions | Committing assets to a proof-of-stake mechanism, or to a liquid-staking representation and back |
| Lending (type 1 and type 2) | Transfers characterized as lending, with a later return of the same asset type |
| Short sales | Transactions characterized by market participants as short sales |
| Notional principal contracts | Notional principal contract transactions |

Two cautions belong with the list. First, the notice states affirmatively that its descriptions are **not** a substantive analysis of any transaction or its component steps, and that no inference is intended as to how any identified transaction is treated for federal income tax purposes. A deferral from reporting is not a conclusion that nothing occurred. Second, the reporting exception does not extend to rewards or compensation earned by participants; staking rewards, for example, may be reportable under other provisions even where the underlying staking transaction is deferred.

### 3.2 The Non-Custodial Exclusion

The final regulations exclude decentralized, non-custodial brokers that do not take possession of the digital assets being sold or exchanged. The clearest way to see the consequence is by asset location:

| Where the client's activity occurs | Form 1099-DA to the client? | Who establishes the record |
|---|---|---|
| Custodial exchange sale, covered security | Yes — proceeds and basis | Broker reports; taxpayer confirms |
| Custodial exchange sale, noncovered security | Yes — proceeds only | Taxpayer supplies basis and records |
| Assets transferred in from another platform or wallet | Proceeds yes; basis typically not | Taxpayer (or an acquisition information statement) |
| Self-custody wallet disposition | Generally no | Taxpayer, under the §6001 recordkeeping standard |
| Non-custodial / DeFi swap | Generally no | Taxpayer |
| Covered deferred transaction (Notice 2024-57) | No (deferred) | Taxpayer |
| Non-U.S. platform | May issue no U.S. form | Taxpayer |

### 3.3 The International Gap

The regime is a U.S. broker-reporting regime. A U.S. digital asset broker is a U.S. person, or a U.S. branch treated as a U.S. person, that effects sales on behalf of others, and the reporting obligation runs to that broker. Sales effected on a platform that is not a U.S. digital asset broker may produce no U.S. payee statement at all, and coordination with the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework remains a work in progress. For a client with activity on a foreign venue, the absence of a Form 1099-DA is expected rather than exculpatory.

### 3.4 Noncovered, Transferred-In, and Aggregate Positions

Three further categories deserve a place in the file. Noncovered assets — everything acquired before 2026, everything acquired outside broker custody — are reported for proceeds but not basis. Transferred-in assets are flagged by the form itself, in boxes 12a and 12b, because the receiving broker may not know the acquisition date or basis; the customer may request an acquisition information statement from the transferring broker, and where such a statement is obtained it is worth as much as the taxpayer's discipline in retaining it. And for qualifying stablecoins and specified NFTs, a broker using an optional aggregate method may report a single aggregate figure with a transaction count, subject to de minimis thresholds — $10,000 of aggregate gross proceeds for designated stablecoin sales, $600 for specified NFT sales, and $600 for processor-of-digital-asset-payments sales, above which all of the customer's sales must be reported.

### 3.5 No Payee Statement Is Not No Tax

The single most important line in the guidance is not about reporting at all. The Service's guidance states that a taxpayer must report income, gain, or loss from all transactions involving digital assets on the federal return for the year of the transaction — **regardless of the amount, and regardless of whether the taxpayer receives a payee statement or information return.** The recordkeeping obligation under §6001 requires records sufficient to establish the positions taken, including records documenting receipts, sales, exchanges, dispositions or transfers and the fair market value of the digital assets.

The system, in other words, is not built so that the taxpayer can wait for a form. It is built so that the taxpayer can substantiate the position, and where the activity falls in a gap — self-custody, DeFi, a deferred transaction, a foreign venue — there is no form to wait for.

## Section 4: Where a Qualified Appraisal Becomes the Taxpayer's Own Record

Everything to this point concerns **basis** — what the client paid. The regime reports basis, or declines to, and the taxpayer reconciles. But basis is a cost concept, and much of what an attorney, a CPA, or a fiduciary needs is a **value**. The two obligations are frequently confused, and the confusion is expensive, because the provision that makes a valuation defensible is not the provision that governs the form.

### 4.1 Basis Substantiation Is Not Fair Market Value Determination

Basis asks what a taxpayer paid for an asset. Fair market value asks what the asset is worth on a given date — the amount a hypothetical willing buyer would pay a willing seller, neither under compulsion, both reasonably informed. The Internal Revenue Code requires fair market value determinations in a specific set of contexts, and for non-cash property above a threshold, it requires something more than a good-faith estimate.

That "something more" is the **qualified appraisal**. Under the IRS Section 170 charitable-contribution rules, IRC §170(f)(11)(C) requires a qualified appraisal for a contribution of property valued at more than $5,000, and Treas. Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(2) defines the "generally accepted appraisal standards" a qualified appraisal must follow as **the substance and principles of the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice.** The same regulation prescribes the contents of a qualified appraisal and the appraiser's declaration. A digital asset appraisal is an intangible asset appraisal, which places it under USPAP Standards 9 and 10 — the development and reporting standards for business and intangible asset appraisals.

### 4.2 The Filing Contexts That Require an Appraisal

The contexts in which the fair market value obligation — and therefore the qualified appraisal — attaches, do not depend on whether a Form 1099-DA was issued. They depend on the return the asset is entering.

| Filing context | Qualified appraisal or defensible appraisal required? | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Charitable contribution of digital assets over $5,000 | Yes — qualified appraisal; Form 8283 Section B | IRC §170(f)(11)(C); Treas. Reg. §1.170A-17; Form 8283 |
| Estate tax valuation (Form 706) | Yes — date-of-death or alternate-date fair market value | IRC §2031; §2032; Treas. Reg. §1.170A-17 standards |
| Gift tax valuation (Form 709) | Yes — value on the date of the gift, for adequate disclosure | IRC §2512; §6501(c)(9) |
| Self-directed IRA annual value attestation | Defensible fair market value is the standard the custodian and the Service expect | Custodian requirements; IRC §408 |
| Audit or examination of a reported value | Yes — the defensible record | IRC §6662; §6664 |

### 4.3 Why Exchange Data Does Not Substitute

The instinct that a broker statement settles value is a natural error of the reporting regime this article describes. It is worth stating plainly what the Service has said: in Chief Counsel Advice 202302012, the Service concluded that cryptocurrency is not a "publicly traded security" for purposes of §170(f)(11)(C), so the Section A exception to the qualified-appraisal requirement — the exception that lets a donor rely on a published market quotation — is unavailable, and exchange-reported values do not substitute for a qualified appraisal. That advice is not precedential, as the Service notes under §6110(k)(3), but it states the position the Service takes.

The technical point is that an exchange price is a price for a **unit** at a **moment**, on **one venue**, for a quantity that may bear no resemblance to the block the client is contributing, gifting, or reporting. It is defensible data about a market. It is not a qualified appraisal of an asset, and it does not establish fair market value for a non-cash contribution.

### 4.4 Why the 2026 Mismatch Environment Raises the Value of a Contemporaneous Appraisal

There is a practical reason the reporting changes and the valuation obligation should be read together. As the regime widens what the Service can see — proceeds for every sale, basis for covered securities, fair market value for real-estate digital-asset payments, transfer-in flags on the form — it also widens the number of returns that can be matched against data the Service already holds. A taxpayer whose fair market value determination consists of a screenshot from an exchange has an assertion; a taxpayer whose determination is a contemporaneous, USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal has a record.

The distinction is the difference between a position that is merely asserted and one that is defensible. In a regime that reports more, flatter numbers are not the goal. Records that will hold under examination are.

## Section 5: A Practical Compliance Architecture for Attorneys and CPAs

### 5.1 Records to Assemble for the 2026 Tax Year

For each client with reportable digital asset activity, the file should be built to answer three questions: what did the client pay, what was the asset worth when it mattered, and what does the record prove? The records that answer them are not exotic, but they must be assembled before the return is prepared rather than after a notice arrives.

- **Every Form 1099-DA received**, mapped to the client's own lot records — not filed as received.
- **Acquisition information statements** requested from transferring brokers for transferred-in positions.
- **The client's books and records for self-custody activity**, sufficient to identify units sold and to establish that identified units left the wallet.
- **Wallet-by-wallet basis schedules**, reconciled to the Rev. Proc. 2024-28 safe harbor allocation where the client relied on it.
- **Contemporaneous market evidence** for every date on which value matters — a contribution date, a date of death, a gift date, an attestation date.
- **A qualified appraisal** wherever a filing context from the table above requires one.

### 5.2 The Reconciliation Workflow

The mismatch that Notice 2026-20 admits should be handled explicitly, not papered over. The workflow is short: take the proceeds the broker reports; take the client's identified lots; determine whether the basis differs and why; report the client's basis on Form 8949, with an attached explanation where the form's figure differs; and retain the working papers that show the reasoning. A reconciliation done in the workfile is a defensible position. A reconciliation done in the preparer's head is not.

### 5.3 When to Engage a Qualified Appraiser

Two questions should move an engagement out of the spreadsheet and into an appraisal. The first is whether a filing context requires a qualified appraisal — a charitable contribution over $5,000, an estate, a gift, an SDIRA attestation. The second is whether the asset's value cannot be established from a unit price on a single venue — an illiquid NFT, a restricted or subject-to-lockup position, an entity-held interest, a large block that could not be liquidated at the quoted price. Where either is true, the broker statement is not the answer, and a USPAP-compliant appraisal is the record.

### 5.4 A Short Checklist

| Question | If yes |
|---|---|
| Did the client sell a covered security in 2026? | Confirm the broker's reported basis against the client's lots |
| Did the client sell a noncovered security? | Supply basis from the client's records; expect no basis on the form |
| Did the client transact in self-custody or on a non-custodial venue? | Build the §6001 record; there is no payee statement |
| Did the client rely on Notice 2026-20 to identify lots? | Document the identification on the books and records; expect a mismatch |
| Did a filing context require fair market value? | Engage a qualified appraiser before the return is filed |

## Section 6: Frequently Asked Questions

**Does Form 1099-DA report my cost basis for the 2026 tax year?**
Only for covered securities. Beginning with sales effected after 2025, brokers must report basis for digital assets that are covered securities — those acquired after 2025 in the broker's custodial account and held there until disposition. For noncovered securities, basis reporting is voluntary, and the form includes a box to flag a noncovered sale.

**What makes a digital asset a "covered security" for Form 1099-DA?**
A digital asset is a covered security only if it was acquired after 2025, for value, in an account for which the broker provided custodial services, and was held in that account until the broker effects the disposition. Anything acquired before 2026, acquired outside broker custody, or transferred in to the broker is a noncovered security.

**Why might the basis on my Form 1099-DA differ from my own records?**
Because the identification relief severs the taxpayer's lot selection from the broker's reporting. Notice 2026-20 states that for 2026 transactions the acquisition date and basis a broker reports to a taxpayer may not match the lot identification and basis on the taxpayer's books and records. The remedy is reconciliation, documented in the workfile.

**What is Notice 2026-20?**
Notice 2026-20, published in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-15, extends for an additional year the temporary relief first granted by Notice 2025-7. It allows eligible taxpayers to make an adequate identification of digital asset units held in a broker's custody by identifying the units on their own books and records or by recording a standing order, without communicating the identification to the broker.

**Do I owe tax on a digital asset transaction if I receive no Form 1099-DA?**
Yes. A taxpayer must report income, gain, or loss from all digital asset transactions on the return for the year of the transaction, regardless of the amount and regardless of whether a payee statement or information return is received. No form is not no tax.

**Are self-custody and decentralized exchange transactions reported on Form 1099-DA?**
Generally not. The final regulations do not include reporting requirements for decentralized or non-custodial brokers that do not take possession of the digital assets, and the identification relief does not apply to units outside a broker's custody. For those dispositions, the taxpayer's own §6001 records are the record.

**When do I need a qualified appraisal instead of a broker statement?**
When the law requires fair market value rather than basis. The clearest trigger is a charitable contribution of digital assets over $5,000, which requires a qualified appraisal under IRC §170(f)(11)(C). Estate valuations on Form 706, gift valuations on Form 709, and SDIRA annual value attestations likewise turn on defensible fair market value, which an exchange price alone does not establish.

**Does an exchange price establish fair market value for a charitable deduction?**
No. The Service's Chief Counsel Advice 202302012 concludes that cryptocurrency is not a publicly traded security for purposes of §170(f)(11)(C), so the exception that allows reliance on a published market quotation is unavailable, and exchange-reported values do not substitute for a qualified appraisal. The qualified appraisal, developed under the substance and principles of USPAP, is the defensible record.

## Conclusion: The Form Reports What the Records Must Prove

Form 1099-DA has grown a basis column in 2026, and the column is narrower than the conversation around it. It reports basis for covered securities only. It leaves noncovered securities — most of what a client holds — to the taxpayer's records, and it leaves an entire category of activity, from self-custody to decentralized venues to the transactions Notice 2024-57 defers, outside the payee-statement system altogether. Notice 2026-20 extends the identification relief another year and, in doing so, tells practitioners exactly what to expect: a broker's reported basis that may not match the client's books.

None of this changes what a digital asset is worth. That determination still rests where it always has — on a defensible fair market value, established by a qualified appraisal under the substance and principles of USPAP wherever a filing context calls for one. The reporting regime tells the Service what a platform knows. It does not tell the Service what an asset is worth. In a year when more of the former is visible than ever, the value of the latter has never been clearer.
