# The Appraisal Intake File: What Attorneys and CPAs Must Assemble Before a Digital Asset Appraisal Can Be Signed, the Three Fixed Valuation Dates That Decide Which Records Matter, and the Ten Gaps That Sink an Otherwise Sound Valuation
*Published: November 3, 2026 | By QDAV*
---
## The intake file is a regulatory instrument, not a formality

Most engagements that go wrong on a digital asset valuation do not go wrong in the methodology. They go wrong in the first two weeks, before the appraiser has formed an opinion, when the file arrives incomplete and everyone proceeds anyway.

The reason this matters more in digital assets than in real estate or fine art is structural. A house has a deed, a tax roll entry, and a recorded sale history. A token has an address, a transaction log, and whatever the client can still retrieve from an exchange that may have changed hands twice since the purchase. When the underlying record is thin, the appraisal becomes an exercise in assumption, and an assumption is precisely what a revenue agent is trained to test.

Treasury Regulation Section 1.170A-17(a)(6) states the consequence in terms. An appraisal is not a qualified appraisal for a particular contribution, even if every other requirement is met, if the donor either failed to disclose facts or misrepresented them, and a reasonable person would expect that failure or misrepresentation to cause the appraiser to misstate the value of the contributed property. The client's disclosure is not background information. It is a statutory element of the appraisal's validity, and the intake file is the physical form that element takes.

For attorneys and CPAs, that reframing changes the work. The intake file is not a client-service convenience handed to an appraiser as a courtesy. It is the evidentiary base of a document that may be examined years later, by a person who was not in the room and who has no obligation to give the taxpayer the benefit of the doubt. Assembling it against the right valuation date, in the right order, with the right signatures, is the difference between a qualified appraisal and an expensive opinion.

## Three fixed valuation dates, three different files

A digital asset has no single value. It has a value on a date, and the date is set by the transaction, not by the taxpayer's convenience. Three dates recur across the work this firm sees, and each one pulls a different slice of the record set.

| Scenario | Valuation date | Who fixes the date | Date-sensitive records the file must hold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charitable contribution, Form 8283 Section B | Date of contribution | The transfer, per Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(5)(ii) | Exchange or wallet records bracketing the transfer; market data on the contribution date; signed appraisal dated within 60 days before that date |
| Estate, Form 706 | Date of death, or the alternate valuation date if §2032 is elected | The statute, and the executor's election | Holdings as of both dates; pricing on both dates; custody statements for both dates; a written §2032 election analysis |
| Self-directed IRA, annual attestation | December 31 of the calendar year | The custodian's Form 5498 reporting obligation | Year-end custody statement; year-end pricing; a schedule of any income-producing activity during the year |
| Gift, Form 709 | Date of the transfer | The transfer, per Reg. §301.6501(c)-1(f) | Transfer records; market data on the gift date; appraisal supporting adequate disclosure |

The distinction that causes the most trouble is the one inside the charitable column. The appraisal report date and the valuation effective date are two different fields, and the regulation treats them differently. Under Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(4), the report must be signed and dated by the qualified appraiser no earlier than 60 days before the date of contribution, and no later than the due date of the return including extensions — or, where the deduction is first claimed on an amended return, no later than the date that amended return is filed. Under Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(5)(ii), if the report is dated before the contribution, the valuation effective date must fall no earlier than 60 days before the contribution and no later than the contribution itself; if the report is dated on or after the contribution, the valuation effective date must be the date of contribution, full stop.

Read together, those two provisions mean a client cannot order an appraisal in November for a gift made in March and receive a compliant document. The window is closed. What can be produced is a retrospective opinion, which has its uses in litigation and almost none in substantiation. Practitioners who understand the sequencing order the appraisal before the transfer, not after the return is on extension.

One further field deserves attention because it is the only one the regulation defines by reference rather than by form. Fair Market Value, for this purpose, is the price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or sell and both having reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts. For a token that traded across dozens of venues on the valuation date, that standard requires the appraiser to identify the principal market and say why it was selected. For an NFT that did not trade that week, it requires a documented inference from comparable transactions. Both are questions about the record set, which is the reason the intake file is assembled before the valuation approach is selected rather than after the opinion is formed.

## The universal intake set

Across all four scenarios, the same six categories of record are required. What changes is the date the records must speak to.

| Category | What the record proves | Who holds it | The substitute that fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identity and authority | That the person directing the engagement can bind the owner — trustee, executor, POA, entity officer | Client, counsel, court | A verbal confirmation from the client's assistant |
| Ownership and custody | That the asset was held, where, and under what arrangement | Custodian, exchange, client | A portfolio screenshot with no account identifier |
| Acquisition and basis | What was paid, in what lots, on what dates | Exchange, client, prior preparer | A single blended cost figure with no lot detail |
| Character and holding period | Which units are long-term, which are ordinary income | Client, exchange, 1099-DA | An assumption that everything held over a year is long-term |
| Market data at the valuation date | What the principal market showed on that date | Pricing services, exchanges, on-chain data | A screenshot of today's price |
| Restrictions and agreements | Whether the asset could be sold, and on what terms | Client, counsel, issuer | Silence, treated as an absence of restriction |

Two of these deserve elaboration because they are the ones clients most often believe they have already satisfied.

Acquisition and basis is where the file is thinnest. An exchange export typically covers the account's current history and not its full life, and it often reports a blended cost basis that is unusable for a lot-level analysis. Where units arrived by airdrop, staking reward, fork, or mining, the acquisition record is an income event rather than a purchase, and the basis question becomes a question about the income recognized at receipt. Where units moved between self-custody and an exchange, the transfer records establish continuity but not cost. The appraiser can work with a reconstructed basis only where the reconstruction is documented, dated, and internally consistent; an undocumented reconstruction is an assumption wearing a spreadsheet.

Restrictions and agreements is where the file is thinnest in a different way: not missing, but undisclosed. A vesting schedule, a lockup, a SAFT with a transfer prohibition, a side letter granting a redemption right, or a staking arrangement that encumbers the position all affect what a hypothetical willing buyer would pay. Under Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(3)(ii), a qualified appraisal must describe the terms of any agreement or understanding by or on behalf of the donor and donee that relates to the use, sale, or other disposition of the contributed property. That is a disclosure obligation running to the donor, and it is discharged by the intake file, not by the appraiser's diligence.

## What on-chain evidence proves and what it does not

Blockchain data is the most misunderstood category of evidence in this practice area, in both directions. Clients overestimate it; some appraisers underuse it.

A block explorer can prove, with high confidence, that a specific quantity of a specific token moved from one address to another at a specific block height, and therefore at a specific time. It can prove that an address received an airdrop, that a wallet interacted with a staking contract, and that a given NFT token identifier exists under a given contract with given metadata. For a valuation, that corroborates timing and quantity, which are the two facts most often misstated. It also corroborates the existence of a market: trade history for an NFT collection, liquidity events, and the depth of a token's trading pairs are all readable.

What it cannot prove is ownership in the legal sense. Control of a private key is evidence of control, not of title. The distinction matters in an estate, where the question is what the decedent owned and whether the executor can reach it; in a gift, where the question is whether a completed transfer occurred; and in an IRA, where the question is whether the asset is held by the custodian for the benefit of the account rather than by the account holder personally. The last of those is not a valuation question at all. It is a prohibited transaction question, and it is answered by a custody agreement, not by a transaction hash.

The practical rule this firm applies: on-chain data corroborates, the client's signed representation establishes, and the custodian's statement controls where a custodian exists. An intake file that contains only block explorer links is a file with a quantity and no owner.

## The charitable file under Section B

The December 2025 revision of the Form 8283 instructions settled a question practitioners had been answering by analogy. Section B, Part I, line 2 now includes digital assets as a named property category, defined as any digital representation of value recorded on a cryptographically secured, distributed ledger or similar technology, without regard to whether each individual transaction is recorded on that ledger, and expressly including convertible virtual currency, cryptocurrency, stablecoins, and non-fungible tokens.

The threshold structure is unchanged. Section A covers noncash contributions of more than $500 and not more than $5,000. Section B covers more than $5,000 per item or group of similar items, and Section B requires a written qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser. The instructions state the relationship between the two documents without ambiguity: Form 8283 is an appraisal summary, not an appraisal, and a separate qualified appraisal is required for any gift of property valued above $5,000.

Three operational consequences follow.

First, "group of similar items" is measured across donees for the purpose of deciding whether Section B applies, though a separate Section B is filed for each donee organization. A donor who splits a token position across three charities in amounts of $3,000 each is filing Section B, not three Section A forms, because the group total exceeds $5,000.

Second, the description standard in line 3, column (a) is substantive. The instructions require a description detailed enough that a person unfamiliar with the property could be sure that the property appraised is the property contributed, with the required detail scaling to value. For a fungible token, that means the network, the contract or asset identifier where one exists, the quantity to the precision the ledger supports, and the custody location. For an NFT, it means the contract address, the token identifier, and the collection. A description reading "various cryptocurrency" satisfies nothing.

Third, the signature blocks run in a fixed order. The donor completes at least column (a) of line 3, and column (b) where applicable, before submitting the form to the donee; the donee completes Part V, the acknowledgment, and returns the form; the appraiser then completes Part IV, the declaration, which must include the appraiser's taxpayer identification number. Where more than one appraiser contributes to a single appraisal, all of them sign both the appraisal and Part IV. Where the deduction exceeds $500,000 for an item or group of similar items, the qualified appraisal itself is attached to the return. Where the contribution is carried forward to a later year, the prior year's Form 8283 and the appraisal are attached again.

A fourth detail matters when a collection of NFTs or a basket of small positions is contributed in a single filing. Section B, Part III is the donor's statement for items included in Section B, Part I that carry an appraised value of $500 or less, and the donee is not required to file Form 8282 for items so identified. Each such item needs its own entry keyed to the description used in Part I, and the donor completes Part III before submitting the form to the donee for acknowledgment. Practitioners who omit Part III on a diversified contribution create disposition-reporting exposure for the charity and lose the donor's own record of the small items. Note also that line 3, column (b) asks for physical condition only where real property or tangible personal property is donated. For digital assets the column is left blank, and the entire burden of identification falls on column (a).

## The estate file: date of death, alternate valuation, and consistent basis

Form 706 has no statutory qualified appraisal requirement of the kind IRS Section 170 imposes on charitable contributions, and practitioners sometimes read that absence as a lower standard. It is not. The exposure is concentrated in two places instead.

The first is Section 6695A, which reaches appraisals prepared for returns under subtitle A or B, including estate and gift returns, and imposes a penalty on the appraiser computed as the greater of a percentage of the underpayment attributable to the misstatement or a fixed floor, capped in relation to the fee charged. The second is Section 6662, whose estate and gift valuation misstatement tiers are reached on percentage thresholds that are easier to cross than the income tax thresholds, with a second, higher tier for gross misstatements. An executor's valuation is therefore defended on the same evidentiary footing as a charitable deduction, whether or not the word "qualified" appears in the statute governing the return.

The estate-specific intake requirements are three.

Both dates. If the executor elects alternate valuation under Section 2032, the estate is valued six months after death, and the election is made on the return. That election cannot be evaluated after the fact: it requires date-of-death values and alternate-date values computed from the same holdings, the same custody evidence, and the same market data methodology. A workfile that holds only one date cannot support the election, and in a volatile asset class the election is often worth more than the entire appraisal fee.

Reachability. The executor's inventory must distinguish assets the estate can access from assets it merely knows about. A hardware wallet without a recovery phrase is a discovery problem, not a valuation problem, and it changes what the fiduciary can represent. Counsel should resolve access before the appraiser is engaged, because an appraisal of an asset the estate cannot produce is an appraisal of a claim rather than of property.

Consistent basis. Section 1014(f) requires the recipient's basis to be consistent with the value reported on the estate tax return, and Section 6035 enforces it through Form 8971 and Schedule A, filed by the executor within 30 days of the earlier of the return's due date or its filing. The number the appraiser signs becomes the number the heir must use years later, in a return prepared by a different accountant who has never seen the appraisal. That is the strongest argument for a documented workfile in the estate context, and it has nothing to do with the estate tax itself.

## The self-directed IRA file: the December 31 the client does not choose

The annual IRA valuation date is not negotiated. It is a consequence of the custodian's reporting obligation: the trustee or issuer reports the account's fair market value as of December 31 on Form 5498, box 5, due to the Internal Revenue Service by May 31 following the calendar year.

For a conventional account holding marketable securities, the custodian computes that figure from a pricing feed. For an account holding digital assets, the custodian generally relies on a value supplied from outside, which is why the attestation exists. Two intake consequences follow.

The first is that the file must be built to a fixed date, and it must be built after that date. A December 31 valuation assembled from a statement dated December 15 is a valuation of a different date. Where the asset traded continuously, the difference may be immaterial; where it did not, the difference can be most of the account.

The second, and the one that clients resist, is that the appraiser must be told the facts that make the account non-compliant if they exist. Whether a distribution was taken in kind, whether the account holder provided services to the asset issuer, whether a related party transacted with the account, whether staking or lending generated unrelated business taxable income — all of these bear on the account's status and on how the position should be characterized. A client who withholds them in the belief that a clean valuation is a better valuation is exposing the account to disqualification while obtaining an appraisal that misdescribes it. The appraisal is not a character reference. It is a factual report, and a report built on withheld facts is worth less than none.

## The gift file: adequate disclosure is the whole point

A gift tax return that is not adequately disclosed does not start the statute of limitations running on the gift. Section 6501(c)(4) provides the exception, and the regulations under Section 301.6501(c)-1(f) define what adequate disclosure requires. For property other than money and publicly traded securities, the disclosure depends on a qualified appraisal prepared by a qualified appraiser.

That is the entire strategic case for appraising a gift of digital assets, and it is usually explained backwards. Clients are told an appraisal protects them against a valuation challenge. More precisely, the appraisal is what converts an open-ended exposure into a closed one. Without it, the Internal Revenue Service can revisit the transfer indefinitely within the general assessment period rules; with it, the clock starts.

The intake file for a gift is therefore built to support disclosure rather than to support a deduction: complete description of the transferred property, the transfer date and the valuation effective date, the donor's acquisition history and basis, any restriction on the recipient's ability to dispose of the property, and the appraiser's qualifications and declaration. Where the gift is to a trust, the trust instrument and the funding schedule belong in the file, because the appraisal must identify the property the trust actually received.

## Ten intake failures and the fix for each

These are the failures that appear repeatedly across engagements, ordered roughly by how often they force a second appraisal.

1. **A blended cost basis with no lot detail.** The fix is a lot-level reconstruction tied to acquisition records, with income-received units separated and dated. Where the exchange cannot produce it, the client's own contemporaneous records and on-chain transfer history fill the gap, and the reconstruction is documented as a reconstruction.

2. **Screenshots offered as market evidence.** The fix is a pricing workfile built from exchange data on the valuation date, with the principal market identified and the source recorded. A screen capture has no audit trail and no methodology.

3. **An appraisal ordered after the transfer.** The fix is sequencing: for a charitable gift above $5,000, the appraisal is signed no earlier than 60 days before the contribution. A file that begins after the gift has already excluded itself from compliance.

4. **Restrictions not disclosed.** The fix is a written restriction questionnaire answered by the client and signed, covering vesting, lockups, transfer prohibitions, redemption rights, and encumbrances. Silence is not a representation of absence.

5. **Custody asserted without a custodian statement.** The fix is a statement or account letter from the custodian, or a signed control attestation where the asset is self-custodied. Control is established by evidence, not by assertion.

6. **Ownership conflated with control of a wallet.** The fix is a title analysis: who holds legal ownership, under what instrument, and whether the party directing the engagement has authority to bind that owner.

7. **A single valuation date for a §2032 election.** The fix is a dual-date workfile assembled before the election is made, not after.

8. **A description that does not identify the property.** The fix is the line 3, column (a) standard applied literally: network, identifier, quantity, custody location, and for NFTs the contract and token identifier.

9. **Signature blocks completed out of order.** The fix is a routing sheet: donor completes line 3 columns (a) and (b), donee completes Part V, appraiser completes Part IV with a taxpayer identification number, and multiple appraisers all sign.

10. **Withheld facts about account activity.** The fix is a disclosure schedule signed by the client covering in-kind distributions, related-party transactions, services provided, and income-producing activity. Under Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(6), withholding is not neutral; it can invalidate the appraisal outright.

## Who signs what

Responsibility in a digital asset appraisal is distributed, and confusion about the distribution is a common source of defective files.

| Document or element | Signed or completed by | What the signature represents |
|---|---|---|
| Restriction and activity questionnaire | Client, donor, executor, or trustee | That the disclosed facts are complete and accurate |
| Custody or control attestation | Client, or custodian where one exists | That the asset was held, where, and under what authority |
| Appraisal report | Qualified appraiser | The value opinion, the methodology, and the appraiser's qualifications |
| Form 8283, Part I, line 3, columns (a) and (b) | Donor, before submission to donee | The property description and, where applicable, condition |
| Form 8283, Part IV | Appraiser, with taxpayer identification number | The declaration of qualified appraiser status |
| Form 8283, Part V | Donee organization | Acknowledgment of receipt and description of the property |
| Form 8971, Schedule A | Executor | The final value used for consistent basis |

Two rules sit underneath that table. The first is independence: under Reg. §1.170A-17(b)(5), the donor, the donee, a party to the transaction in which the donor acquired the property, related individuals and their employees, and persons barred from practicing before the Internal Revenue Service cannot serve as qualified appraisers. A narrow exception permits a party to the acquisition transaction where the property is contributed within two months of acquisition and the appraised value does not exceed the acquisition price. That exception is narrow for a reason, and it should never be relied on as a planning assumption.

The second is that the appraiser's signature is not a warranty of the client's facts. It is a declaration about the appraiser's qualifications and the appraisal's compliance with generally accepted appraisal standards — the substance and principles of USPAP. When the underlying facts are later shown to be wrong, the signature does not absorb the client's error. It localizes it.

## A deadline calendar built backwards from the return

Deadlines in this practice run backwards from a filing, and the ones that matter most are the earliest, not the latest.

| Milestone | Timing | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Restriction and activity questionnaire returned | At engagement, before any analysis | Client or counsel |
| Acquisition and basis reconstruction complete | Before the valuation is formed | Appraiser, with client records |
| Valuation effective date fixed | No earlier than 60 days before a contribution, and no later than the contribution | Appraiser |
| Appraisal signed and dated | No earlier than 60 days before the contribution | Appraiser |
| Appraisal delivered to donor | Before the return's due date including extensions, or before an amended return is filed | Appraiser |
| Donee acknowledgment, Part V | Before the donor completes the remaining Part I columns | Donee |
| Return filed with Section B, or with the appraisal attached above $500,000 | Filing deadline | Preparer |
| Form 8971 and Schedule A | Within 30 days of the earlier of the due date or the filing date | Executor |
| Form 5498, box 5 | By May 31 following the calendar year | Custodian |
| Donee disposition reporting where the charity sells within three years | Per Section 6050L | Donee |

The calendar carries one message for practitioners. The binding constraint is almost never the return due date. It is the contribution date plus sixty days, working forward, and the acquisition record, working backward. Engagements that begin inside the last thirty days before a filing are engagements in which the appraisal is either rushed or retrospective, and neither outcome serves the client.

## The decision rule

Assemble the file before engaging the appraiser, and assemble it against the valuation date that governs the return. If the acquisition record cannot be reconstructed from primary evidence, expect the appraisal to state that limitation rather than to paper over it — and expect a revenue agent to test it. If the charitable gift has not yet been made and the position exceeds $5,000, order the appraisal now, because the sixty-day window runs forward from the transfer and cannot be reopened.

For firms in Oakland, Wayne, and Genesee counties and for the national advisors who refer into them, the practical division of labor is straightforward. Counsel establishes authority and title. The accountant establishes character, basis, and the reporting position. The appraiser establishes value on a stated date under USPAP, and states the facts on which that value depends. A file that assigns each of those to the right party, and collects it in writing, produces defensible data and a defensible appraisal. A file that assigns all three to the appraiser produces a document that fails the first time it is read by someone with subpoena power.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What documents do I need for a digital asset appraisal?

Six categories, regardless of the purpose: identity and authority documents for the person directing the engagement, custody or control evidence for the asset, acquisition and basis records at lot level, records establishing holding period and which units are ordinary income, market data as of the valuation date, and a signed disclosure of any restriction or agreement affecting sale. The purpose of the appraisal determines the valuation date those records must address, not the categories themselves.

### Does a cryptocurrency donation require a qualified appraisal?

Yes, where the claimed deduction exceeds $5,000 per item or group of similar items. Form 8283, Section B applies and requires a written qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser; the form itself is only an appraisal summary. Above $500,000, the qualified appraisal must be attached to the return.

### Can I use an exchange statement or screenshot as the valuation?

No. An exchange statement establishes that units were held and, sometimes, what was paid. It does not establish fair market value on the valuation date, does not document methodology, and carries no appraiser declaration. A screenshot has no audit trail at all. Both fail the requirement that a qualified appraisal be prepared under generally accepted appraisal standards.

### When must the appraisal be signed for a charitable gift?

No earlier than 60 days before the date of contribution, and the donor must receive it before the return is due, including extensions. If the deduction is first claimed on an amended return, the appraisal must be received before that amended return is filed. The valuation effective date must fall within the same 60-day window, or be the contribution date where the report is dated later.

### Does a Form 706 estate return need a qualified appraisal for crypto?

There is no Section 170-style statutory mandate, but the practical standard is the same. Section 6695A penalizes appraisers for valuation misstatements on estate returns, and Section 6662 imposes accuracy-related penalties on the estate at its own valuation misstatement thresholds. A date-of-death and, where Section 2032 is elected, alternate-date workfile is the defense.

### Who cannot sign as a qualified appraiser?

The donor, the donee, a party to the transaction in which the donor acquired the property, individuals related to those parties and their employees, and anyone barred from practicing before the Internal Revenue Service. A seller may sign only in the narrow case where the property is contributed within two months of acquisition and the appraised value does not exceed the acquisition price.
