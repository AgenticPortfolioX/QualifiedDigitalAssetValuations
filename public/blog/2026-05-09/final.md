# Digital Asset Tax Compliance in 2026: Qualified Appraisals, Cost Basis Reporting, and the New IRS Form 1099-DA

*Published: May 9, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

The era of ambiguous cryptocurrency tax reporting has officially ended. With the issuance of the first Form 1099-DA for the 2025 tax year, the Internal Revenue Service has established a reporting framework that brings digital asset transactions into parity with traditional securities. For taxpayers, tax preparers, estate attorneys, and wealth managers alike, understanding the intersection of these new reporting requirements with existing qualified appraisal obligations is no longer optional — it is essential to maintaining compliance and avoiding costly penalties.

This guide provides a comprehensive examination of the digital asset tax landscape in 2026, with particular emphasis on the qualified appraisal requirements under Internal Revenue Code Section 170(f)(11)(C), the mechanics of the new Form 1099-DA reporting regime, and the practical steps taxpayers must take to ensure their reporting withstands IRS scrutiny.

## Part I: The Form 1099-DA — A New Reporting Paradigm

### What Is Form 1099-DA?

Form 1099-DA, *Digital Asset Proceeds From Broker Transactions*, represents the culmination of a legislative mandate originating from the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021 (IIJA). The statute directed the Treasury Department and the IRS to establish information reporting requirements for digital asset brokers, closing what had been a significant gap in the IRS's ability to match reported income against taxpayer returns.

Beginning in early 2026, brokers — primarily centralized cryptocurrency exchanges, hosted wallet providers, and digital asset payment processors — have been required to file Form 1099-DA with the IRS and furnish copies to their customers for transactions occurring during the 2025 calendar year. This development marks a fundamental shift in the IRS's enforcement capabilities with respect to digital assets.

### What Form 1099-DA Reports

For the 2025 tax year (returns filed in 2026), Form 1099-DA reports the **gross proceeds** from digital asset sales and dispositions executed through custodial brokers. The IRS Tax Tip 2026-07, published January 28, 2026, reminded taxpayers that brokers must send copies of Form 1099-DA by February 17, 2026, and that taxpayers remain responsible for reporting all digital asset transactions regardless of whether they receive the form.

Critically, for 2025 transactions, Form 1099-DA reports gross proceeds **only** — it does not include cost basis information. As the IRS itself acknowledged, "Most of these statements will not include the basis for DA transactions in 2025 and taxpayers will have to calculate basis to determine their gain or loss."

### What Form 1099-DA Does NOT Cover

Understanding the boundaries of Form 1099-DA reporting is as important as understanding its scope. Notably excluded from current reporting requirements are:

- **Decentralized exchange (DEX) transactions:** Noncustodial brokers — including decentralized exchanges and unhosted wallet providers — are outside the scope of the current regulations.
- **Certain DeFi activities:** As identified in IRS Notice 2024-57, transactions involving staking, lending, wrapping and unwrapping, liquidity provision, and notional principal contracts have been deferred from reporting requirements pending further study.
- **Wallet-to-wallet transfers:** Transfers between personal wallets that do not involve a broker transaction are not captured by Form 1099-DA.
- **NFT marketplaces:** While specified NFTs are within scope, the reporting infrastructure for many NFT platforms remains in development.

This gap between what is reported and what is taxable represents a significant compliance challenge. Taxpayers cannot rely solely on their Form 1099-DA to determine their full tax liability; they must independently track and report all taxable digital asset transactions across all platforms and wallet environments.

### The Cost Basis Reporting Transition

Beginning in 2027, for transactions occurring on or after January 1, 2026, brokers will be required to report **cost basis** alongside gross proceeds on Form 1099-DA. This phased approach — gross proceeds first, cost basis later — mirrors the historical implementation of broker reporting for traditional securities and reflects the logistical complexity of implementing cost basis tracking across the digital asset ecosystem.

However, a critical rule change has already taken effect: the IRS has eliminated the so-called "universal wallet" method. Taxpayers must now maintain cost basis records on a **per-wallet or per-account basis**, physically tracing each digital asset from acquisition through disposition within a specific wallet or account. This change significantly increases the documentation burden for taxpayers who hold assets across multiple exchanges, wallets, and platforms.

## Part II: The Qualified Appraisal Requirement — What Every Taxpayer Must Know

### The Statutory Framework

The most frequently overlooked — and potentially most consequential — aspect of digital asset tax compliance concerns the qualified appraisal requirements under Internal Revenue Code Section 170(f)(11)(C). This provision generally requires taxpayers claiming a charitable contribution deduction for property valued in excess of $5,000 to obtain a **qualified appraisal** prepared by a **qualified appraiser**.

The IRS has been unequivocal in its position that digital assets fall within this requirement. In Chief Counsel Advice (CCA) 202302012, issued January 13, 2023, the IRS Office of Chief Counsel analyzed whether a taxpayer who donated cryptocurrency valued at $10,000 and claimed a charitable deduction based solely on the exchange-reported value — without obtaining a qualified appraisal — could sustain that deduction. The conclusion was definitive: the deduction must be denied.

### Why Exchange Values Are Not Sufficient

A common misconception among digital asset holders is that the price displayed on a cryptocurrency exchange — whether Coinbase, Kraken, Binance, or any other platform — constitutes a sufficient valuation for tax purposes. The IRS has rejected this position on multiple grounds:

**First**, cryptocurrency is legally classified as **property**, not a security, for federal tax purposes. Notice 2014-21 established this classification, and subsequent guidance has reaffirmed it. Because cryptocurrency is not a "security" within the meaning of Section 165(g)(2) — which is limited to corporate stock, rights, bonds, and debentures — it does not qualify for the publicly traded securities exemption from the qualified appraisal requirement.

**Second**, the Tax Court has made clear that reliance on exchange-reported values does not satisfy the requirements of Section 170(f)(11). In *Pankratz*, T.C. Memo. 2021-26, the court observed that "four mentions of 'appraisal,' 'appraiser,' or 'appraised' on one page of one form is pretty good notice that substantial noncash donations need to be backed up by an appraisal." The court was referring to Form 8283, Noncash Charitable Contributions, which Tax Court has held provides clear notice of the appraisal requirement.

**Third**, exchange prices reflect marginal transaction values at a point in time — they are not comprehensive fair market value determinations that account for the particular characteristics of the donated asset, market conditions affecting liquidity, or the specific valuation date. A qualified appraisal, by contrast, applies established USPAP (Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice) methodology to arrive at a supported, documented, and defensible fair market value opinion.

### The Tax Court Precedents

Several Tax Court decisions have reinforced the qualified appraisal requirement for digital assets:

In *Schweizer*, T.C. Memo. 2022-102, the court reiterated that the reasonable cause exception to the appraisal requirement "was not intended to provide taxpayers with the choice of whether to obtain a qualified appraisal, but to provide relief where an unsuccessful attempt was made in good faith to comply." In other words, a taxpayer who simply decides not to obtain an appraisal — even if the value is "obvious" — cannot rely on the reasonable cause exception to salvage the deduction.

The Tax Court's reasoning in *Pankratz* is equally instructive. The taxpayer argued that the appraisal requirement should not apply because the value of the donated cryptocurrency was "readily available" from the exchange. The court rejected this argument, emphasizing that Form 8283 itself provides sufficient notice of the appraisal requirement, and that the taxpayer's failure to obtain an appraisal — despite completing the form partially — did not constitute a good-faith effort to comply.

### Practical Implications for Charitable Contributions

For taxpayers considering charitable contributions of digital assets, the compliance requirements are clear:

- **Donations of $5,000 or less:** A qualified appraisal is generally not required, though taxpayers should still document fair market value at the time of donation.
- **Donations exceeding $5,000:** A qualified appraisal prepared by a qualified appraiser is mandatory. The appraisal must be contemporaneous — obtained on or near the date of contribution.
- **Form 8283:** The form must be fully completed and attached to the taxpayer's return. Partial completion is insufficient, as *Pankratz* makes clear.
- **No shortcuts:** Exchange screenshots, blockchain explorer data, or portfolio tracking application reports do not substitute for a qualified appraisal.

## Part III: Beyond Charitable Contributions — Wider Implications of Digital Asset Valuation

### Estate Tax Planning (Form 706)

The qualified appraisal requirement extends well beyond charitable contributions. Estate tax returns (Form 706) require the fair market value of all assets owned by the decedent at the date of death, including digital assets. For estates with significant cryptocurrency, NFT, or other digital asset holdings, a qualified appraisal is essential to establishing the value reported on the estate tax return.

The stakes are particularly high in this context because estate tax valuations are subject to IRS scrutiny, and undervaluation can result in substantial penalties. The IRS has established valuation examination programs that specifically target high-value estates, and digital assets are increasingly a focus of these examinations.

### Self-Directed IRA (SDIRA) Annual Valuations

Self-directed IRA custodians are required to obtain annual valuations of the assets held within the IRA. For SDIRAs that hold digital assets, this means an annual qualified appraisal is necessary to maintain compliance with IRS requirements. The self-directed IRA market represents one of the most significant — and most underserved — segments of the digital asset valuation market.

### Gift Tax Returns (Form 709)

Transfers of digital assets by gift — whether to family members, trusts, or other beneficiaries — trigger gift tax reporting requirements when the value exceeds the annual exclusion amount. For 2026, the annual gift tax exclusion is $18,000 per donee. Gifts of digital assets exceeding this threshold require a qualified appraisal to support the value reported on Form 709.

### Divorce and Marital Dissolution

The increasing prevalence of digital assets in marital estates has created a growing need for qualified appraisals in divorce proceedings. Courts require accurate valuations of all marital assets for equitable distribution, and digital assets — with their unique volatility and liquidity characteristics — present particular valuation challenges that require professional appraisal expertise.

## Part IV: USPAP Methodology for Digital Asset Valuation

### The Professional Standard

The Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP) establish the ethical and performance standards for appraisers in the United States. Developed by the Appraisal Standards Board of The Appraisal Foundation, USPAP is recognized throughout the United States as the authoritative standard for professional appraisal practice.

For digital asset valuation, USPAP compliance requires:

1. **Definition of the appraisal problem:** Identification of the asset to be valued, the property rights to be appraised, the intended use of the appraisal, the intended users, the effective date of value, and the type of value to be estimated (typically fair market value).

2. **Scope of work determination:** The appraiser must determine and disclose the scope of work necessary to produce credible assignment results. For digital assets, this includes analysis of the specific blockchain, verification of ownership, assessment of market liquidity, and consideration of any restrictions on transfer.

3. **Data collection and analysis:** The appraiser must collect and analyze all data relevant to the valuation. For digital assets, this includes transaction data from relevant exchanges and marketplaces, on-chain data where applicable, and consideration of market conditions at the valuation date.

4. **Application of valuation approaches:** USPAP recognizes three approaches to value — the cost approach, the sales comparison approach, and the income approach. For digital assets, the sales comparison approach is typically most relevant, though the income approach may be applicable for certain staking or yield-bearing assets.

5. **Reconciliation and conclusion:** The appraiser must reconcile the indications of value derived from the applicable approaches and arrive at a final value conclusion supported by the evidence.

### Why USPAP-Compliant Valuation Matters

A USPAP-compliant appraisal provides the documentation necessary to withstand IRS scrutiny. When the IRS examines a taxpayer's return and questions the reported value of a digital asset, the existence of a professional appraisal prepared in accordance with USPAP standards shifts the burden of proof and provides a credible, defensible basis for the valuation.

Moreover, for charitable contributions exceeding $5,000, the IRS requires not merely a "valuation" but a **qualified appraisal** as defined in the Treasury Regulations — and that definition incorporates USPAP standards. Without USPAP compliance, the appraisal does not satisfy the statutory requirement.

## Part V: Practical Compliance Framework for 2026

### For Taxpayers Holding Digital Assets

1. **Reconcile all Form 1099-DA forms with your own records.** Do not assume that the gross proceeds reported on Form 1099-DA represent your complete transaction history. Review each form carefully and ensure all taxable transactions — including those on platforms that did not issue a form — are properly reported.

2. **Maintain per-wallet cost basis records.** With the elimination of the universal wallet method, taxpayers must track cost basis separately for each wallet or account. This requires comprehensive recordkeeping of all acquisition dates, purchase prices, transaction fees, and transfer history.

3. **Obtain qualified appraisals for charitable contributions exceeding $5,000.** This is not optional. As the Tax Court has repeatedly affirmed, the failure to obtain a qualified appraisal will result in denial of the deduction — regardless of the actual value of the donated asset.

4. **Plan for estate and gift tax compliance.** If your digital asset holdings exceed applicable exclusion amounts, engage a qualified appraiser to prepare contemporaneous valuations for estate and gift tax purposes.

5. **Review SDIRA valuation requirements annually.** If you hold digital assets in a self-directed IRA, confirm that your custodian's valuation practices satisfy IRS requirements.

### For CPAs and Tax Preparers

1. **Educate clients on the qualified appraisal requirement.** Many taxpayers — and even some tax professionals — remain unaware that digital asset donations over $5,000 require qualified appraisals. Proactive education reduces audit risk for your clients and your practice.

2. **Verify Form 8283 completion.** Ensure that clients who have made charitable contributions of digital assets have fully completed Form 8283 and attached the required qualified appraisal.

3. **Reconcile multi-platform holdings.** Clients who trade across multiple exchanges and wallets may receive multiple Form 1099-DA forms. Ensure all reported proceeds are properly aggregated and reconciled against the client's complete transaction history.

4. **Consider cost basis software with wallet-level tracking.** Given the elimination of the universal wallet method, software solutions that support wallet-level cost basis tracking are increasingly essential for clients with complex holdings.

### For Estate Attorneys and Wealth Managers

1. **Include digital assets in estate planning discussions.** Many high-net-worth clients have digital asset holdings that are not reflected in traditional financial account statements. Proactive identification and valuation of these assets is essential to effective estate planning.

2. **Engage qualified appraisers early.** Valuation of digital assets requires specialized expertise that most general appraisers do not possess. Identify and engage qualified appraisers with digital asset expertise well in advance of filing deadlines.

3. **Document transfer instructions.** For clients who intend to transfer digital assets to trusts, beneficiaries, or charitable organizations, ensure that clear transfer instructions are documented and that the valuation date is properly established.

## Part VI: Enforcement Trends and What They Mean

### Heightened IRS Scrutiny

The introduction of Form 1099-DA is not an isolated administrative change — it is part of a broader IRS enforcement initiative targeting digital asset noncompliance. The agency has invested significantly in data analytics capabilities designed to identify discrepancies between reported transactions and filed returns.

For taxpayers with unreported digital asset transactions, the risk of detection has increased substantially. The IRS now receives third-party reports from brokers that can be matched against individual returns, and discrepancies that would previously have gone unnoticed are now readily identifiable.

### Penalty Exposure

Failure to comply with digital asset reporting requirements carries significant penalty exposure:

- **Substantial valuation misstatement penalties** under Section 6662 can reach 40% of the underpayment where the claimed value is 150% or more of the correct value.
- **Failure to obtain a qualified appraisal** may result in complete disallowance of the claimed deduction, plus accuracy-related penalties.
- **Failure to report digital asset income** may result in negligence penalties, substantial understatement penalties, and, in egregious cases, fraud penalties.

### The Path Forward

The regulatory landscape for digital assets continues to evolve. The White House issued recommendations on digital asset transactions in January 2026, and additional guidance from Treasury and the IRS is expected. Taxpayers who maintain comprehensive records, obtain qualified appraisals where required, and engage qualified professionals with digital asset expertise will be best positioned to navigate this evolving compliance environment.
