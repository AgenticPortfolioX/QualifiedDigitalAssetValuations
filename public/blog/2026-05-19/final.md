# Estate Planning for Digital Assets: Qualified Appraisals, Form 706, and Protecting Your Crypto Legacy

*Published: May 19, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

## Introduction

In 2026, over 560 million people worldwide own cryptocurrency. What was once a niche interest for early adopters has become a mainstream asset class held by high-net-worth individuals, estate planners, and institutional investors. Yet despite this explosive growth, the estate planning profession has been slow to develop standardized approaches for handling digital assets at death.

Consider this: a recent survey found that **89% of cryptocurrency owners are concerned their families may struggle to identify and access these assets after their death**. That concern is well-founded. Without proper planning, a lifetime of digital asset accumulation — Bitcoin, Ethereum, NFTs, DeFi positions — can become irretrievably lost or, worse, a source of costly IRS disputes.

This guide provides a comprehensive framework for estate planning professionals, executors, and digital asset holders navigating the complex intersection of estate tax law, qualified appraisal requirements, and blockchain asset management.

---

## Section 1: The Unique Challenges of Digital Asset Estate Administration

### 1.1 The Access Problem

Unlike traditional assets, cryptocurrency and NFTs are secured by private keys. Lose the keys, lose the assets — permanently. The decentralized nature of blockchain means there is no "forgot password" button, no bank to contact, no central authority to restore access. When a digital asset holder dies without documenting their private key storage, their wealth effectively vanishes.

**The cold storage paradox**: Hardware wallets and cold storage solutions are the most secure method for holding crypto, but they present a unique estate planning challenge. The seed phrase or private keys are typically known only to the owner. Without explicit documentation of how to access these devices, even a sophisticated executor cannot retrieve the assets.

### 1.2 The Valuation Challenge

Digital assets present valuation complexities that traditional estate appraisers rarely encounter:

**Volatility**: Bitcoin has historically experienced drawdowns of 80% or more within a single tax year. The value of a portfolio on the date of death may differ dramatically from its value even days earlier. Yet the IRS requires the fair market value (FMV) on the specific date of death — not a moving average, not a range.

**Illiquidity**: NFTs, in particular, pose significant valuation challenges. A Bored Ape Yacht Club NFT, for example, may trade infrequently, making comparable sales analysis difficult. Trait-specific valuations, floor price analysis, and market condition adjustments all require professional judgment grounded in USPAP standards.

**Portfolio complexity**: A single decedent may hold assets across multiple blockchains, exchanges, DeFi protocols, and wallets, each requiring separate valuation analysis. Wrapped tokens, staked assets, and liquidity pool positions add further layers of complexity.

### 1.3 The Jurisdictional Variability

Digital assets sit uncomfortably in existing legal frameworks. Different jurisdictions classify crypto differently — as property, as currency, as commodities, or as something else entirely. The Uniform Law Commission's Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (UFADAA) has been adopted in varying forms across states, creating a patchwork of rules governing executor authority over digital property.

## Section 2: IRS Form 706 and Digital Asset Reporting

### 2.1 When Is Form 706 Required?

For estates of decedents dying in 2025, the federal estate tax exemption is **$13.99 million per individual** (or $27.98 million for married couples using portability). Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) sunset scheduled for the end of 2025 — and which has now taken effect in 2026 — this exemption is projected to revert to approximately **$7 million per individual**, indexed for inflation.

This means many more estates will face Form 706 filing requirements in 2026 and beyond. For digital asset holders who accumulated significant wealth during the crypto market's growth cycles, estate tax exposure is a real and pressing concern.

**Key consideration**: Even if the gross estate falls below the exemption threshold, it may be advantageous to file Form 706 to elect portability of the deceased spousal unused exclusion (DSUE) amount. This election must be made on a timely filed Form 706.

### 2.2 Reporting Digital Assets on Form 706

The Internal Revenue Service requires comprehensive disclosure of all assets owned by the decedent at death, including digital assets. Schedule F (Other Miscellaneous Property Reportable on Form 706) is the typical location for reporting cryptocurrency and NFTs.

For each digital asset, the executor must report:

- **Description**: Type of asset (Bitcoin, Ethereum, specific NFT collection and token ID)
- **Quantity**: Number of units or tokens
- **Date-of-death fair market value**: Determined by a qualified appraisal
- **Valuation method**: How the FMV was determined (market approach, cost approach, income approach)

**Critical requirement**: Under Treasury Regulation §20.2031-6(b), when an estate includes property of a kind that requires expert valuation, the executor must file with the return an appraisal of the expert, executed under oath. This is the qualified appraisal requirement, and it applies to digital assets just as it applies to fine art, jewelry, or closely held business interests.

### 2.3 The Consequences of Inadequate Valuation

The *Kollsman v. Commissioner* case (2019) illustrates the stakes. In that matter, an estate's expert valued two Old Master paintings at $500,000 and $100,000 respectively. The IRS challenged the valuations, and the Tax Court ultimately valued the paintings at approximately $2 million and $375,000 — a difference of nearly $1.8 million. The court specifically noted that the expert was not a qualified appraiser and had a conflict of interest.

For digital asset estates, the risk is even greater. Without a USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal from a recognized digital asset appraiser, an estate faces:

1. **IRS audit scrutiny**: The IRS Art Appraisal Services (AAS) unit has expanded its scope to include digital assets. Improper valuations trigger automatic review.
2. **Penalties and interest**: Underpayment of estate tax due to undervaluation can result in accuracy-related penalties under IRC §6662.
3. **Executor liability**: Executors who file returns without proper appraisals may face personal liability for tax deficiencies.

## Section 3: The Step-Up in Basis — A Critical Tax Planning Opportunity

### 3.1 How Step-Up in Basis Works for Digital Assets

Under IRC §1014, when a decedent owns property at death, the beneficiary's basis in the property is "stepped up" to its fair market value on the date of death (or the alternate valuation date, if elected). Because the IRS classifies cryptocurrency as property under IRS Notice 2014-21, digital assets are eligible for this treatment.

**Practical example**: If a decedent purchased 10 Bitcoin for a total of $50,000 in 2015, and those Bitcoin are worth $600,000 on the date of death, the beneficiary's new basis is $600,000. If the beneficiary sells immediately, they owe little to no capital gains tax.

This step-up in basis can result in massive tax savings, particularly for long-term holders with low cost bases — a common scenario among early crypto adopters.

### 3.2 When Step-Up Does NOT Apply

The step-up in basis does not apply to:

- **Digital assets held in retirement accounts** (Traditional IRA, Roth IRA, Solo 401(k)) — these accounts have their own distribution rules under IRC §408 and §401.
- **Assets gifted during life** — gifts carry over the donor's basis under IRC §1015.
- **Assets held by a partnership or corporation** — the entity's basis, not the decedent's, controls.

### 3.3 Documentation Requirements for Step-Up in Basis

To successfully claim a step-up in basis, the executor must be able to demonstrate the date-of-death FMV. This requires:

1. **A qualified appraisal report** prepared in compliance with USPAP Standards 7 and 9 (Personal Property Appraisal)
2. **Date-specific valuation** reflecting market conditions on the exact date of death
3. **Supporting data**, including exchange prices, comparable sales, and market analysis
4. **Signed certification** from a qualified appraiser

Without this documentation, the IRS may challenge the claimed basis upon a subsequent sale by the beneficiary, potentially subjecting the beneficiary to additional tax, penalties, and interest.

## Section 4: The Digital Executor — A New Fiduciary Role

### 4.1 Why a Digital Executor Is Necessary

Traditional executors may lack the technical expertise to identify, access, and value digital assets. The role of the **digital executor** — a person specifically appointed to handle digital assets within the estate — has emerged as a best practice among sophisticated estate planners.

A digital executor should have:

- Familiarity with blockchain technology and cryptocurrency wallets
- Understanding of exchange account access and recovery procedures
- Knowledge of NFT marketplaces and valuation
- The ability to coordinate with a qualified digital asset appraiser

### 4.2 Legal Authority of the Digital Executor

Under the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA), adopted in most states, fiduciaries — including executors — have authority over digital assets. However, this authority is limited by:

- The decedent's expressed consent (in a will, trust, or online tool)
- The terms of service of the digital platform
- State-specific variations in RUFADAA adoption

Estate planning documents should explicitly authorize the digital executor to access, manage, and distribute digital assets, including the authority to engage a qualified appraiser for valuation purposes.

### 4.3 Practical Steps for Appointing a Digital Executor

1. **Name the digital executor in the will or trust** — Separate from the traditional executor, with overlapping but distinct authority
2. **Create a digital asset inventory** — Including wallet addresses, exchange accounts, seed phrase storage locations, and password managers
3. **Document access procedures** — How to retrieve private keys, access hardware wallets, and recover accounts
4. **Update regularly** — As new assets are acquired and new platforms adopted

## Section 5: Qualified Appraisals for Digital Asset Estates — What the IRS Requires

### 5.1 The Qualified Appraisal Standard

Under the Pension Protection Act of 2006 and Treasury Regulation §1.170A-13, a qualified appraisal must:

1. Be made by a qualified appraiser (as defined by IRC §170(f)(11)(E))
2. Relate to an appraisal that is treated as a qualified appraisal under the regulations
3. Be prepared in accordance with generally accepted appraisal standards (USPAP)

For estate tax purposes, the same qualified appraisal standard applies through Treasury Regulation §20.2031-6.

### 5.2 Who Is a Qualified Appraiser for Digital Assets?

A qualified appraiser must:

- Hold a recognized appraisal designation from a professional appraisal organization
- Have at least two years of experience in the specific type of property being appraised
- Regularly prepare appraisals for compensation
- Not be an "excluded" individual (the donor, donee, certain related parties)

For digital assets, this means the appraiser must have demonstrable experience in cryptocurrency and NFT valuation — not merely general business valuation credentials.

### 5.3 USPAP Standards for Digital Asset Valuation

The Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP) provide the framework for credible digital asset valuations:

**USPAP Standard 7** (Personal Property Appraisal, Development): Requires the appraiser to:
- Identify the problem to be solved
- Determine the scope of work necessary
- Collect and analyze market data
- Apply appropriate valuation approaches
- Reach a well-supported value conclusion

**USPAP Standard 9** (Personal Property Appraisal, Reporting): Requires the appraiser to:
- Communicate the appraisal in a manner that is meaningful to the client
- Disclose all assumptions and limiting conditions
- Provide sufficient information for the intended user to understand the analysis
- Include the appraiser's certification

For digital asset estates, the appraisal report should include:

- **Blockchain verification**: Transaction hashes, wallet addresses, and on-chain evidence of ownership
- **Market data**: Exchange prices, trading volumes, and liquidity analysis
- **Comparable sales**: For NFTs, analysis of recent sales of similar tokens with comparable traits
- **Volatility adjustments**: Discussion of how market conditions on the date of death were assessed
- **Methodology statement**: Clear explanation of the valuation approach and why it was selected

## Section 6: The Charitable Donation Alternative — Form 8283 Planning

### 6.1 Lifetime Giving vs. Testamentary Transfer

For high-net-worth individuals with strong charitable inclinations, donating digital assets during life — rather than transferring them at death — can provide significant tax benefits:

- **Income tax deduction**: Under IRC §170, donors who itemize can deduct the FMV of appreciated digital assets held for more than one year, up to 30% of adjusted gross income, with a 5-year carryforward
- **Avoidance of capital gains**: Unlike selling and donating cash, donating the asset directly avoids capital gains tax
- **Estate tax reduction**: Removing assets from the gross estate reduces estate tax exposure

### 6.2 Form 8283 Requirements for Digital Asset Donations

For non-cash charitable contributions exceeding $5,000, the donor must obtain a qualified appraisal and file **Form 8283** with their income tax return. For contributions of art or collectibles valued at $20,000 or more, a complete copy of the appraisal must be attached.

For digital asset donations, the Form 8283 must be signed by both the donor and the qualified appraiser, and by the donee organization acknowledging receipt. The IRS uses this form to verify that the claimed value is supportable.

## Section 7: Practical Planning Recommendations

### 7.1 For Digital Asset Holders

1. **Document your holdings comprehensively** — Create a digital asset inventory that includes wallet addresses, exchange accounts, seed phrase storage locations, and password manager credentials. Update this inventory at least quarterly.

2. **Incorporate digital assets into your estate plan explicitly** — General language in a will or trust may not suffice. Explicitly bequeath digital assets and authorize your executor to engage a qualified appraiser.

3. **Appoint a digital executor** — Choose someone with sufficient technical knowledge to navigate blockchain-based assets, or provide your traditional executor with clear instructions for engaging specialists.

4. **Maintain a qualified appraisal** — For significant holdings, consider obtaining a baseline qualified appraisal during life. This creates a reference point and familiarizes the appraiser with your portfolio.

5. **Consider the TCJA sunset** — With the estate tax exemption now reduced to approximately $7 million in 2026, review your estate tax exposure and consider lifetime gifting strategies where appropriate.

### 7.2 For Estate Attorneys and CPAs

1. **Screen for digital assets in the intake process** — Every new estate planning client with significant wealth should be asked about cryptocurrency, NFTs, and other digital asset holdings.

2. **Engage qualified appraisers early** — Do not wait until the Form 706 deadline is approaching. Qualified digital asset appraisers with USPAP credentials are a specialized resource — secure their engagement early.

3. **Document the valuation methodology** — Ensure that any appraisal report submitted with Form 706 clearly explains the methodology used and provides supporting market data.

4. **Consider the alternate valuation date** — Under IRC §2032, if elected, the estate may value assets as of six months after death (or earlier distribution date). Given digital asset volatility, this election may be advantageous.

5. **Educate clients** — Many digital asset holders are unaware of the estate tax implications of their holdings. Proactive education can prevent costly surprises for their heirs.

### 7.3 For Executors and Trustees

1. **Identify all digital assets promptly** — Search for cryptocurrency exchange accounts, hardware wallets, digital asset references in financial records, and communications about digital holdings.

2. **Secure access without triggering transactions** — Changing passwords or accessing accounts should be done carefully to avoid inadvertently triggering taxable events.

3. **Engage a qualified appraiser before filing** — A USPAP-compliant appraisal provides the evidentiary foundation for the estate tax return and protects the executor from personal liability.

4. **File Form 706 timely** — The 9-month deadline applies regardless of the complexity of the digital asset portfolio. If an extension is needed, file Form 4768 before the original due date.

5. **Document the basis for heirs** — After distribution, provide each beneficiary with documentation of the date-of-death FMV to support their stepped-up basis in the inherited assets.

## Section 8: The Consequences of Inaction

The costs of failing to properly plan for digital asset estates are substantial and multifaceted:

**Tax costs**: Without a qualified appraisal to establish date-of-death FMV, beneficiaries may overpay capital gains tax when selling inherited assets — or underpay and face IRS scrutiny. The accuracy-related penalty under IRC §6662 can add 20% to any underpayment.

**Legal costs**: Estate disputes over digital asset valuation can consume significant legal fees. The *Kollsman* litigation, which involved relatively traditional assets, spanned years and multiple appeals.

**Lost assets**: The most devastating cost is the complete loss of digital assets due to inaccessible private keys. An estimated 20% of all Bitcoin — worth hundreds of billions of dollars — is currently locked in inaccessible wallets. A portion of this represents the estates of decedents who did not plan for succession.

**Family conflict**: When digital assets with volatile values are distributed among beneficiaries, disputes over valuation and equitable distribution are common. A qualified appraisal provides an objective, third-party valuation that can preempt such conflicts.

## Conclusion

Digital assets represent a paradigm shift in estate planning. The combination of technological complexity, valuation challenges, and stringent IRS requirements creates a perfect storm for unprepared executors and estate planners. The solution is not to avoid digital assets — they are too significant a component of modern wealth for that — but to approach them with the same rigor and professionalism applied to any other high-value asset class.

The key takeaway is this: **a qualified appraisal from a USPAP-compliant, experienced digital asset appraiser is not an optional luxury for digital asset estates — it is a regulatory requirement and a fiduciary necessity**. Without it, executors face personal liability, beneficiaries face tax exposure, and the IRS has an open door to challenge every valuation on the return.

For estate planning professionals, the time to develop expertise in digital asset valuation is now. The clients are here, their holdings are substantial, and their legacies depend on getting this right.
