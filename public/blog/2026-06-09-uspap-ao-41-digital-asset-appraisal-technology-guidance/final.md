# USPAP Advisory Opinion 41 and Digital Asset Appraisals: What the New Technology Guidance Means for Crypto and NFT Valuation Professionals

*Published: June 9, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

On April 23, 2026, the Appraisal Standards Board (ASB) of The Appraisal Foundation took a significant step into the future of professional valuation. In a public meeting whose outcome will reverberate across every appraisal discipline — from real estate to personal property to the rapidly evolving field of digital asset valuation — the board formally adopted **Advisory Opinion 41 (AO-41)** , providing comprehensive guidance on the use of technology in appraisal and appraisal review assignments.

For professionals engaged in the valuation of cryptocurrencies, non-fungible tokens (NFTs), and other digital assets, AO-41 is more than a routine standards update. It is a foundational document that defines how we must engage with the technological tools that are inextricably woven into blockchain-based valuation. This guide provides a thorough examination of AO-41, its key provisions, and — most importantly — what it means for digital asset appraisers, their clients, and the CPAs and estate attorneys who rely on qualified appraisals for IRS compliance.

---

## The Context: Why AO-41 Matters Now

### The Technology Landscape in 2026

The appraisal profession operates in an environment that would have been unrecognizable a decade ago. Automated valuation models (AVMs) process millions of data points in seconds. Machine learning algorithms identify patterns in markets that human analysts might miss. Generative artificial intelligence can draft narrative reports, analyze market trends, and — in the context of digital assets — evaluate thousands of NFT trait combinations across multiple marketplaces simultaneously.

Yet until April 2026, the formal USPAP guidance governing technology use had not been meaningfully updated since the adoption of Advisory Opinion 18 (AVMs) and Advisory Opinion 37 (computer-assisted valuation tools) — advisories developed in an era before blockchain, before generative AI, and before digital assets constituted a trillion-dollar asset class requiring professional valuation.

### What AO-41 Replaces

AO-41 consolidates and supersedes two existing advisories:

- **Advisory Opinion 18 (AO-18)** : Automated Valuation Models, which provided guidance on the use of AVMs in appraisal practice
- **Advisory Opinion 37 (AO-37)** : Computer-Assisted Valuation Tools, which addressed spreadsheet software and basic analytical applications

Both advisories were designed for a technology landscape that no longer exists. AO-18 focused narrowly on AVM outputs from real estate databases. AO-37 addressed what were then considered sophisticated analytical tools — regression models in Excel, database queries, and statistical software packages. Neither advisory contemplated blockchain data aggregation, AI-powered trait analysis for NFTs, machine learning price prediction models, or cloud-based generative AI platforms capable of producing appraisal narratives.

### The Adoption Timeline

The journey to AO-41 began with a first exposure draft released in January 2026, followed by public comment periods, stakeholder feedback, and a revised second exposure draft. On April 23, 2026, the ASB adopted AO-41 effective immediately, with board members emphasizing that the advisory is intended to *encourage*, not discourage, the responsible use of technology in appraisal practice. The final version is expected to be posted to The Appraisal Foundation's website in the coming weeks.

## The Core Principles of AO-41

### Technology as Tool, Not Replacement

AO-41's foundational principle is both simple and profound: **technology may assist an appraiser, but it cannot replace the appraiser's judgment or responsibility.** A tool cannot comply with USPAP; only the appraiser can.

This principle is especially critical for digital asset valuation, where the line between "tool output" and "assignment result" can blur. When an appraiser runs a blockchain data aggregator to compile transaction histories, queries an NFT marketplace API for comparable sales data, or uses a machine learning model to assess trait rarity premiums — those outputs are raw *information*. They become assignment results only after the appraiser analyzes them, determines their relevance, and decides to rely on them in forming an opinion of value.

### The Key Distinction: Output vs. Assignment Results

One of AO-41's most important contributions is its explicit articulation of the distinction between tool output and assignment results:

> **Output** — from spreadsheets, regression tools, AVMs, machine learning systems, or generative AI — remains information until the appraiser evaluates it, determines its relevance, and decides to rely on it.

> **Assignment results** are the appraiser's opinions and conclusions — not the product of any tool.

For digital asset appraisers, this distinction carries practical weight. Consider a typical digital asset appraisal engagement:

1. The appraiser queries a blockchain explorer (Etherscan, Solscan) for transaction history
2. The appraiser uses an NFT marketplace analytics tool (e.g., OpenSea Pro, Reservoir) to identify comparable sales
3. The appraiser runs a trait rarity analysis using market data aggregation software
4. The appraiser cross-references wallet activity to verify ownership and transaction provenance

Each of these steps involves technology tools producing *output*. None of them, individually or collectively, constitutes an *assignment result*. The assignment result emerges only when the appraiser synthesizes these data points, applies professional judgment, and forms a defensible opinion of fair market value. AO-41 makes clear that the appraiser bears full responsibility for that synthesis, regardless of which tools contributed.

### The Competency Requirement

AO-41 reaffirms that the COMPETENCY RULE applies in full to technology use. However, the advisory provides important clarification about what competency means in the context of modern appraisal tools:

**Appraisers must understand a tool's capabilities and limitations to the extent necessary for the assignment.** This does not require:

- Reverse engineering proprietary algorithms
- Fully understanding internal programming or model architecture
- Possessing the technical expertise of a software engineer

It *does* require:

- Sufficient knowledge to use the tool competently within the scope of the assignment
- The ability to recognize when output is unreliable, inconsistent, or outside the tool's validated parameters
- The judgment to determine appropriate levels of reliance on tool output

For digital asset appraisers, the competency requirement has specific implications. Using a blockchain data aggregator requires understanding what data it draws from, whether it accounts for wash trading on NFT marketplaces, how it handles cross-chain transactions, and whether its timestamping methodology aligns with the appraisal's effective date. Using a generative AI tool to assist with analysis requires understanding the model's training data, its known biases, and the appropriate level of reliance on its output.

## AO-41's Specific Provisions and Digital Asset Applications

### Confidentiality in Cloud-Based Systems

One of the most timely provisions in AO-41 addresses confidentiality risks associated with AI and cloud-based platforms. The advisory requires appraisers to take **reasonable steps** before entering confidential information or assignment results into any tool — especially AI or cloud-based systems — to ensure compliance with the USPAP ETHICS RULE's confidentiality requirements.

This has profound implications for digital asset appraisal. Consider the following scenarios:

**Scenario 1: Generative AI for Analysis**
An appraiser uploads wallet addresses, transaction histories, and portfolio composition data to a generative AI platform to assist with market analysis. The platform processes this data through cloud-based servers, potentially exposing the client's digital asset holdings, transaction patterns, and wallet identities — including personally identifiable information linked to those wallets.

**Scenario 2: Cloud-Based Valuation Tools**
An appraiser uses a Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) platform for NFT comparable sales analysis. The client's NFT collection data, including blockchain identifiers, purchase prices, and portfolio metadata, is transmitted to and stored on third-party servers.

**Required Action Under AO-41:**
The appraiser must evaluate whether the tool's privacy policy, data handling practices, and terms of service adequately protect client confidentiality. If the tool retains, shares, or uses client data in ways that could compromise confidentiality, the appraiser must either:
- Refrain from entering confidential information into the tool, or
- Obtain informed client consent regarding the data handling practices, or
- Use an alternative tool with adequate confidentiality protections

For QDAV, this provision reinforces our commitment to data security in digital asset appraisal. Client blockchain holdings, wallet relationships, and portfolio compositions are sensitive information that require protections commensurate with their financial and privacy significance.

### Workfile Documentation Requirements

AO-41 provides updated guidance on workfile documentation when technology tools are used. The advisory distinguishes between two scenarios:

**When Tool Output is Relied Upon:**
If the appraiser relies on technology output in forming assignment results, the workfile must contain sufficient documentation to support the opinions and conclusions. This may include:
- The tool's output data
- Related input data and parameters
- Prompts or instructions provided to the tool
- The appraiser's analysis explaining how the output was used and why reliance was appropriate

**When Tool Output is Reviewed but Not Relied Upon:**
Documentation requirements depend on the scope of work, intended user expectations, and whether the decision not to rely affected assignment results. If a tool produced output that contradicted other data sources and the appraiser decided not to rely on it, documentation of that decision-making process may be necessary to demonstrate USPAP compliance.

**Digital Asset Application:**
For a typical digital asset appraisal, the workfile documentation might include:
- Blockchain explorer output showing transaction history and wallet holdings
- Marketplace comparable sales data with query parameters and dates
- Trait rarity analysis output with methodology documentation
- Notes on why certain data points were included or excluded (e.g., excluding sales identified as wash trading)
- AI-assisted analysis output with prompts used and appraiser evaluation of results

### Disclosure Requirements

AO-41 addresses a question that has been the subject of considerable debate in the appraisal profession: when must an appraiser disclose the use of technology?

The advisory provides a nuanced answer:

- **No need** to disclose every tool used merely because it was used
- **Disclosure is necessary** when omission would make the report misleading or prevent intended users from understanding the work performed
- **Examples requiring disclosure**: A proprietary algorithm or generative AI that materially contributed to analysis or conclusions — in such cases, the appraiser should explain how the tool was used and how its output was incorporated
- **No disclosure needed** for tools used only for formatting, grammar, spell-checking, or non-analytical report preparation

For digital asset appraisals, material technology contributions that may warrant disclosure include:
- Machine learning models used to analyze NFT trait rarity
- Automated market data aggregation systems
- AI-assisted comparable selection or adjustment analysis
- Algorithmic identification of wash trading or market manipulation

### Client Restrictions on Technology Use

AO-41 also addresses a practical concern: what happens when a client prohibits or mandates the use of specific tools?

The advisory states that clients may prohibit or require specific tools, but they **cannot** limit the scope of work to a degree that prevents credible assignment results. If a restriction prevents USPAP compliance, the appraiser must decline the assignment, withdraw, or modify the scope of work.

This provision is particularly relevant for digital asset appraisals where clients may have preferences about:
- Which blockchain data providers are used
- Whether AI-powered analysis tools are employed
- The level of technological sophistication applied to valuation methodology

## AO-41 and the Unique Challenges of Digital Asset Valuation

### The Inherently Technological Nature of Digital Asset Appraisal

Digital asset appraisal differs from traditional personal property appraisal in a fundamental way: the subject property exists *within* a technological infrastructure. Unlike a painting or a piece of jewelry, where technology is a tool applied *externally* to the valuation process, digital assets are blockchain-based instruments whose very existence, ownership, and transaction history are recorded on distributed ledgers that can only be accessed through technology.

This creates a unique compliance environment under AO-41:

**A. Blockchain Data as Foundation Evidence**
Every digital asset appraisal relies on blockchain data to establish fundamental facts: ownership, transaction history, provenance, and market context. Under AO-41, blockchain explorer output constitutes "tool output" that the appraiser must evaluate, interpret, and decide whether to rely upon. The appraiser must understand the limitations of blockchain data — including the potential for wash trading, the difference between listing prices and actual sales, and the implications of cross-chain transactions.

**B. Market Data Aggregation**
Digital asset markets generate enormous volumes of data across dozens of marketplaces, multiple blockchains, and varying trading pairs. Aggregating this data into a coherent picture requires technology tools. AO-41 requires that appraisers understand the aggregation methodology, recognize when aggregated data may be incomplete or misleading, and document the basis for relying on aggregated rather than raw data.

**C. NFT Trait Analysis**
Valuing an NFT often requires analyzing the rarity and market premium of individual traits — a task that is practically impossible without technology given the thousands of combinations in a typical collection. AO-41 acknowledges that such technology-assisted analysis is permissible but requires that the appraiser understand the methodology, verify its reliability for the specific collection being appraised, and document the analytical process.

### The "Black Box" Problem

AO-41 provides important guidance on what has become known as the "black box" problem — when appraisers use proprietary tools whose internal workings are not fully transparent. The advisory acknowledges that appraisers are not required to reverse-engineer proprietary algorithms but must have sufficient understanding to use the tool competently and recognize unreliable output.

For digital asset appraisers, this is especially relevant when using:
- Third-party NFT valuation platforms whose pricing algorithms are proprietary
- Machine learning models for market prediction that operate as "black boxes"
- AI-powered trait analysis tools whose methodology is not fully disclosed

The practical implication is clear: appraisers must exercise professional judgment in selecting tools and must be prepared to demonstrate that their choice of tools, and their level of reliance on tool outputs, was appropriate for the assignment.

## The Road Ahead: AO-41 as a Living Framework

The ASB has emphasized that AO-41 is not intended as a final statement on technology and appraisal practice. Board members noted during the April 23 public meeting that the advisory represents a framework designed to accommodate rapidly evolving technology. As AI, blockchain analytics, and digital asset markets continue to develop, AO-41 provides the principles-based structure within which appraisers can responsibly integrate new tools.

For the digital asset appraisal profession specifically, AO-41 establishes a critical foundation. It affirms that technology is not merely permissible in appraisal practice but can enhance the quality and defensibility of valuation conclusions — provided that appraisers maintain the professional judgment, competency, and ethical responsibility that USPAP has always required.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Does AO-41 create new USPAP requirements?
No. AO-41 does not create new standards or reinterpret existing ones. It illustrates how existing USPAP obligations — including the ETHICS RULE, COMPETENCY RULE, SCOPE OF WORK RULE, and RECORD KEEPING RULE — apply when appraisers use technology in their practice.

### 2. Does AO-41 apply to digital asset appraisals specifically?
While AO-41 is written generically to apply across all appraisal disciplines, its provisions are directly relevant to digital asset appraisals given the inherently technological nature of blockchain-based valuation. The advisory's guidance on confidentiality, documentation, and competency has particular force in this context.

### 3. Do I need to disclose every tool used in my digital asset appraisal?
No. Under AO-41, disclosure is required only when omission would make the report misleading. Tools used for formatting, grammar, or non-analytical purposes do not require disclosure. Tools that materially contribute to the analysis or conclusions — such as AI models or proprietary algorithms — may warrant disclosure.

### 4. Can the IRS challenge a digital asset appraisal because of technology use?
Not on the basis of technology use alone. AO-41 explicitly affirms that appraisers may use technology tools. The question for IRS purposes is whether the appraisal is a qualified appraisal under Section 170 of the Internal Revenue Code, performed by a qualified appraiser, and supported by competent evidence — regardless of which tools were used in its preparation.

### 5. What happens if a client restricts my use of certain valuation tools?
Under AO-41, clients may prohibit or require specific tools, but they cannot restrict the scope of work to a degree that prevents credible assignment results. If a restriction would prevent the appraiser from forming a reliable opinion of value, the appraiser must decline or modify the assignment.

### 6. How should digital asset appraisers document their use of AI tools?
Under AO-41, if AI output is relied upon, the workfile should include the tool's output, the prompts or instructions provided, and the appraiser's analysis explaining how the output was incorporated into the assignment results. If AI is reviewed but not relied upon, documentation requirements depend on the scope of work.

### 7. When did AO-41 take effect?
AO-41 was adopted by the Appraisal Standards Board on April 23, 2026, and is effective immediately. It replaces Advisory Opinion 18 (Automated Valuation Models) and Advisory Opinion 37 (Computer-Assisted Valuation Tools).

### 8. How does AO-41 protect client confidentiality in cloud-based systems?
AO-41 requires appraisers to take reasonable steps to ensure that entering confidential information into any tool — especially AI or cloud-based platforms — does not improperly disclose confidential information or violate client confidentiality requirements. This includes reviewing the tool's privacy policy and data handling practices.
