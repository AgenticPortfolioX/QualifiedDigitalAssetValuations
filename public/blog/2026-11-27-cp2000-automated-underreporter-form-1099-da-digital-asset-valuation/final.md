# The Notice That Arrives Before Any Examination

An examination has a beginning. The revenue agent issues an Information Document Request, the practitioner assembles the file, the parties negotiate, and Appeals reviews. Every stage is legible, and every stage involves a person looking at a document.

The Automated Underreporter program has no such structure. A computer compares the information returns the IRS received against the return the taxpayer filed, finds a difference, and a notice prints. No examiner decided to look; no one formed a view about the taxpayer's credibility, the market, or the asset. The comparison runs on numbers, and the numbers arriving on a Form 1099-DA for a 2025 disposition are, in most cases, half a transaction.

That asymmetry is now operational. Gross proceeds reporting on Form 1099-DA began for transactions effected on or after January 1, 2025. Basis reporting did not begin until covered securities were sold on or after January 1, 2026 — and a covered security is a narrow category that excludes anything acquired before 2026 or transferred into a broker's custody. In fiscal year 2025 the Automated Underreporter program closed 987,460 cases and produced $5.9 billion in additional assessments, against 497,621 examinations producing $26.8 billion, according to the IRS Data Book. The matching program reached roughly twice as many taxpayers as the entire examination function, at about a ninth of the yield per case. It is a volume instrument, and volume instruments do not distinguish a taxpayer who omitted a sale from one who reported it without the basis the broker never supplied.

What follows is an account of what the notice is, what it is not, which of its two varieties a transaction export can answer, and what has to be in the file for the variety it cannot.

## Key Takeaways

- A CP2000 is a proposal generated by document matching, not a bill and not an audit; Tax Topic 652 says so, and the notice carries a Response form rather than a demand.
- Gross-proceeds reporting on Form 1099-DA began for transactions effected on or after January 1, 2025; basis reporting began only for covered securities sold from January 1, 2026, leaving assets acquired before 2026 and assets transferred in outside broker basis reporting.
- The thirty-day response window runs from the date printed on the notice, not from delivery, and it is administrative; the jurisdictional deadline is the ninety-day period under IRC Section 6213(a) that follows a statutory notice of deficiency.
- Under IRC Section 6201(d), a taxpayer who raises a reasonable dispute over a third-party information return and has cooperated fully shifts the burden of production to the Commissioner.
- IRC Section 6501(e)(1)(A) extends the assessment period from three years to six where omitted gross income exceeds twenty-five percent of the gross income stated on the return — a threshold a proceeds-only match can clear on its own.
- Some CP2000s turn on cost basis, which records answer. Others turn on fair market value at a specific date — staking income, inherited assets, non-cash exchanges — which only a valuation can answer.
- In *Walquist v. Commissioner*, the Tax Court held that machine-generated Section 6662 penalties are exempt from supervisory approval, closing the Section 6751(b) argument for automated notices.

## Part One: The Examination That Has No Examiner

The IRS describes the mechanic without embellishment. It receives information from third parties, and the Automated Underreporter function compares that information against the return to identify potential discrepancies. Where a discrepancy appears, a tax examiner reviews the return, and if it survives that review, a Notice CP2000 issues.

The phrase that matters is "proposal to adjust." Tax Topic 652 states it directly: the CP2000 is not a bill, it is a proposal to adjust income, payments, credits, or deductions. That is not public relations. It describes the notice's legal posture, and it determines how the notice should be answered.

A proposal is answered with evidence, and the notice is built to receive evidence. Its first page carries the proposed changes and the response steps. The comparison section sets out, payer by payer, what the return reported against what the payer reported to the IRS, with the payer's name, identification number, document type, and the taxpayer identification number of the person to whom the document issued. A Response form follows, with an Authorization section permitting a representative to speak for the taxpayer, and channels to return the response by document upload, fax, or mail.

Three consequences follow. First, the notice names the document that triggered the match, so the practitioner need not guess which payer, asset, or year produced the discrepancy. Second, it discloses the IRS's figure but not its reasoning, because there is no reasoning beyond arithmetic. Third, the response is the only place a factual record enters the process at low cost; everything afterward costs more in fees, time, and interest that began accruing on the return's original due date.

## Part Two: The Proceeds-Only Gap in Form 1099-DA

The regime comes from Section 6045 as amended by Section 80603 of the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, implemented in final regulations at T.D. 10000, 89 Fed. Reg. 56480 (July 9, 2024). Those regulations stage the reporting deliberately.

Brokers must report gross proceeds for transactions effected on or after January 1, 2025. Brokers must report basis on certain transactions effected on or after January 1, 2026. Real estate reporting persons must report the fair market value of digital assets paid by buyers and received by sellers where closing occurs on or after January 1, 2026. And once issued, the form reaches the IRS whether or not it reached the taxpayer.

The word doing the work is "certain." Basis reporting attaches only to covered securities, which the 2026 Instructions for Form 1099-DA define narrowly: a digital asset acquired after 2025 for cash, stored-value cards, other digital assets, or property or services, in an account for which the broker provided custodial services, and held in that account until the broker effects the disposition. Every element must be satisfied. Purchase in April 2026 and transfer to self-custody in June 2026 means a September 2026 sale is not a covered disposition.

Everything else is a noncovered security, and for those the broker need not report basis. The instructions name the category: a digital asset the broker did not custody at acquisition, one acquired before 2026, and one transferred in. A broker may voluntarily report basis for a noncovered security and receive relief from information-return penalties — but voluntary is voluntary.

The result is a form that, for a large share of 2025 dispositions, states what the taxpayer received and says nothing about what the taxpayer paid. A position bought for $42,000 and sold for $50,000 has an $8,000 economic gain. The form reports $50,000 of proceeds and no basis. If the return omits the sale, or reports figures that do not tie to the form, the matching engine sees as much as the whole $50,000 as unreported. At a 24 percent rate that is roughly $12,000 of proposed tax on a year realizing $8,000 of gain, before penalty and interest.

Narrower rules soften the edges without changing the shape. Brokers using optional aggregate reporting need not report qualifying stablecoin sales where aggregate gross proceeds do not exceed $10,000, nor specified NFT sales where they do not exceed $600, and processors of digital asset payments receive a $600 threshold of their own. Notice 2024-56 provides penalty relief for 2025 Forms 1099-DA where the broker makes a good-faith effort; Notice 2024-57 defers reporting for certain staking, lending, liquidity-pool, and wrapping activity.

Then there is the asymmetry Congress introduced. The December 2024 regulations extending broker reporting to certain decentralized finance front-ends were nullified by H.J. Res. 25, signed April 10, 2025, and designated Public Law 119-5. Non-custodial front-ends sit outside the reporting net. A taxpayer whose activity moved between a custodial exchange and a decentralized venue may find one leg of a transaction in the IRS's data and the other absent — enough information to generate a notice, not enough to resolve it.

## Part Three: Reading the Notice as a Document

The first act in a CP2000 response is not to gather records. It is to read the notice and write four facts onto a single page.

The notice date comes first. The window runs thirty days from the date on the notice — sixty where the taxpayer lives outside the United States — and not from the postmark, the delivery, or the day the envelope was opened. Where the window cannot be met, the reply channel can be used to request additional time, which the IRS grants in its discretion; a documented request made before the date is a materially different posture from a late response.

The proposed amount and its composition come second. The notice states proposed tax, generally with interest to a date, and may or may not display penalties. A notice showing no penalty has not necessarily waived one; the Service states that certain penalties may apply and may not be shown. A practitioner who treats a clean balance as the ceiling of exposure understates the case.

The comparison line comes third, and it is the theory of the case: which payer, which document type, which amount. It is usually wrong in an identifiable way. The printed response date comes fourth, because correspondence occasionally carries a date different from the thirty-day convention and the printed date governs.

With those recorded, the options are simple. Agree, which requires both spouses' signatures on a joint return and should follow a review of the taxpayer's own records rather than precede it. Or disagree, which requires marking the box and returning a signed statement explaining the disagreement with supporting documentation. Where the notice is correct but other items also need correction, the IRS asks for a Form 1040-X annotated "CP2000," and the Service makes the corrections itself.

One procedural note is routinely missed. The Authorization section is not a power of attorney; it permits contact only. Full representation runs through Form 2848.

## Part Four: Thirty Days Is Administrative; Ninety Days Is Jurisdictional

The most consequential error in the popular literature is conflating two deadlines that sit in different legal universes.

The thirty-day window is administrative — a service standard, printed on a form, managed by a function. It is not a statute of limitations and does not extinguish a right. What it governs is cost. Interest under Section 6601 runs from the original due date of the return, without regard to extensions, so every month the dispute remains open accrues against the taxpayer on the amount ultimately determined.

The ninety-day period is jurisdictional. Section 6212 authorizes a notice of deficiency. Section 6213(a) then bars assessment of a deficiency and any levy or court proceeding until a notice of deficiency is mailed and the ninety-day period — one hundred fifty days where the notice is addressed outside the United States — has expired, or until a Tax Court decision becomes final. Where no petition is filed, Section 6213(c) assesses the deficiency upon expiration.

Two things follow. The first is protective: a taxpayer who ignores a CP2000 does not lose the right to contest before payment. The unanswered proposal matures into a notice of deficiency, and that notice is a ticket into the Tax Court. That right is real, and it is worth stating plainly in a field where fear-based content is common.

The second is expensive. The right is preserved; the position is not. Once the notice of deficiency issues, the taxpayer is litigating rather than corresponding. If no petition is filed within ninety days, assessment occurs under Section 6213(c), and the remaining path runs through payment and a refund claim — Form 1040-X or Form 843, then a refund suit in a district court or the Court of Federal Claims. That path requires paying first.

*Paschall v. Commissioner*, decided June 4, 2026, shows what the far end looks like. The taxpayers first learned of a $33,354 Form 1099-MISC reporting staking income from a Notice CP2000 dated November 13, 2023, having never received the form because it went to a former address. The matter became a deficiency of $24,599 with a $4,920 accuracy-related penalty, then a fully stipulated Tax Court case in which the Commissioner prevailed under Section 61. The lesson is not that the taxpayer should have conceded. It is that a form the taxpayer never saw still generated a notice, still matured into a deficiency, and still required a litigated answer two and a half years later. The thirty-day letter was the inexpensive one.

## Part Five: Two Different CP2000s, and Only One Is About Basis

Here the analysis departs from nearly everything currently published, because the published material treats the CP2000 as a basis-reconstruction problem. For most notices built on 2025 Form 1099-DA data, that is correct: the return reported proceeds and no basis, or omitted the disposition, and the fix is a lot-level ledger and a corrected Form 8949.

There is a second variety, and it generates the larger numbers.

The distinction is between two questions. *What did the taxpayer pay for this asset?* is a records question, answered by exchange exports, wallet histories, acquisition dates, and fees. *What was this asset worth on a particular date?* is a valuation question, and no transaction export answers it. An export proves what a platform recorded. It does not prove what an arm's-length buyer would have paid for the position in the relevant market on the relevant day, and it carries no professional determination, no stated methodology, and no signature.

Three categories of adjustment turn on the second question.

The first is income recognized at receipt. Where digital assets arrive as staking rewards, airdrops, hard-fork distributions, mining proceeds, or compensation, the amount includible is fair market value on the date of receipt, and that same figure becomes basis in the asset received. *Paschall* is exactly this: the dispute was not what the taxpayer paid but what the rewards were worth when they arrived. A challenged figure in that category is answered by a valuation of the asset on a stated date, at a stated time, in a stated market — not by a spreadsheet of purchases.

The second is inherited property. Section 1014(a) sets the basis of property acquired from a decedent at fair market value at the date of death, or at the alternate valuation date elected under Section 2032. That number does double duty: it is the value reported for estate tax purposes and the basis the beneficiary carries into a later sale. A discrepancy between the two — a Form 8971 Schedule A reporting one figure and a Form 8949 another — is not merely a matching problem; Section 6662(b)(8) treats an inconsistent estate basis as its own category of accuracy-related penalty. Only a date-of-death determination reconciles both filings.

The third is the amount realized on a non-cash disposition. Where digital assets are exchanged for other digital assets, property, or services, the amount realized is the fair market value of what was received. A broker's proceeds figure may be right, may embed a fee treatment the taxpayer disputes, or may misattribute a transfer as a sale. Each is a valuation dispute dressed as an arithmetic one.

A fourth category sits adjacent. Where digital assets are contributed to a charitable organization, the deduction is measured by fair market value at the contribution date; above $5,000, Section 170(f)(11)(C) requires a qualified appraisal, and Section 170(f)(11)(E) requires it be attached to the return where the claimed value exceeds $500,000. That question usually surfaces in examination rather than automated matching, because the appraisal travels with the return — but it belongs to the same family.

## Part Six: Section 6201(d) and the Burden of Production

The most useful statutory lever in a CP2000 response appears in almost none of the consumer-facing material.

Section 6201(d) provides that where a taxpayer asserts a reasonable dispute with respect to any item of income reported on an information return filed by a third party, and has fully cooperated with the Secretary, the Secretary bears the burden of producing reasonable and probative information concerning the deficiency in addition to the information return.

Three elements: a reasonable dispute, full cooperation, an information return filed by a third party.

The provision does not hand the taxpayer the case. It shifts the burden of *production* — the obligation to come forward with evidence — not the ultimate burden of persuasion, which ordinarily remains with the taxpayer under Rule 142(a) of the Tax Court Rules and *Welch v. Helvering*. Section 7491(a) offers a broader shift of the burden of proof on a factual issue, but conditions it on the taxpayer's producing credible evidence, substantiating items, maintaining records, and cooperating fully — conditions a bare assertion of disagreement does not satisfy.

What makes the provision practically important is what it does to posture. A letter asserting that the taxpayer disagrees and the numbers are wrong raises no reasonable dispute in any meaningful sense. A submission identifying the specific disposition, stating the correct treatment, and supplying a determination of value prepared under professional standards — with a stated valuation date, a stated methodology, and a signing appraiser — frames the dispute as a documented factual disagreement rather than a protest, and demonstrates the cooperation the statute requires rather than asserting it.

That is the narrow and honest place of the qualified appraisal in an automated-matching dispute. It is not a substitute for records. But where the disputed number is fair market value at a date, it is the only instrument addressing the question the notice actually raises, and it gives Section 6201(d) something to operate on.

## Part Seven: The Limitations Question Nobody Asks

The instinctive answer to a notice arriving years after a return was filed is that the statute of limitations has run. Sometimes it has. Frequently it has not.

Section 6501(a) sets the general assessment period at three years from the later of the date the return was due or the date it was filed. Section 6501(e)(1)(A) extends it to six years where the taxpayer omits from gross income an amount properly includible exceeding twenty-five percent of the gross income stated in the return.

That extension matters most in a proceeds-only world. A taxpayer who omitted a $50,000 disposition entirely, on a return stating $120,000 of gross income, has omitted more than twenty-five percent — and the six-year period applies on the face of the statute, without any finding of bad faith. A taxpayer who reported the disposition and supplied a basis may have omitted nothing, because the omission must be of gross income properly includible, and gain computed with correct basis is not an omission of gross proceeds. The reported-proceeds-with-no-basis taxpayer is therefore in a materially better position than the omitting taxpayer, even though both receive similar notices. Section 6501(c)(1) then removes the limitation period entirely where a false or fraudulent return is filed with intent to evade tax — a provision to discuss candidly, because a client who asks deserves a straight answer.

## Part Eight: Sizing the Penalty, and Disclosing Before It Attaches

Penalty exposure in a digital asset matching case is usually larger than the tax at stake, which is why the notice should be read as a penalty document even when no penalty appears on its face.

Section 6662(a) imposes a twenty percent accuracy-related penalty on the portion of an underpayment attributable to listed conduct. Two categories are in play. Section 6662(b)(2), substantial understatement of income tax, applies where the understatement exceeds the greater of ten percent of the tax required to be shown or $5,000 for an individual, under Section 6662(d)(1)(A). Section 6662(b)(3), substantial valuation misstatement, applies under Section 6662(e)(1)(A) where the value of property or adjusted basis claimed on the return is 150 percent or more of the correct amount, subject to the $5,000 floor in Section 6662(e)(2).

The thresholds are asymmetric between income tax and transfer tax, and the difference catches practitioners working in only one discipline. For estate and gift tax, Section 6662(g)(1) defines a substantial valuation understatement at a claimed value of 65 percent or less of the correct amount — an undervaluation, not an overvaluation — again subject to a $5,000 floor under Section 6662(g)(2). Section 6662(h) doubles the rate to 40 percent for gross valuation misstatements, substituting 200 percent for 150 percent under Section 6662(h)(2)(A) and 40 percent for 65 percent under Section 6662(h)(2)(C).

| Provision | Threshold | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| §6662(b)(2) substantial understatement | Exceeds greater of 10% of tax required to be shown or $5,000 | 20% |
| §6662(e)(1)(A) substantial valuation misstatement (chapter 1) | Value or basis claimed is 150% or more of correct amount | 20% |
| §6662(g)(1) substantial estate or gift valuation understatement | Value claimed is 65% or less of correct amount | 20% |
| §6662(h) gross valuation misstatement | 200% (income) or 40% (estate/gift) substitution | 40% |
| §6662(b)(8) inconsistent estate basis | Basis inconsistent with estate reporting | 20% |

Two defenses exist, and only one can be prepared after the notice arrives.

Section 6664(c)(1) provides that no penalty is imposed where the taxpayer shows reasonable cause and acted in good faith. Treas. Reg. Section 1.6664-4(b)(1) makes that a facts-and-circumstances inquiry, and Section 1.6664-4(c) directs that reliance on professional advice be evaluated by whether the advice was reasonable and the taxpayer acted in good faith in relying on it. A qualified appraisal obtained before the return was filed, in a form a preparer could rely on, is the cleanest reasonable-cause record a digital asset taxpayer can hold. One commissioned after the notice arrives is a different evidentiary object.

The other defense is structural, and it is where disciplined planning pays. Section 6662(d)(2)(B) reduces the understatement by the portion attributable to a position supported by substantial authority or adequately disclosed with the relevant facts affecting its treatment. Reg. Section 1.6662-4 governs disclosure, and Form 8275 is the standard vehicle. A disclosed position supported by a valuation performed under professional standards is a materially different exposure from an undisclosed one — not because disclosure immunizes an incorrect number, but because it converts a penalty question into a valuation question.

One argument should be abandoned, and saying so is part of useful advice. Section 6751(b)(1) requires written supervisory approval before assessment of most penalties, and it is tempting to attack an Automated Underreporter penalty because no supervisor signed off. *Walquist v. Commissioner*, 152 T.C. No. 3 (2019), foreclosed that path: penalties determined by an IRS computer program without human review are "automatically calculated through electronic means" under Section 6751(b)(2)(B), and are therefore exempt from the approval requirement. *Chai v. Commissioner*, 851 F.3d 190 (2d Cir. 2017), does not rescue the argument here.

## Part Nine: The Response File

A CP2000 response is an evidentiary submission on a short clock, and the file supporting it should be assembled in a fixed order, because order is what makes it reviewable by a person holding ninety seconds.

The sequence practitioners describe is consistent. Inventory every wallet, exchange, and custodian used during the year. Match each Form 1099-DA line to a disposition in the taxpayer's own ledger. Rebuild the acquisition lots so every sale carries a documented basis. Prepare Form 8949 as it should have been filed, using the digital asset boxes — box H for short-term dispositions where basis was not reported to the IRS, box K for long-term — with proceeds in column (d) taken from the form and basis in column (e) from the taxpayer's records. Where a broker-reported basis exists and is wrong, the instructions direct that the reported figure go in column (e) with a correction by adjustment. Retain the records.

Then add the layer the matching engine cannot see. For each item in the valuation category, a determination of fair market value on the stated date, developed and reported under USPAP Standards 9 and 10, identifying the asset by contract address and network, stating the valuation date and time zone, naming the price-evidence hierarchy applied and the sources considered and rejected, disclosing any restriction discount applied to a locked or vesting position, and signed by the appraiser. Where the asset is inherited, the same determination should reconcile the estate reporting with the beneficiary's basis. Where it arrived as income, it must fix value at receipt.

Two disciplines govern the submission. First, concede what is true: where a disposition was in fact omitted, say so, state the correct gain, and separate that admission from the items in genuine dispute. A response disputing everything invites a reviewer to disbelieve everything. Second, be complete on first submission. The thirty-day window is a request, not a negotiation, and a partial response consumes it.

| Step | Timing | What the file must contain |
|---|---|---|
| Read and calendar the notice | Day 1–3 | Notice date, printed response date, proposed amount and composition, comparison line, payer and form type |
| Pull the IRS's own data | Day 3–7 | Wage and Income transcript showing every information return on file for the year |
| Reconstruct lots | Day 7–21 | Wallet and exchange inventory, acquisition dates, amounts paid, fees, transfer records |
| Prepare Form 8949 | Day 14–25 | Proceeds per the form, basis per records, boxes H and K, column (f)/(g) adjustments |
| Commission determinations of value | Day 10–30 | Date-specific fair market value for every item in the valuation category, under USPAP Standards 9 and 10 |
| Prepare the signed statement | Day 20–28 | Specific disagreements, correct treatment, evidence, and an express cooperation statement for §6201(d) |
| Transmit and retain | By the printed date | Document upload, fax, or mail; copies only; complete retained set with transmission proof |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a CP2000 notice an audit?

No. A CP2000 is a proposed-changes notice generated by the IRS Automated Underreporter program when third-party information returns do not match a filed return. Tax Topic 652 states that it is not a bill but a proposal to adjust income, payments, credits, or deductions. It becomes consequential only if it goes unanswered, at which point the Service issues a statutory notice of deficiency.

### What happens if I ignore a CP2000 about cryptocurrency?

If the IRS does not hear from the taxpayer by the response date, it issues a statutory notice of deficiency. Under IRC Section 6213(a) no assessment may be made until that notice is mailed and the ninety-day period — one hundred fifty days outside the United States — expires, so the right to contest is preserved. If no petition is filed in the Tax Court within that period, Section 6213(c) assesses the deficiency upon expiration, and interest under Section 6601 has been running from the return's original due date the entire time.

### Why did I receive a CP2000 for a crypto sale I reported correctly?

Because for 2025 dispositions the broker reported gross proceeds and, in most cases, no cost basis. Basis reporting began only for covered securities sold on or after January 1, 2026, and a covered security excludes any asset acquired before 2026 or transferred into the broker's custody. When the matching engine holds a proceeds figure and no basis figure, the difference can appear as unreported income unless the return supplies the basis needed to compute actual gain.

### What is a covered security for Form 1099-DA purposes?

A digital asset acquired after 2025 for cash, stored-value cards, other digital assets, or property or services, in an account for which the broker provided custodial services, and held in that account until the broker effects the disposition. Every element must be satisfied. Assets acquired before 2026, assets the broker did not custody at acquisition, and assets transferred in are noncovered securities, for which basis reporting is voluntary rather than required.

### How long do I have to respond to a CP2000, and can the deadline be extended?

The IRS asks for a response within thirty days of the date printed on the notice, or sixty days where the taxpayer lives outside the United States. The window runs from the notice date, not delivery. Additional time can be requested through the notice's reply channels and is granted in the Service's discretion. The thirty-day window is administrative; the jurisdictional deadline is the ninety-day period following a notice of deficiency.

### Can a qualified appraisal resolve a CP2000?

It can resolve the category in which the disputed number is fair market value at a specific date rather than cost basis — staking rewards and other income recognized at receipt, inherited assets whose basis is fixed under Section 1014, non-cash dispositions, and charitable contributions measured under Section 170. It cannot substitute for records where the dispute is what the taxpayer paid. Where value is the question, a determination prepared under USPAP Standards 9 and 10 raises the documented dispute and demonstrates the cooperation IRC Section 6201(d) requires.

### Does the three-year statute of limitations protect me if the return was filed long ago?

Not always. Section 6501(a) sets a three-year period, but Section 6501(e)(1)(A) extends it to six years where omitted gross income exceeds twenty-five percent of the gross income stated on the return — a threshold an omitted proceeds-only disposition can clear without any finding of bad faith. Section 6501(c)(1) removes the period entirely for a false or fraudulent return filed with intent to evade tax. A taxpayer who reported the disposition and supplied a basis is in a stronger position than one who omitted it.

## Conclusion: The Notice Is a Question About Evidence

The Automated Underreporter program is not an accusation. It is a comparison, run at industrial scale, between what third parties said and what the taxpayer said. In fiscal year 2025 it closed 987,460 cases for $5.9 billion — more taxpayers than the examination function touched, resolved in minutes rather than months, without anyone forming a judgment about the file.

That is precisely why the response matters. A program of that size cannot distinguish a taxpayer who concealed a sale from one who reported honestly without the basis the broker was not yet required to supply. The distinction has to be made by the taxpayer, in writing, within thirty days, with evidence attached.

For most notices built on 2025 Form 1099-DA data, the evidence is a lot-level ledger and a corrected Form 8949 — forensic work, but bounded. For the notices that turn on fair market value at a date, the ledger is not the answer, because the question is not what was paid but what the asset was worth on a stated date in a stated market, and who will sign a determination saying so under professional standards. A defensible number filed before a notice arrives costs a fee. One filed after the ninety-day letter costs a fee, interest from the original due date, and the loss of the cheapest forum the taxpayer will ever have.
