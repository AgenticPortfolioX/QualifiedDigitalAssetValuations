# The Michigan Probate Inventory and Digital Assets: MCL 700.3706 Filing Duties, PC 577 Valuation Standards, and When a Qualified Appraisal Becomes Fiduciary Duty

*Published: September 1, 2026 | By QDAV — Qualified Digital Asset Valuations, Auburn Hills, Michigan*

---

**Executive Summary.** The Michigan probate inventory is the first document in estate administration to put a number on every asset the decedent owned at death — including cryptocurrency, NFTs, and other digital assets. Under MCL 700.3706(1), the personal representative must prepare an inventory within 91 days of appointment, listing property "with reasonable detail" and stating each item's fair market value as of the date of death. For digital assets, that statutory phrase collides with a market that moves by the minute, trades on fragmented venues, and includes tokens with no active market at all. MCL 700.3707 gives the personal representative the authority — and, in practice, the duty — to employ a qualified and disinterested appraiser for property "the value of which may be subject to reasonable doubt." This guide explains what the inventory requires, why nearly every digital asset portfolio meets the reasonable-doubt test, and how a USPAP-compliant, IRS-qualified appraisal protects the estate, the beneficiaries, and the fiduciary personally.

---

## The Statutory Moment: What the Michigan Probate Inventory Is

Every estate administration rests on one foundational question: **what did the decedent own at the moment of death, and what was it worth?** Michigan answers with the inventory. MCL 700.3706(1) of the Estates and Protected Individuals Code (EPIC) provides:

> Within 91 days after appointment or other time specified by court rule, a personal representative, who is not a special personal representative or a successor to another representative who has previously discharged this duty, shall prepare an inventory of property owned by the decedent at the time of death, listing it with reasonable detail, and indicating as to each listed item, its fair market value as of the date of the decedent's death, and the type and amount of an encumbrance that may exist with reference to each listed item.

Four obligations are embedded in that single sentence:

1. **A deadline** — 91 days from appointment (the issuance of Letters of Authority), unless the court orders otherwise.
2. **A completeness standard** — all property owned at death, listed with reasonable detail.
3. **A valuation standard** — fair market value as of the date of death, not the date of preparation, not the date of filing.
4. **A transparency standard** — encumbrances must be disclosed against each item.

The inventory is executed on SCAO form **PC 577, Inventory (Decedent Estate)**, signed under a perjury declaration. MCL 700.3706(2) requires the personal representative to send copies to all presumptive distributees and interested persons who request them; the inventory also feeds the probate inventory fee calculation under MCR 5.307(A).

**Why this matters for digital assets.** The inventory is the point in administration where a wallet with no paper trail, an NFT collection with no brokerage statement, or a DeFi position with no tax form must be converted into a defensible dollar figure. Get it wrong, and the error propagates: distributions, accountings, estate tax returns, and the step-up in basis beneficiaries carry for the rest of their lives.

## Digital Assets Are Personal Property Under EPIC

Whether a probate inventory must include cryptocurrency is not an open question in Michigan. EPIC defines "property" broadly — MCL 700.1106(x) provides that "property" means "anything that may be the subject of ownership, and includes both real and personal property or an interest in real or personal property."

The Internal Revenue Service has treated cryptocurrency as property, not currency, since Notice 2014-21, and the federal definition of "digital asset" in IRC §6045(g)(3)(D) covers any digital representation of value recorded on a cryptographically secured distributed ledger. For Michigan probate purposes, a Bitcoin balance, an Ethereum wallet, a Solana NFT, a stablecoin position, and an interest in a staking or DeFi protocol are all personal property of the estate.

That classification drives everything that follows. Personal property on the PC 577 inventory must be described with enough detail to determine its value, and items of high value must be listed separately. The form's instructions are explicit: *"When listing personal property, provide enough detail to adequately determine the value. Some items should be listed separately and some items should be combined under one category."* A $2 million NFT collection cannot be lumped into "household goods"; it must be identified, described, and valued on its own line — with the appraiser's name and address noted, if an appraisal was obtained.

The practical consequence: **every wallet, exchange account, NFT holding, and token position owned by the decedent at death belongs on the inventory.** Omitting them is not a clerical shortcut; it is an incomplete inventory under MCL 700.3706(1) and exposes the personal representative to liability discussed later in this guide.

## The 91-Day Clock: Deadlines That Do Not Wait

MCL 700.3706(1) gives the personal representative 91 days from appointment. MCR 5.307(A) reinforces the deadline for fee purposes: within 91 days of the Letters of Authority, the personal representative must submit the information necessary for computation of the probate inventory fee.

The 91-day clock is one of the least forgiving deadlines in Michigan probate. Extensions may be granted for good cause, but they are not automatic, and failure to timely file can result in suspension of the personal representative. For an estate with digital assets, 91 days is simultaneously *plenty of time* and *almost no time at all*: plenty of time to obtain a qualified appraisal of a well-documented portfolio, and almost no time at all if the estate is still searching for wallets, waiting on exchanges to respond to Letters of Authority, or reconstructing a decedent who kept no records.

The disciplined approach is to treat the inventory clock and asset discovery as parallel tracks from day one. Engage counsel and, where digital assets are suspected, an appraiser early. A qualified appraiser can value a documented portfolio of liquid assets quickly; the slow part is discovery, and discovery is where estates lose the race.

## The Valuation Standard: Fair Market Value as of the Date of Death

MCL 700.3706(1) fixes the valuation date: fair market value **as of the date of the decedent's death**. This is the statutory phrase, and it is materially different from the approaches estates sometimes take: not the value on the day the inventory is prepared (a Bitcoin price on September 1, 2026 tells the court nothing about an estate whose decedent died in February), not the value on the day the wallet is finally accessed (the valuation date never moves), and not the value at distribution (distribution values are a separate question from the inventory).

For publicly traded securities, date-of-death value is readily available from a brokerage statement or a closing price. For digital assets, the determination is more demanding. Cryptocurrency trades 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, across dozens of venues with different prices, volumes, and liquidity profiles. "The price of Bitcoin on February 14, 2026" is not a single number; it is a range that depends on the reference rate, the exchange, the time zone, and the weighting methodology.

This is precisely why a qualified appraisal — not a screenshot from a price aggregator — is the defensible path. A USPAP-compliant appraiser does not simply look up a price. The appraiser identifies the authoritative reference rate or venue for the specific asset, weighs trading volume and liquidity on the valuation date, applies appropriate discounts or premiums where the asset class requires them, and documents every step so the figure can withstand scrutiny by the court, beneficiaries, and the IRS.

**The volatility problem.** Consider an estate with a significant Bitcoin position. In a single 24-hour period, Bitcoin has historically moved 5–10 percent or more. With a 91-day preparation window, the date-of-death value could differ from the value on the day the inventory is signed by a wide margin. If the personal representative records the wrong date's price — or, worse, the *current* price at preparation — the inventory is inaccurate on its face, beneficiaries may object, and the estate's inventory fee may be wrong.

## MCL 700.3707: The Appraisal Trigger for Digital Assets

MCL 700.3707 authorizes the personal representative to bring in professional help:

> The personal representative may employ a qualified and disinterested appraiser to assist in ascertaining the fair market value as of the date of the decedent's death of property, the value of which may be subject to reasonable doubt. Different persons may be employed to appraise different kinds of property included in the estate. Each appraiser's name and address shall be indicated on the inventory with the item or items he or she appraised.

The statute uses "may," which is permissive on its face. But read against the personal representative's duty of care under MCL 700.3703 — to act as a prudent person would and to be personally liable for losses from breach — the discretion collapses into an obligation wherever the value is genuinely subject to reasonable doubt.

**When is digital asset value "subject to reasonable doubt"?** In practice, nearly always. Consider the categories:

1. **Illiquid or thinly traded tokens.** A token with $50,000 in daily volume across all venues has no reliable market price. Any figure the personal representative writes down is a judgment call — exactly the condition MCL 700.3707 contemplates.
2. **NFTs.** An NFT is unique. Its value depends on comparable sales, collection performance, trait rarity, provenance, and market conditions at the valuation date. There is no "closing price" for a Bored Ape or a CryptoPunk — comparable-sales analysis is the appraiser's craft, and the definition of reasonable doubt.
3. **DeFi and staking positions.** LP tokens, yield-bearing positions, vesting schedules, and lock-ups require analysis beyond a spot price — withdrawal restrictions, impermanent loss, and pending rewards may all affect value.
4. **Large positions in liquid assets.** A position large enough to move the market, or whose liquidation would take days or weeks, may require a size/liquidity adjustment; a retail-priced reference rate does not capture the cost of monetizing a whale-size position.
5. **Assets without any active market.** Tokens delisted from major venues, pre-launch allocations, or abandoned projects may have speculative or nominal value requiring reasoned analysis and documentation.
6. **Wash-sale or manipulated markets.** Certain token markets are subject to wash trading and manipulation. A competent appraiser examines order books and volume quality rather than taking the last print at face value.

For each of these, the personal representative who self-values is guessing on the record, under penalties of perjury, with their own assets at risk.

MCL 700.3707 also expressly allows **different appraisers for different kinds of property**: a real estate appraiser for the house, a business appraiser for the company, and a digital asset appraiser for the crypto. The estate should hire the specialist for each asset class — an appraiser with verifiable education and experience in the digital asset category, not a general business appraiser who has "done a few crypto jobs."

## What "Qualified and Disinterested" Means

MCL 700.3707 uses the phrase "qualified and disinterested appraiser." The phrase is not defined in EPIC, but its meaning is informed by the federal qualified appraisal regime and by professional standards.

**"Qualified" has two layers:**

1. **Michigan probate context.** The appraiser must have the competence to ascertain fair market value of the specific property at issue. For digital assets, that means demonstrated experience with blockchain records, exchange data, NFT marketplaces, and valuation methodology — not merely an opinion that the asset "feels valuable."
2. **IRS context.** For appraisals that will also support federal tax filings — Form 706 estate tax returns, Form 709 gift tax returns, or Form 8283 charitable contribution substantiation — the appraisal must meet the qualified appraisal requirements of IRC §170(f)(11)(E) and the Treasury regulations at 26 CFR §1.170A-17. A qualified appraiser under those rules must have verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property, must declare those qualifications, and must not be excluded from practice before the IRS.

A **USPAP-compliant** appraisal — performed under the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, including Standards 7 and 9 for personal property — is the professional floor for any appraisal presented to a court or the IRS. USPAP governs development (problem identification, scope of work, data analysis) and reporting (the written report's content and structure). Fiduciaries should treat "USPAP Compliant" as a threshold requirement, not a differentiator.

**"Disinterested"** means the appraiser has no financial or personal interest in the outcome — not a beneficiary, creditor, heir, or business partner, and not paid on a contingent basis. A contingent fee creates an incentive to inflate or deflate value. QDAV's practice is fixed-fee engagement letters — the fee is agreed up front and does not vary with the value reported. That is the model fiduciaries should expect.

The PC 577 form requires the appraiser's name and address to appear on the inventory next to the items appraised, and the court may require additional information to support the values stated — so the appraisal report itself should be preserved in the estate file.

## Completing PC 577 for Wallets and NFTs

The mechanics matter. PC 577's instructions and the county courts that administer it expect specific treatment of specific asset classes:

- **Description.** Each asset must be described with enough detail to determine value: identify the asset (e.g., "0.85 BTC — Bitcoin"), the wallet or venue where held (without disclosing account numbers on the form — sensitive identifiers go on SCAO form MC 97 if needed), and the category (e.g., "cryptocurrency," "NFT collection").
- **Separate listing for high-value items.** The form instructs that items of high value — the examples are automobiles, jewelry, antiques, stocks and bonds, and "any other individual item of high value" — must be listed separately. An NFT collection worth six or seven figures is such an item; even a single high-value NFT should have its own line. Small, immaterial balances may be grouped in a single line item.
- **Appraiser attribution.** Where value is determined by appraisal, the appraiser's name and address are noted on the inventory with the item appraised (MCL 700.3707; PC 577 instructions). The appraisal report itself should be retained.
- **Encumbrances.** If a digital asset position is pledged, lent, or subject to a security interest, the nature and amount of the encumbrance must be shown. For personal property, the lien is not deducted from gross value on the form — unlike real property, where certain liens reduce the inventory value.
- **No account numbers on the form.** The inventory is served on beneficiaries and interested persons. Private keys, seed phrases, and wallet addresses that would compromise security must never appear on the inventory. Use MC 97 where identifiers are necessary.

**The discovery problem.** A personal representative cannot inventory what they cannot find. The Michigan Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (UFADAA), MCL 700.1001–700.1018 (Act 59 of 2016), gives fiduciaries statutory tools to obtain access to digital accounts, including the power to direct custodians to provide records and to terminate accounts. MCL 700.1015 applies the fiduciary duties of care, loyalty, and confidentiality to digital assets. For estate administration, UFADAA is the bridge between "we think he had crypto" and "here is the wallet, now value it." A complete inventory depends on a complete discovery process: a search of email and password managers, a review of tax records (Forms 1099-DA, exchange statements, prior Schedule D filings), queries to known exchanges with Letters of Authority, and, where the decedent planned ahead, the records compiled in a digital asset estate plan.

## MCL 700.3708: Supplemental Inventory When New Assets Surface

Estate administration rarely runs in a straight line, and digital assets make surprise discoveries common. MCL 700.3708 imposes a continuing duty: if property not included in the original inventory comes to the knowledge of a personal representative, or if the representative learns that the value or description of an item is erroneous or misleading, the representative "shall make a supplementary inventory or appraisal showing the market value as of the date of the decedent's death of the new item or the revised market value or description, and showing the appraiser or other data relied upon, if any."

The triggers are broad: a wallet discovered after filing (common when exchanges respond slowly or a password manager is cracked late); an original value found erroneous or misleading (a wrong date's price, an incomplete comparable set); or a token position omitted because it was held on an obscure platform.

The obligation is to make a **supplementary inventory or appraisal** showing the date-of-death market value of the new item or the revised value, with the appraiser or other data relied upon, and to furnish copies to interested persons and file with the court where the original was filed. The supplemental inventory also feeds the probate inventory fee recalculation under MCR 5.307.

Two practical points. First, the supplemental inventory is not a penalty — it is the statutory mechanism for correcting the record, and using it promptly is the responsible course. Second, an estate that engaged a qualified appraiser for the original inventory should return to that appraiser for the supplement: the file already contains the methodology, reference-rate selection, and date-of-death analysis, so the supplement is fast, consistent, and defensible.

## Probate Inventory Fees: Why Accuracy Is Priced

Michigan probate courts compute the inventory fee on the value of the estate's property. Under MCR 5.307(A), the personal representative must submit the information necessary for computation of the probate inventory fee within 91 days of the Letters of Authority, and the fee must be paid no later than the filing of the petition for complete estate settlement, the settlement order petition, or the sworn statement closing administration — or one year after appointment, whichever is earlier.

The fee is a percentage of the stated inventory value, creating a direct, mechanical connection between accuracy and fees: an undervalued inventory underpays the fee, an overvalued one overpays and distorts beneficiary expectations. The inventory fee is one more reason the stated figures should be right the first time — fair market value as of the date of death, not a guess.

## Fiduciary Liability: The Personal Cost of a Bad Inventory

The most important paragraph in this guide: **the personal representative can be personally liable for an inaccurate inventory.** MCL 700.3703(5) provides that a personal representative is liable to interested persons "for damage or loss resulting from breach of fiduciary duty to the same extent as a trustee of an express trust." Michigan courts enforce that exposure through **surcharge** — a court order requiring the fiduciary to restore losses to the estate from personal funds. Surcharge covers the classic failures: failing to preserve assets, self-dealing, imprudent management, and — directly relevant here — failing to identify and inventory estate assets or providing erroneous valuations.

The liability theories that arise from digital asset inventories are specific and foreseeable:

1. **Omitted assets.** A personal representative who files an inventory that omits a discovered wallet, or who fails to pursue known exchange accounts, can be surcharged for the value that should have been captured for the estate. "I didn't know how to access it" is not a defense when the fiduciary had the authority and the duty to pursue it — including under UFADAA.
2. **Valuation errors.** An inventory value that is not defensible — a wrong date's price, a screenshot instead of an analysis, a self-serving low number — can support a surcharge where beneficiaries relied on it to their detriment. If the personal representative later distributes at the understated value, the conflict is compounded.
3. **Delay losses.** Digital assets are volatile. A personal representative who dawdles on discovery, waits months to engage an appraiser, or holds a position through a market collapse without a reasoned decision can be surcharged for the resulting loss where a prudent fiduciary would have acted.
4. **Self-dealing red flags.** A fiduciary who values an asset low and then buys it from the estate, or who directs it to a favored beneficiary at an undervalued figure, faces the most severe exposure — surcharge plus removal, and possible sanctions.

The protection is not luck; it is process. A fiduciary who (a) conducts diligent discovery, (b) obtains a qualified, disinterested, USPAP-compliant appraisal for any asset whose value is subject to reasonable doubt, (c) documents every decision, and (d) files a timely, accurate inventory is in the strongest possible position. The appraisal is, in effect, professional-liability insurance for the fiduciary — and for the estate attorney who advised the filing: the fee is a rounding error next to the exposure it eliminates.

## The 2026 Federal Context: $15 Million, No Michigan Estate Tax, and the Step-Up

Three 2026 developments frame why probate inventory accuracy matters even for estates that will never file a federal estate tax return:

**1. The federal exemption is now $15 million — and permanent.** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, raised the federal estate and gift tax exemption to $15,000,000 per individual and repealed the scheduled 2026 sunset, making the higher exemption permanent with inflation adjustments beginning in 2027. For a 2026 death, Form 706 is required only when the gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts exceeds $15 million (or when the estate elects portability). The practical effect: the vast majority of Michigan estates will never owe federal estate tax, and many will never file Form 706 at all.

**2. Michigan has no state estate tax.** Michigan imposes no state estate or inheritance tax for decedents dying after September 30, 1993; the old inheritance tax applies only to pre-October 1993 deaths.

**The trap:** because no tax return is due, the probate inventory is often the **only** valuation document the estate ever produces. The inventory value becomes the de facto official value of the estate — the number used for distribution, for the step-up in basis, and for any future dispute. An estate that skips the appraisal because "we're under the exemption" does not escape valuation; it simply locks in an undocumented number.

**3. The step-up in basis is permanent and powerful.** Under IRC §1014, property acquired from a decedent receives a basis equal to its fair market value at the date of death — wiping out the decedent's entire unrealized gain. For digital assets, the beneficiary's cost basis resets to the date-of-death value, so a later sale is taxed only on post-death appreciation.

The step-up is **only as good as the documentation.** If the estate's inventory shows Bitcoin at the date-of-death fair market value, the beneficiary has a documented basis. If the inventory is vague, the beneficiary may face an IRS challenge with nothing but a screenshot — and in the absence of adequate basis documentation, the IRS may treat the recipient's basis as zero, converting a tax-free step-up into a fully taxable disposition. A qualified appraisal that fixes the date-of-death value is the documentation that makes IRC §1014 work. Estates should keep the appraisal report with the estate file and pass a copy to each beneficiary receiving digital assets.

## Working With a Qualified Digital Asset Appraiser: A Fiduciary's Checklist

The engagement should be deliberate. The following checklist reflects the standards QDAV applies and the documentation a court or the IRS will expect:

**Before engaging:**
- [ ] Verify qualifications: education, training, and experience in digital asset valuation; IRS-qualified status where the appraisal supports a federal filing; USPAP compliance.
- [ ] Verify independence: no family, business, or beneficiary relationship; fixed-fee engagement, not contingent on value; professional liability coverage.

**At engagement:**
- [ ] Provide the complete asset list: wallets, exchanges, NFTs, DeFi positions, staking records, and purchase records for provenance and cost data.
- [ ] Confirm the date of death and valuation date; authorize verification of on-chain and exchange data from primary sources, not family screenshots.
- [ ] Agree on the scope of work and report format in writing.

**On delivery:**
- [ ] The written appraisal should state the asset(s), the valuation date, the methodology (e.g., market approach with comparable sales; reference-rate selection with liquidity analysis), the data relied upon, and the appraiser's qualifications.
- [ ] Record the appraiser's name and address on PC 577 next to the appraised items; retain the appraisal in the estate file and provide copies to beneficiaries receiving digital assets (for their basis records).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**1. What is a Michigan probate inventory, and who must file it?**
The statutory list of all property the decedent owned at death, with fair market values as of the date of death, prepared on SCAO form PC 577 by the personal representative under MCL 700.3706, served on interested persons and supporting the probate inventory fee.

**2. When is the Michigan probate inventory due?**
Within 91 days of the personal representative's appointment (Letters of Authority), unless the court orders otherwise — MCL 700.3706(1); MCR 5.307(A).

**3. Do digital assets have to be listed on the Michigan probate inventory?**
Yes. Digital assets are personal property under EPIC's broad definition of property (MCL 700.1106(x)), and the inventory must list all property owned at death with reasonable detail. Cryptocurrency, NFTs, tokenized assets, and DeFi positions held by the decedent belong on the inventory.

**4. How are cryptocurrencies valued for a Michigan probate inventory?**
At fair market value as of the date of death. For liquid assets, this means an authoritative reference rate for the specific date; for illiquid assets, NFTs, and large positions, it requires professional analysis — comparable sales, liquidity and size adjustments, and documented methodology — which is why MCL 700.3707 authorizes a qualified and disinterested appraiser.
**5. When does MCL 700.3707 require a qualified appraiser?**
The statute authorizes a qualified and disinterested appraiser for property "the value of which may be subject to reasonable doubt." Digital assets — especially NFTs, thinly traded tokens, DeFi positions, and large holdings — almost always meet that test, and the fiduciary duty of care under MCL 700.3703 makes the appraisal the prudent course.

**6. What does "qualified and disinterested appraiser" mean under Michigan law?**
"Qualified" means verifiable competence in valuing the specific property — for digital assets, demonstrated blockchain and valuation expertise, and IRS-qualified status where federal filings are involved. "Disinterested" means no financial or personal interest in the outcome and no contingent fee.

**7. What happens if new crypto wallets are discovered after the inventory is filed?**
MCL 700.3708 requires a supplementary inventory or appraisal showing the date-of-death market value of the newly discovered item or corrected value, with the appraiser or data relied upon, served on interested persons and filed with the court.

**8. Can a personal representative be personally liable for an inaccurate inventory?**
Yes. Under MCL 700.3703(5), a personal representative is liable to interested persons for damage or loss from breach of fiduciary duty to the same extent as a trustee of an express trust. Omitted digital assets, indefensible valuations, and imprudent delay can support surcharge.

**9. Does Michigan have a state estate tax?**
No. Michigan imposes no state estate tax or inheritance tax for decedents dying after September 30, 1993; the only death tax a Michigan estate can face is federal.

**10. What is the 2026 federal estate tax exemption?**
$15,000,000 per individual, made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 2025), with inflation adjustments beginning in 2027. Form 706 is required when the gross estate exceeds that amount or when portability is elected.

**11. Do beneficiaries get a step-up in basis on inherited crypto?**
Yes. Under IRC §1014, inherited digital assets take a basis equal to fair market value as of the date of death. The step-up is only as good as the documentation — a qualified appraisal at the date-of-death value preserves it.

**12. How does the Michigan Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act help?**
The Michigan UFADAA (MCL 700.1001–700.1018) gives personal representatives statutory tools to access the decedent's digital accounts and records — the discovery layer that makes a complete inventory possible. The qualified appraisal is the valuation layer.
