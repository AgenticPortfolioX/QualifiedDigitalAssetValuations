# Digital Assets in Donor-Advised Funds and Charitable Remainder Trusts: Valuation at Contribution, Form 8283 Substantiation, Form 8282 Disposition Reporting, and the Section 6664(c)(3) Reasonable-Cause Rule

*Published: September 18, 2026 | By Qualified Digital Asset Valuations (QDAV)*

---

## Introduction: The Question Donors Ask and the Question the Service Asks

A client holding a concentrated digital asset position decides to give. The conversation usually turns to the vehicle: direct to the operating charity, into a donor-advised fund so granting decisions can be spread across years, or into a charitable remainder trust that pays an income stream for a term and leaves the remainder to charity? Each has a real answer, and the answers differ in consequential ways.

Then the accountant asks the question that determines whether the deduction survives: **how is the contributed asset valued on the contribution date, and who signs the report?**

Under IRS Section 170, those questions resolve into one requirement. The vehicle changes the donee, the percentage-of-income ceiling, the annual administrative burden, and the party that must report a later disposition to the Service. It does not change the appraisal requirement. A contribution of digital assets with a claimed value above $5,000 requires a Qualified Appraisal meeting the standards of Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17 — whether the recipient is a food bank, a donor-advised fund sponsor, or a charitable remainder unitrust.

## Key Takeaways

- **The appraisal requirement follows the contribution, not the vehicle.** Cryptocurrency is not a publicly traded security under §170(f)(11)(C), so the $5,000 Qualified Appraisal threshold applies without exception — CCA 202302012 confirms it even where an exchange price is available.
- **A donor-advised fund sponsor is the donee.** The sponsor owns the asset, retains control, and must sign Form 8283 Section B, Part V. The donor holds only advisory privileges.
- **A charitable remainder unitrust must be revalued annually** under §664(d)(2), making the appraisal a recurring trust obligation rather than a one-time filing exhibit.
- **§6664(c)(3) converts the appraisal into a penalty defence.** For charitable deduction property, the reasonable-cause exception to the §6662 accuracy penalty is unavailable unless the claimed value was based on a Qualified Appraisal by a qualified appraiser and the donor made a good-faith investigation of value.
- **Form 8282 creates an automatic cross-check.** A donee that disposes of charitable deduction property within three years must report the disposition and the amount received to the Service and to the donor.

## Part One: The 2026 Deduction Framework for Appreciated Digital Assets

**The 0.5%-of-AGI floor.** Under IRC §170(b)(1)(I), an individual who itemizes may deduct charitable contributions only to the extent they exceed 0.5% of adjusted gross income. The floor applies to cash and non-cash contributions alike and is applied before the percentage ceilings are calculated — the statute preserves the full contribution in the numerator of the ceiling computation, so the floor reduces the deduction rather than the ceiling. On a $2,000,000 AGI, the first $10,000 of giving produces no itemized benefit, which is the strongest argument for concentrating gifts into appreciable, well-documented assets rather than spreading small cash contributions across the year.

**The 30% ceiling that applies here.** Cash to public charities sits at 60% of AGI and is now permanent, while appreciated long-term capital gain property contributed to a public charity — including a donor-advised fund sponsor — sits at 30%. The same property contributed to a private foundation sits at 20%, and unused amounts carry forward five years. Because the ceiling applies to the appreciated asset, the valuation conclusion controls not only the deduction but how much ceiling capacity the gift consumes.

## Part Two: The Documentation Ladder — and Why It Does Not Move With the Vehicle

The substantiation requirements for a non-cash contribution are cumulative. Each threshold adds a layer; no layer is removed by choosing a different recipient.

| Tier | Requirement | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Any single contribution of $250 or more | Contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the donee | IRC §170(f)(8) |
| Total non-cash contributions above $500 | Form 8283 attached to the return | IRC §170(f)(11)(B) |
| Any single item, or group of similar items, claimed above $5,000 | Qualified Appraisal by a qualified appraiser, plus Form 8283 Section B, plus the donee's signature | IRC §170(f)(11)(C); Reg. §1.170A-17 |
| Claimed deduction above $500,000 | The complete appraisal must be **attached to the return** | IRC §170(f)(11)(D) |

**The $5,000 threshold applies by item or group of similar items, not by donee.** §170(f)(11)(F) requires that property and all similar items donated to one or more donees be treated as a single property for threshold purposes. A donor who contributes $3,000 of one token to a donor-advised fund and $3,000 of the same token to a university in the same year has contributed $6,000 of similar items, and Section B requirements attach even though neither gift individually crossed the line.

**Digital assets enjoy no exception.** The Code exempts certain readily valued property — cash, inventory, publicly traded securities, intellectual property, and specified vehicles — from the qualified appraisal requirement. In CCA 202302012 the Office of Chief Counsel concluded that cryptocurrency is not a publicly traded security, that no exception is available, and that the appraisal requirement applies notwithstanding the ready availability of exchange prices. The same boundary appears in the penalty statute from the other direction: under §6664(c)(4)(A), "charitable deduction property" includes all property contributed for which a Section 170 deduction is claimed, **excluding only securities for which market quotations are readily available on an established securities market**. Digital assets remain charitable deduction property. There is no route around the appraisal by pointing at a price feed.

**Content and timing are exacting.** Reg. §1.170A-17 requires the report to describe the property precisely enough that an unfamiliar reader could identify it; to state the valuation effective date and the Fair Market Value on that date; to disclose the terms of any agreement relating to the use, sale, or disposition of the property; to set out the appraiser's qualifications, signature, and statutory declaration; and to state the method of valuation and the specific basis for the conclusion. Under Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(4) the report must be signed and dated **no earlier than 60 days before the contribution** and no later than the return due date, including extensions. In *Pankratz v. Commissioner*, T.C. Memo. 2021-26, the court denied deductions where no appraisal was obtained. Independence is a separate condition: under §1.170A-17(b)(5) the donor, the donee, related parties, and any appraiser paid a percentage of appraised value are disqualified.

**Nothing in the ladder refers to the identity of the recipient.** The vehicle is downstream of the appraisal, not upstream of it.

## Part Three: The Donor-Advised Fund — Sponsorship, Signature, and the Liquidation Problem

A donor-advised fund is defined in §4966(d)(2) as a fund or account separately identified by reference to contributions made by a donor, owned and controlled by a sponsoring organisation, and with respect to which the donor — or a person appointed by the donor — has or reasonably expects to have advisory privileges over the distribution of assets.

**The sponsor is the donee.** The contribution is a completed gift to a public charity. The donor retains the privilege of recommending grants, but the privilege is advisory; the sponsor is not obligated to follow a recommendation, and the assets cease to be the donor's property on the contribution date. Because the sponsor is the donee, the sponsor's signature is required on Form 8283 Section B, Part V for any contribution above $5,000. Where Section B is prepared after the fact and the acknowledgment cannot be obtained, the deduction is exposed — the donee's signature is a required component of the substantiation, not an administrative formality.

**The contribution date governs the valuation date, not the confirmation date.** A transfer frequently runs through an intermediary: the donor sends from a wallet to an address controlled by the sponsor or by a digital asset donation processor acting for the sponsor. The valuation effective date is the date the contribution is complete. Where a donor captures a price on Monday, initiates the transfer on Tuesday, and receives confirmation on Thursday, three candidate dates exist in the file, and the appraisal must be built on the correct one.

**Sponsors liquidate.** Standard sponsor practice is to sell contributed cryptocurrency promptly — frequently on receipt, occasionally within hours — because the sponsor's investment mandate and custody posture are not designed to hold volatile assets. The prompt sale is how the sponsor converts an illiquid gift into grantable funds, and it produces a documentation effect donors do not anticipate: the sale is a **disposition of charitable deduction property**, and a donee that disposes of such property within three years of receipt must file Form 8282 and furnish a copy to the donor, reporting the donor's identity, the property, the date of receipt, the date of disposition, and **the amount received**.

This is the DAF-specific valuation problem, and it has nothing to do with the size of the deduction. It has to do with whether the claimed number can be explained. Consider a position large relative to daily volume on the venue the sponsor uses. The appraiser concludes a Fair Market Value at the contribution date; the sponsor liquidates across a session; realized proceeds come in modestly below the claimed figure because the sale itself moved the market. Neither number is wrong. They measure different things — a value conclusion at an observation date versus proceeds from an execution — and the difference is exactly what a contemporaneous appraisal documents through its pricing convention, venue selection, observation window, and any liquidity or depth adjustment. An appraisal prepared before or alongside the contribution explains that difference in advance; a valuation reconstructed eighteen months later cannot.

**Governance risk under §4966 and §4967.** A taxable distribution from a donor-advised fund — a distribution to a natural person, or for a purpose other than a charitable purpose — carries a 20% excise tax under §4966(a)(1); a prohibited benefit, meaning an agreement by the sponsor that a person will receive a benefit more than incidental, carries a 125% excise tax under §4967(a)(1). Advisory privileges are not control, and a structure designed so that a donor's family or business benefits from the pattern of grants has moved from planning into exposure.

## Part Four: The Charitable Remainder Trust — Annual Revaluation as a Recurring Appraisal Obligation

A charitable remainder trust is a split-interest trust: an income interest payable to non-charitable beneficiaries for a term or a life, and a remainder interest that must pass to charity. A charitable remainder annuity trust under §664(d)(1) pays a fixed annuity amount equal to 5% to 50% of the initial Fair Market Value of trust assets, and that amount never changes. A charitable remainder unitrust under §664(d)(2) pays a fixed percentage, also between 5% and 50%, of the Fair Market Value of trust assets **determined annually**. Both must satisfy a minimum remainder test: the present value of the remainder interest, computed at funding using the §7520 rate, must be at least 10% of the Fair Market Value of the contributed property.

For a volatile asset that distinction is decisive. A CRAT's annuity is fixed at funding, so the trust must fund a constant payment from a fluctuating base and a severe drawdown can force sales into weakness. A CRUT pays a percentage of whatever the corpus is worth on the valuation date, so the payment moves with the asset — and that flexibility is purchased with a recurring obligation. Under §664(d)(2) and the regulations implementing the unitrust amount, including Reg. §1.664-3(a)(1)(i)(a), the trustee must determine the Fair Market Value of the trust's assets each year. Where those assets are digital, that annual determination is a valuation engagement performed for a purpose that is neither the deduction nor the estate tax return: it exists so the trustee can calculate the amount payable to the income beneficiary.

Three consequences follow.

**The engagement is recurring.** A contribution-date appraisal supports the donor's §170 deduction. It does not support the year-three, year-four, and year-five unitrust calculations. Each valuation date requires its own determination, with its own observation window, market data, and documentation.

**The deduction is only the remainder.** The deduction is the present value of the remainder interest, not the full value of the contributed asset, and that figure depends on the Fair Market Value of the contributed property at funding, the payout rate, the term, and the §7520 rate. An understated valuation understates the deduction as surely as an overstated valuation invites examination, and against the 30%-of-AGI ceiling a low valuation can leave capacity unused that the donor may not recover within the five-year carryover.

**The trust is subject to a different tax regime.** Under §664(c)(1) a charitable remainder trust is exempt from income taxation; under §664(c)(2) it is subject to a **100% excise tax on unrelated business taxable income**. For digital assets that exposure is not hypothetical: it arises where the trust receives income from a trade or business or from debt-financed property, including partnership interests carrying recourse debt, mining operations, and certain staking or validator arrangements routed through an operating entity. A portfolio that looks like a passive holding in one structure can look like a business in another, and the excise tax is total rather than graduated.

**Self-dealing is the second regime constraint.** Under §4947(a)(2), split-interest trusts are subject to the private foundation self-dealing rules of §4941. The donor, the donor's family, and the trustee are disqualified persons, and §4941(d)(1)(E) treats use of trust income or assets for the benefit of a disqualified person as self-dealing. A related risk is the prearranged sale: where the donor is already committed to selling, or the donee can be compelled to sell under an arrangement put in place at funding, the gain may be attributed to the donor rather than the trust. The contribution must be unconditional and complete before any sale obligation attaches.

## Part Five: The Vehicle Comparison

| Dimension | Direct gift to operating charity | Donor-advised fund | Charitable remainder trust |
|---|---|---|---|
| Donee | The charity | The sponsor | The trust |
| Who signs Form 8283 Section B, Part V | The charity | The sponsor | The trustee |
| AGI ceiling — appreciated long-term digital assets | 30% | 30% | 30% of the remainder value, subject to the same capital gain limit |
| Deduction measure | Full Fair Market Value at contribution | Full Fair Market Value at contribution | Present value of the remainder interest |
| Qualified Appraisal above $5,000 | Required | Required | Required |
| Appraisal attached to return above $500,000 | Required | Required | Required |
| Annual valuation obligation after funding | None | None (sponsor's investment responsibility) | Required — annual determination of trust asset Fair Market Value under §664(d)(2) |
| Typical liquidation timing by the donee | Varies; often soon after receipt | Prompt; frequently on or near receipt | Trustee's discretion, subject to self-dealing constraints |
| Form 8282 exposure | Yes, if disposed within three years | Yes, and typically in the contribution year | Yes, and typically in the contribution year |
| Excise tax regimes | §4958 if applicable | §4966 taxable distributions; §4967 prohibited benefits | §664(c)(2) 100% excise tax on UBTI; §4941 self-dealing |
| Donor retains control of investment or grants | No | Advisory privileges only | No |

Every row in the documentation block — the Qualified Appraisal, the Section B signature, the $500,000 attachment — is identical across all three columns; the differentiators are all downstream. Both the DAF and the CRT also create a post-contribution valuation event a direct gift does not: the sponsor's prompt liquidation in one case, the annual unitrust valuation in the other.

## Part Six: Section 6664(c)(3) — Where the Appraisal Stops Being Paperwork

If one provision explains why professional valuation of a contributed digital asset is not discretionary, it is IRC §6664(c)(3).

**Be aware of the penalty the appraisal sits beside.** Under §6662(b)(3) an accuracy-related penalty of 20% applies to the portion of an underpayment attributable to a substantial valuation misstatement under chapter 1. Under §6662(e)(1)(A) a substantial valuation misstatement exists where the value claimed on a return is **150% or more** of the correct value; under §6662(h) a gross valuation misstatement — the 40% tier — uses a substituted threshold of **200%**; and under §6662(e)(2) no such penalty is imposed unless the portion of the underpayment exceeds **$5,000**. The general defence is the reasonable-cause exception in §6664(c)(1): no penalty where the taxpayer shows reasonable cause and acts in good faith.

Then comes the carve-out. Under §6664(c)(3), in the case of an underpayment attributable to a substantial or gross valuation overstatement with respect to **charitable deduction property**, §6664(c)(1) **does not apply**. The reasonable-cause exception is switched off for overstated charitable deductions, and switched back on only where two conditions are both satisfied: the claimed value of the property was based on a **Qualified Appraisal made by a qualified appraiser**, and, in addition to obtaining that appraisal, the taxpayer made a **good-faith investigation of the value** of the contributed property. The definitions reinforce the point: under §6664(c)(4)(B) and (C) those terms take their meanings from §170(f)(11)(E)(i) and (ii), and under §6664(c)(4)(A) charitable deduction property excludes only securities for which market quotations are readily available on an established securities market — the exclusion digital assets do not fall within.

Read together, §6664(c)(3) tells a donor, in the text of the Internal Revenue Code, that a Qualified Appraisal by a qualified appraiser is the statutory condition on which penalty relief depends. Absent the appraisal, the reasonable-cause defence is unavailable as a matter of law — not merely weak on the facts, and not merely a question of persuading an examiner.

The second condition deserves equal attention, because it is the one donors most often satisfy accidentally and appraisers most often fail to document. The file should show engagement with the conclusion rather than mere receipt of it: the basis on which the appraiser was selected, the information the donor or the donor's advisor supplied, and the reconciliation of the conclusion with any observable market data the donor already held. In practice this is best documented at the time of the appraisal, in correspondence evidencing a deliberate valuation process rather than a document procured at filing.

Two collateral exposures complete the picture. Under §6695A an appraiser faces a penalty where a misstatement of contributed property is attributable to the appraisal: the greater of $1,000 or 10% of the underpayment, capped at 125% of the fee.

## Part Seven: Form 8282 and the Three-Year Disposition Lookback

A donee — or a successor donee — must file Form 8282 when it sells, exchanges, consumes, or otherwise disposes of charitable deduction property (or a portion of it) **within three years** after the date the original donee received it. Filing is due **125 days** after the disposition, and the donee must send a copy to the donor. Failure to file carries a penalty generally of **$50 per form**. The information reported includes the donor's name and identifying number, a description of the property, the date of receipt, the date of disposition, and the amount received.

That last item is the operative one. Form 8283 Section B reports the claimed Fair Market Value; Form 8282 reports what the property actually fetched. Both sit in the Service's files, relate to the same property, and within the three-year window are linked by the donor's identifying number. For digital assets the window will almost always be live: a DAF sponsor selling on receipt generates Form 8282 in the contribution year, and a unitrust trustee realizing assets to fund the first payment generates it within a year. The donor need do nothing, but the file will eventually contain a proceeds figure comparable to the claimed value.

This is not a reason to avoid a professional appraisal. It is the reason to require one. Where the claimed value and the realized proceeds are close, the appraisal is corroborated; where they differ, the difference must be explained by something in the record, and the explanation almost always lies in the valuation methodology — the pricing convention applied, the venues considered, the observation window used, the treatment of volume and depth, and the distinction between a value conclusion at a point in time and proceeds from a specific execution. An appraisal that states its pricing convention and supports any liquidity or depth adjustment answers the Form 8282 before it arrives. Because the form is furnished to the donor, it should be filed with the contribution-year substantiation package, not treated as correspondence.

## Part Eight: A Dated Sequencing Checklist for Attorneys and CPAs

Most documentary failures in digital asset charitable giving are sequencing failures. The following order implements the statutory requirements rather than reacting to them.

1. **Confirm the asset, then the vehicle.** Identify the token, quantity, custody arrangement, and holding period, then confirm the intended recipient will accept it. Holding period matters, because property not held for the long-term capital gain period is subject to the ordinary income property reduction, which can limit the deduction to adjusted basis.
2. **Confirm the recipient's documentation capacity.** For a DAF, confirm the sponsor will accept the asset and identify the office that signs Form 8283 Section B, Part V. For a trust, confirm the trustee's capability; for a direct gift, that the charity has a signatory process.
3. **Engage the appraiser before the transfer.** The signature date must fall no earlier than 60 days before the contribution and no later than the return due date including extensions; engaging after the transfer makes contemporaneous market data more expensive to source.
4. **Define the valuation effective date and observation window in advance**, agreeing which date constitutes the contribution under the applicable transfer mechanics and how value at that date and time will be observed.
5. **Execute the transfer and preserve the record** — sending transaction, on-chain identifiers, receipts from any intermediary, and any contemporaneous price capture; where an intermediary is involved, obtain written confirmation of the date the donee took control.
6. **Obtain the §170(f)(8) contemporaneous written acknowledgment** for any single contribution of $250 or more, by the earlier of the return filing date or the due date including extensions.
7. **Complete Form 8283 Section B** — Part I property description, Part III donor statement, Part V donee acknowledgment. For digital assets, the revised form carries a discrete digital assets property type. Do not finalise the return until Part V is signed.
8. **Attach the appraisal where the claimed deduction exceeds $500,000** — an attachment obligation, not merely an obtain obligation.
9. **Document the good-faith investigation of value** — appraiser selection rationale, information package provided, and correspondence evidencing the donor's engagement with the conclusion.
10. **For a unitrust, calendar the annual valuation date**, setting the first valuation engagement before the first unitrust calculation and aligning the valuation date, payment date, and trust taxable year under the governing instrument.
11. **When Form 8282 arrives, file it with the substantiation package** and compare reported proceeds to the claimed Fair Market Value, documenting any variance contemporaneously rather than reconstructively.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do you need a Qualified Appraisal to donate crypto to a donor-advised fund?

Yes, where the claimed deduction exceeds $5,000. The requirement under IRC §170(f)(11)(C) attaches to the contribution of property other than cash or publicly traded securities. Cryptocurrency is not a publicly traded security, as the Office of Chief Counsel concluded in CCA 202302012, so it applies whether the recipient is an operating charity, a donor-advised fund sponsor, or a charitable remainder trust.

### Who signs Form 8283 Section B when the gift goes to a donor-advised fund?

The sponsor signs. A donor-advised fund sponsor is the legal owner of the fund and the donee of the contribution, so the sponsor provides the Part V acknowledgment. The donor retains advisory privileges over grants but is not the party acknowledging receipt for substantiation purposes.

### Can a charitable remainder trust hold cryptocurrency?

Yes. Digital assets may fund either a charitable remainder annuity trust under §664(d)(1) or a charitable remainder unitrust under §664(d)(2). The practical constraints are the trustee's administrative capability, the liquidity needed to satisfy the payment obligation, exposure to the 100% excise tax on unrelated business taxable income under §664(c)(2), and the §4941 self-dealing rules.

### What is IRC §6664(c)(3) and why does it matter to digital asset donors?

It provides that the reasonable-cause exception in §6664(c)(1) does not apply to an underpayment attributable to a substantial or gross valuation overstatement with respect to charitable deduction property — unless the claimed value was based on a Qualified Appraisal made by a qualified appraiser and the taxpayer made a good-faith investigation of the value of the contributed property. For a donor claiming a deduction above $5,000, the Qualified Appraisal is therefore the statutory condition on which penalty relief depends.

### What is Form 8282 and what happens when a charity sells donated crypto?

Form 8282 is the donee information return. A donee that disposes of charitable deduction property within three years of receiving it must report the disposition, including the amount received, within 125 days, and must send a copy to the donor. Because DAF sponsors liquidate digital assets promptly and unitrust trustees realize assets to fund payments, the form is commonly generated in the contribution year.

### What AGI limit applies to a donation of appreciated cryptocurrency in 2026?

Appreciated long-term capital gain property contributed to a public charity or donor-advised fund sponsor is deductible up to 30% of adjusted gross income, with a five-year carryover for unused amounts; the same property contributed to a private foundation is subject to a 20% ceiling. For 2026 the §170(b)(1)(I) floor also applies: an itemizer may deduct contributions only to the extent they exceed 0.5% of adjusted gross income, and the floor applies to cash and non-cash contributions alike.

### When does the appraisal date have to fall relative to the contribution date?

Under Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(4), the Qualified Appraisal must be signed and dated no earlier than 60 days before the date of the contribution and no later than the due date of the return, including extensions, on which the deduction is first claimed. Where the deduction is first claimed on an amended return, the appraisal must be received before the amended return is filed.

### Is the valuation date the contribution date or the date the charity liquidates the asset?

The valuation date is the contribution date. The deduction is measured by the Fair Market Value of the contributed property as of the effective date of the completed contribution, which the appraisal must state under Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(3). A subsequent liquidation is reported separately on Form 8282, and any variance between the claimed value and realized proceeds is reconciled through the methodology stated in the appraisal rather than by re-dating the valuation.

## Conclusion: The Vehicle Is a Wrapper. The Valuation Is the Asset.

The charitable vehicle decision is worth the time it takes. A donor-advised fund converts a single contribution into a multi-year grant-making programme; a charitable remainder unitrust converts a concentrated, appreciated, volatile position into an income stream with a charitable remainder beyond it, revaluing the corpus annually so the payment obligation moves with the asset rather than against it; a direct gift gives the operating charity the asset and the responsibility for it.

But the vehicle is a wrapper. It determines who holds legal title, which excise regime applies, when the asset is likely to be sold, and whether an annual valuation obligation attaches. It does not determine whether the contribution requires a Qualified Appraisal, who may sign it, what it must contain, when it must be dated, or whether it must be attached to the return. Those questions are answered by IRS Section 170 and Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17, and the answers are the same in all three columns.

Three provisions, read together, make the point more forcefully than any advisory argument can. Under §170(f)(11)(C), the appraisal is required above $5,000 for property that is not a publicly traded security, and CCA 202302012 confirms that digital assets fall outside that exception. Under §6664(c)(3), the reasonable-cause exception to the accuracy-related penalty is unavailable for an overstated charitable deduction unless the claimed value was based on a Qualified Appraisal by a qualified appraiser and the donor made a good-faith investigation of value. And under the Form 8282 rules, the donee must report the disposition and the amount received within three years, furnishing a copy to the donor. Read as a sequence: obtain a contemporaneous conclusion of Fair Market Value, prepared to standard, from a qualified appraiser; document that you engaged with it; then, when the proceeds appear on a return you did not file, the record will explain itself.

That is the difference between an appraisal and a valuation. An appraisal is a record created at the time the value was effective, by an appraiser qualified to state it, documented well enough to be testable. A valuation is a number produced when someone asks. Digital asset contributions to donor-advised funds and charitable remainder trusts are structured to reward the first and to expose the second.
