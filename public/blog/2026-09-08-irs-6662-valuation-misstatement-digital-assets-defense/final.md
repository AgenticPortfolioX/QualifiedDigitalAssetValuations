# IRS Section 6662 Valuation Misstatement Penalties and Digital Assets: Substantial and Gross Overstatement Thresholds, the Reasonable Cause Standard Under Section 6664, and the Qualified Appraisal Defense
*Published: September 8, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

Every digital asset tax dispute eventually becomes a valuation dispute. The IRS may open an examination because a Form 1099-DA does not match a return, because a charitable deduction draws a second look, or because an estate inventory raises a question. But once the examination is open, the fight usually narrows to a single number: the value claimed on the return. And when that number is wrong in the IRS's view, the consequences are not discretionary. They are computed.

That computation is the subject of this guide. Under IRC Section 6662, the accuracy-related penalty attaches mechanically once a claimed valuation crosses a statutory threshold. The practitioner who understands those thresholds — 150 percent, 200 percent, 65 percent, 40 percent — can counsel clients before a return is filed, when a defense file can still be built. The practitioner who does not will meet them for the first time in an examination, when the only remaining question is the size of the penalty.

This guide is written for estate attorneys, CPAs, tax directors, and the high-net-worth holders they advise. It covers the statutory mechanics of the valuation misstatement penalty, the reasonable cause standard under Section 6664, the charitable-donation trap that eliminates the reasonable cause defense, appraiser-side liability under Section 6695A, and the documentation the IRS actually demands when it challenges a digital asset value. It is educational material, not legal advice; every matter should be reviewed with qualified tax counsel.

## Part I: Why the Valuation Claim Is the Exposed Point for Digital Assets

Digital assets concentrate valuation risk in ways traditional property does not. A share of publicly traded stock has an objective price on an established market, and the IRS rarely disputes it. A digital asset has a price display, not necessarily a price. The distinction matters because Section 6662 does not penalize a taxpayer for failing to predict a market; it penalizes a taxpayer for claiming a value that is measurably wrong relative to the correct Fair Market Value determined under the standard of *United States v. Cartwright* — the price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither under compulsion, both reasonably informed.

Several structural features of digital assets make valuation misstatements more likely — and more visible:

**Volatility between valuation and filing.** A value defensible on the transaction date can look extreme by filing season. The effective date of every appraisal must match the transaction date, not the date the paperwork was completed.

**Illiquidity and manipulated markets.** NFTs, thinly traded tokens, and DeFi positions do not have a continuous two-sided market. Floor prices can be inflated by wash trading, and a single sale can be unrepresentative. A claimed value built from a self-selected screenshot is not evidence of market behavior; it is evidence of selection.

**Reconstruction risk.** The 2026 cost basis environment — wallet-by-wallet tracking, specific identification for assets acquired after January 1, 2026 — forces taxpayers to reconstruct years of transaction history. Reconstruction produces errors, and an overstated basis is a valuation misstatement in exactly the same way an overstated donation is.

**IRS guidance that has already defined the battlefield.** In CCA 202302012, the IRS Office of Chief Counsel concluded that charitable contributions of cryptocurrency above the Section 170 threshold require a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser. In CCA 202302011, the IRS addressed when a taxpayer may claim a loss for cryptocurrency that has declined in value — a claim that turns entirely on the value and basis asserted.

The point is not that digital asset holders are presumptively wrong. The point is that valuation claims are the portion of a digital asset return most likely to be challenged and most expensive to defend after the fact. The penalty statute converts that risk into arithmetic.

## Part II: The Accuracy-Related Penalty Framework — Section 6662 in Operation

Section 6662(a) imposes an addition to tax equal to **20 percent of the portion of any underpayment** attributable to one or more of the accuracy-related categories listed in Section 6662(b). The categories relevant to digital assets include:

- **Negligence or disregard of rules or regulations** — Section 6662(b)(1). Negligence includes any failure to make a reasonable attempt to comply with the Code, which in practice captures the taxpayer who ignores a reporting obligation or asserts a value with no methodology at all.
- **Any substantial understatement of income tax** — Section 6662(b)(2). For individuals, a substantial understatement exists when the understatement exceeds the greater of 10 percent of the tax required to be shown or $5,000 (Section 6662(d)). Omitted digital asset income — unreported sales, airdrops, staking rewards — frequently produces this category.
- **Any substantial valuation misstatement** — Section 6662(b)(3), defined in Section 6662(e) and discussed in detail below. This is the provision that governs challenged values.

Two features matter. The penalty applies only to the *portion of the underpayment* attributable to the misconduct — not the entire tax liability, and not the entire value claimed. And the categories do not stack: under Treasury Regulation Section 1.6662-2(c), a portion attributable to more than one category draws a single penalty at the highest rate. A return that is both negligent and contains a gross misstatement is penalized once at 40 percent, not twice.

Civil fraud is separate. Section 6663 imposes a penalty of **75 percent** of the underpayment attributable to fraud, provable only by clear and convincing evidence. The line between a valuation misstatement and fraud is the line between a wrong number and an intentionally wrong number — and Defensible Data is what keeps a case on the right side.

The accuracy-related penalty is also an *addition to tax* — it accrues interest from the return due date, and it is not deductible. A 20 percent penalty on a substantial underpayment is therefore best understood as the baseline cost of losing a valuation dispute; the 40 percent gross rate is the cost of losing badly.

## Part III: Substantial and Gross Valuation Misstatements — Thresholds and Worked Calculations

The valuation misstatement penalty is defined by thresholds, and the thresholds deserve precision because they are counterintuitive in both directions.

### The 150 Percent Rule — Section 6662(e)(1)(A)

Under Section 6662(e)(1)(A), a substantial valuation misstatement exists when **the value of any property (or the adjusted basis of any property) claimed on a return of tax imposed by chapter 1 is 150 percent or more of the amount determined to be the correct value or basis**. Chapter 1 is the income tax. The rule therefore reaches any income-tax claim built on a value or basis: a charitable deduction for donated crypto or an NFT, a basis figure that reduces gain on sale, a loss claim, an IRA or SDIRA value used in a taxable computation.

A penalty is imposed only if the underpayment attributable to all valuation misstatements for the year exceeds **$5,000** ($10,000 for most corporations) under Section 6662(e)(2). The floor is an aggregate test: multiple smaller misstatements count together.

### The 200 Percent Rule — Section 6662(h)

If the claimed value or basis is **200 percent or more** of the correct amount, the misstatement is *gross*, and the penalty rate doubles to **40 percent** under Section 6662(h), which applies by substituting "200 percent" for "150 percent" in the substantial misstatement test.

### The Arithmetic, Applied

The penalty is 20 or 40 percent of the underpayment of tax attributable to the misstatement — not of the misstated value — and the underpayment depends on the taxpayer's marginal rate. The following table shows how the thresholds translate into penalty tiers:

| Claimed value as a percentage of correct value | Classification | Penalty rate on attributable underpayment |
|---|---|---|
| Below 150% | No valuation misstatement penalty under Section 6662(e) | 0% (other accuracy-related categories may still apply) |
| 150% to 199% | Substantial valuation misstatement | 20% |
| 200% or more | Gross valuation misstatement | 40% |

**Example 1 — A gross charitable overvaluation.** A donor contributes an NFT collection to a public charity and claims a deduction of $900,000 on the theory that comparable sales support the figure. On examination the IRS determines the correct Fair Market Value of the contributed property on the contribution date was $300,000. The claimed value is 300 percent of the correct value — three times — which is a gross valuation misstatement. In the 37 percent bracket, the underpayment attributable to the misstatement is 37 percent of the $600,000 overstatement, or $222,000. The Section 6662(h) penalty is 40 percent of that underpayment: **$88,800**, plus interest from the due date. As explained in Part V, no reasonable cause defense is available for this position at all.

**Example 2 — A substantial, but rescuable, overvaluation.** Assume the same donation, but the correct value is $500,000 and the claimed value is $900,000 — 180 percent of correct. That is a substantial, not gross, misstatement. The underpayment is 37 percent of $400,000, or $148,000, and the 20 percent penalty is **$29,600**. If the deduction rests on a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser and a good-faith investigation of value, the reasonable cause exception of Section 6664(c)(3) can eliminate this penalty.

**Example 3 — A basis overstatement on sale.** A holder sells Bitcoin for $1,200,000 and claims a $900,000 basis from reconstructed wallet records, reporting a $300,000 gain. The IRS determines the correct basis was $300,000 and the correct gain $900,000 — a claimed basis 300 percent of correct, hence gross. At the 23.8 percent long-term rate (20 percent plus the 3.8 percent net investment income tax), the underpayment is 23.8 percent of the $600,000 understated gain, or $142,800, and the 40 percent penalty is **$57,120**. Because this is not a charitable position, the general reasonable cause exception is theoretically available — but the taxpayer must prove the reconstruction was a good-faith exercise, not a hindsight selection of the highest plausible figure.

The pattern to internalize: a claimed value sitting near a round number was likely selected rather than derived. Derived values, supported by method and records, have a defensible floor; selected values have only the taxpayer's assertion.

## Part IV: The Estate and Gift Analog — Section 6662(g) and the 65 Percent Rule

Estate and gift tax returns are governed by subtitle B, not chapter 1, so the 150 percent rule does not apply to them. Instead, Section 6662(g) defines a **substantial estate or gift tax valuation understatement** as a claimed value that is **65 percent or less of the correct value** — an understatement, not an overstatement, because the taxpayer's incentive on a 706 or 709 is to minimize value. The penalty is the same 20 percent of the underpayment attributable, subject to the same $5,000 floor. Under Section 6662(h)(2)(C), a claimed value of **40 percent or less** of the correct value is a gross misstatement carrying the 40 percent rate.

This matters to every estate with digital assets. Bitcoin estates, NFT collections, and DeFi positions held at death must be reported at date-of-death Fair Market Value (or the alternate valuation date elected under Section 2032), and the estate tax rate on the excess is 40 percent at the top bracket.

**Example 4 — Estate undervaluation.** A decedent's estate holds Bitcoin with a date-of-death Fair Market Value of $3,000,000. The personal representative reports $1,500,000 — exactly 50 percent of the correct value — relying on a single exchange screenshot from a low-volatility hour. Fifty percent is below the 65 percent threshold, so the position is a substantial estate tax valuation understatement. The additional estate tax attributable to the $1,500,000 understatement is 40 percent of that amount, or $600,000, and the Section 6662(g) penalty is 20 percent of the underpayment: **$120,000**. Had the reported value been $1,200,000 or less — 40 percent or below — the position would be gross, and the penalty would double to 40 percent of the attributable underpayment.

Gift tax presents the mirror image: a gift of digital assets reported below value shifts the same exposure onto the donor under the same 65 percent rule, and crypto transfers have become routine wealth-transfer vehicles. The defense in both contexts is a contemporaneous, USPAP-compliant appraisal documenting the valuation date, the methodology, and the market evidence — prepared before the return is filed, not after the notice arrives.

## Part V: The Charitable Donation Trap — Section 6664(c)(3) and Why Reasonable Cause Is Not Always Available

The reasonable cause exception is where most practitioners believe valuation disputes are won. Section 6664(c)(1) provides that no Section 6662 penalty applies to any portion of an underpayment if the taxpayer shows **reasonable cause and good faith**. The IRS evaluates this under Treasury Regulation Section 1.6664-4 by asking whether the taxpayer exercised ordinary business care and prudence — which in the valuation context means, at minimum, an honest investigation of value and, where the claim is large, a qualified appraisal.

But Section 6664(c)(3) carves out charitable deduction property, and the carve-out is the most misunderstood rule in this area:

> In the case of any underpayment attributable to a **substantial or gross valuation overstatement with respect to charitable deduction property**, the reasonable cause exception **shall not apply**.

The general rule for donated property is therefore: no reasonable cause, no good faith, no penalty relief — period. Two statutory exceptions soften the rule, and only one of them is meaningful:

1. **Substantial overstatements (150 percent to 199 percent of correct value) can be rescued** if the claimed value was based on a qualified appraisal made by a qualified appraiser *and* the taxpayer made a good-faith investigation of the value of the contributed property.
2. **Gross overstatements (200 percent or more) cannot be rescued at all.** No appraisal, no investigation, no professional advice — a gross overvaluation of charitable deduction property carries the 40 percent penalty without a reasonable cause defense.

"Charitable deduction property" is defined broadly as any property contributed in a contribution for which a Section 170 deduction was claimed. The definition excludes securities for which market quotations are readily available on an established securities market — a carve-out that covers listed stocks but does not cover cryptocurrency, NFTs, or other digital assets, which trade on venues that are not established securities markets within the meaning of the statute.

The practical consequence is severe and specific: **a donor who overvalues a crypto or NFT donation by 200 percent or more faces a 40 percent penalty that no amount of good faith can waive.** The only true defense is not to have the misstatement — which means the value must be established by a qualified appraisal and a genuine investigation *before* the contribution is reported. CCA 202302012 has already confirmed the IRS's view that a qualified appraisal is required for cryptocurrency contributions above the Section 170 threshold. Section 6664(c)(3) supplies the enforcement teeth: in a challenge, the appraisal is not merely good practice; it is the difference between a waivable penalty and a permanent one.

Attorneys and CPAs should treat the pre-contribution qualified appraisal as a condition of the deduction itself: the appraiser must be independent of both donor and charity and must satisfy the qualified appraiser definition of IRS Section 170(f)(11)(E)(ii), including verifiable education and experience in digital assets. For contributions exceeding $500,000, the appraisal must be attached to the return.

## Part VI: Appraiser-Side Exposure — Section 6695A and the Limits of Professional Shelter

The penalty framework is not one-sided. Section 6695A imposes a penalty on an appraiser who prepares an appraisal knowing, or having reason to know, that it will be used in connection with a return, where the value claimed on the return and based on that appraisal results in a substantial or gross valuation misstatement. The penalty is the **lesser of**:

- the **greater of** 10 percent of the underpayment attributable to the misstatement **or** $1,000, or
- **125 percent of the gross income** the appraiser received from preparing the appraisal.

An appraiser can escape the penalty by establishing that the value in the appraisal was **more likely than not the proper value** — a standard that rewards genuine methodology and punishes advocacy dressed as analysis.

The exposure is not hypothetical. An appraisal that adopts a donor's target number, or that reasons backward from a desired deduction, shifts risk onto both parties: the taxpayer inherits the Section 6662 exposure analyzed above, and the appraiser inherits Section 6695A exposure plus the professional consequences of a finding that the appraisal was not prepared in good faith. Willful conduct can draw Section 6701 penalties for aiding and abetting an understatement and, for practitioners before the IRS, discipline under Circular 230. Return preparers face their own regime under Section 6694 when they take positions on a return that lack a reasonable basis or substantial authority.

This is why "any appraisal will do" is a trap. A compliant appraisal is methodology documented against market evidence. That structure keeps the taxpayer on the right side of Section 6662 and the appraiser on the right side of Section 6695A.

## Part VII: What the IRS Actually Demands When It Challenges a Digital Asset Value

The penalty statute answers *how much*; the examination answers *what the IRS wants to see*. When an examiner challenges a digital asset valuation, the requests follow a predictable pattern — and the defense rises or falls on documentation that should already exist.

**The initial contact.** A mismatch between Form 1099-DA data and a return, a disproportionate charitable deduction, or a flagged estate inventory typically produces a notice or an examination. The examiner's first tool is the Information Document Request — often on Form 4564 — asking for transaction history, wallet addresses, exchange records, the appraisal, and the methodology behind the reported value. Practitioners should respond completely and on time; delay converts a documentation problem into a credibility problem.

**The valuation-specific requests.** Once the examiner identifies a value dispute, expect requests for:

- The complete appraisal report, not a summary, with the appraiser's qualifications and certification;
- The market data relied on, including comparable sales and the venues from which they were drawn;
- Evidence the data was screened for wash trading, self-dealing, and other manipulation — an examiner who finds undisclosed wash trades in the comparables will discount the entire analysis;
- The records establishing the transaction date and the valuation date, because Fair Market Value is date-specific;
- For charitable contributions, the fully completed Form 8283, including the donee acknowledgment and the Section B appraisal summary.

**The review posture.** Examiners do not need to prove the taxpayer's number wrong to the penny. They need to show the claimed value is not supported — and the 150 percent and 200 percent thresholds are generous enough that a wholesale failure of proof often lands a case in gross territory. The appraisal review focuses on three questions: Was the appraiser qualified for the asset class? Was the methodology appropriate to the asset and its market? Is the conclusion consistent with the evidence in the report? A report that answers all three with verifiable support rarely produces a penalty assertion. A report that fails any one of them invites the examiner to substitute a number.

## Part VIII: Building the Defense File Before Filing

The most important lesson of the penalty statute is timing: every defense mechanism in this guide — the 150 percent threshold, the reasonable cause exception, the qualified appraisal rescue, the Section 6695A safe harbor — operates on documentation that exists *at the moment the return is filed*. Reconstructing a valuation after a notice arrives is litigation support, not compliance, and it carries no weight in the reasonable cause analysis.

For digital assets, the defense file has six components:

1. **A contemporaneous qualified appraisal for every material valuation event** — a charitable contribution, an estate or trust distribution, a gift, a date-of-death valuation, an SDIRA value attestation, a Roth conversion or in-kind distribution. The appraisal must be dated to the transaction and prepared by an independent qualified appraiser with verifiable digital asset expertise.
2. **Verified transaction records** — exchange records, wallet addresses, transaction hashes, timestamps, and the purchase documentation that establishes basis. In the 2026 environment of wallet-by-wallet basis tracking, the record is the position.
3. **Market evidence with provenance** — for less-liquid assets, the specific sales, venues, dates, and screening methodology that support the conclusion. A defensible conclusion cites its evidence; a vulnerable one asserts it.
4. **The methodology narrative** — why the approach chosen (typically the market approach, with cost and income considerations where relevant) fits the asset, and why alternative values were rejected. Examiners and the courts reward reasoning, not conclusions.
5. **Form 8283 compliance for donations** — the appraisal summary, signatures, and the attached appraisal where the contribution exceeds $500,000.
6. **Retention without limit** — the IRS can examine a year years after filing, and digital asset documentation — exchange portals, wallet interfaces — does not persist. The permanent file is the taxpayer's, not the platform's.

## Part IX: The Response Playbook for Attorneys and CPAs

When a client receives a notice challenging a digital asset valuation, the sequence matters as much as the substance.

**First, establish the deadline and the scope.** A CP-2000 or examination notice carries response deadlines measured in weeks. Calendar every date: the response deadline, the 30-day letter window, the 90-day period for a Tax Court petition after a statutory notice of deficiency. Missing a deadline converts a disputable position into an assessed one.

**Second, gather the defense file before responding.** Pull the appraisal, the transaction records, the market data, and the Form 8283. If the file is incomplete, say so candidly and reconstruct what can be reconstructed — but understand that reconstruction is weaker than contemporaneous documentation, which is why Part VIII's file must be built before filing on every future matter.

**Third, respond to the substance.** If the value was appraised, walk the examiner through the methodology with supporting evidence. If it was not, be honest about the basis for the position and evaluate penalty exposure under this guide's thresholds before choosing a litigation posture.

**Fourth, evaluate the reasonable cause position.** For non-charitable misstatements, Section 6664(c)(1) applies and should be documented with the client's investigation and reliance on qualified advice. For charitable misstatements, Section 6664(c)(3) governs: a qualified appraisal plus good-faith investigation can rescue a substantial overstatement, and nothing rescues a gross one — there the penalty is a fixed cost of the dispute.

**Fifth, preserve the administrative record.** The 30-day letter leads to Appeals, where most valuation cases settle, on the same documentation an examiner saw. In Tax Court, the petition must be filed within 90 days of the statutory notice, and the court will test the same methodology, comparables, and appraiser qualifications — with the qualified appraisal as the centerpiece.

**Finally, separate the penalty fight from the value fight.** It is possible to lose the valuation and win the penalty. A taxpayer whose value is adjusted but whose reliance on a qualified appraisal and good-faith investigation is proven can escape Section 6662 entirely even where the tax is increased. The penalty defense is its own workstream, with its own documentation, and it should be prepared at the same time as the value defense — not after the value is lost.

## Conclusion

The Section 6662 valuation misstatement penalty is the mechanism by which the IRS enforces the integrity of every digital asset number on a return. It is mechanical, it is unforgiving at the 200 percent threshold, and for charitable donations it can be entirely beyond the reach of good faith. But it is also predictable. The practitioner who knows the thresholds can calculate exposure before filing, the client who commissions a contemporaneous USPAP-compliant appraisal can document Fair Market Value while it can still be proven, and the attorney or CPA who builds the defense file early can keep a valuation dispute where it belongs — on the merits of the value, not the severity of the penalty.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the penalty for overstating the value of crypto or an NFT on a tax return?

Under IRC Section 6662, the penalty is 20 percent of the underpayment attributable to a substantial valuation misstatement — a claimed value 150 percent or more of the correct value. At 200 percent or more — a gross valuation misstatement — the penalty rises to 40 percent under Section 6662(h). Interest accrues from the return due date.

### When does an overstated valuation become a gross valuation misstatement?

A gross valuation misstatement exists when the claimed value or basis is 200 percent or more of the correct amount. An NFT claimed at $900,000 with a correct Fair Market Value of $300,000 is a 300 percent overstatement — gross — carrying the 40 percent penalty rate on the tax underpayment attributable to the misstatement.

### Can I avoid the penalty if I made an honest mistake about the value of my digital assets?

For most income tax positions, yes — Section 6664(c)(1) waives the penalty on a showing of reasonable cause and good faith, which requires an honest investigation of value and, for material claims, a qualified appraisal. For charitable donations of digital assets, the rules are stricter: under Section 6664(c)(3), a substantial overstatement can be rescued only by a qualified appraisal plus a good-faith investigation, and a gross overstatement of charitable deduction property cannot be rescued at all.

### What is the reasonable cause exception under Section 6664, and when does it apply to digital assets?

Section 6664(c)(1) waives the Section 6662 penalty where the taxpayer shows reasonable cause and good faith, measured by ordinary business care and prudence — including reliance on qualified professional advice and a genuine investigation of the claimed value. The exception covers non-charitable misstatements (basis errors, SDIRA values, estate and gift positions) but is restricted or eliminated for charitable deduction property under Section 6664(c)(3).

### Can my appraiser be penalized if the IRS rejects the appraisal?

Yes. Section 6695A penalizes an appraiser whose appraisal results in a substantial or gross valuation misstatement, when the appraiser knew or should have known the appraisal would be used on a return. The penalty is the lesser of 10 percent of the attributable underpayment (but at least $1,000) or 125 percent of the appraisal fee, and it is avoided only if the appraiser shows the value was more likely than not correct. This is why appraiser independence and documented methodology protect both parties.

### Does the IRS require a qualified appraisal for charitable crypto donations?

Yes. Under Section 170(f)(11) and IRS Chief Counsel Advice 202302012, a donor claiming a charitable contribution deduction of more than $5,000 for cryptocurrency must obtain a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser and complete Form 8283. Contributions exceeding $500,000 require the appraisal to be attached to the return. Without the qualified appraisal, the deduction can be disallowed and valuation misstatement penalties under Section 6662 can attach.

### What happens if I undervalue digital assets on an estate or gift tax return?

Under Section 6662(g), a substantial estate or gift tax valuation understatement exists when the claimed value is 65 percent or less of the correct value — a 20 percent penalty on the tax underpayment attributable. At 40 percent or less of correct, the misstatement is gross and the penalty doubles to 40 percent. Date-of-death and date-of-gift valuations of digital assets should therefore rest on a contemporaneous qualified appraisal.

### What should I do if I receive an IRS notice challenging my digital asset valuation?

First, calendar every deadline, including the response date and the 90-day window for a Tax Court petition. Second, assemble the complete defense file: the appraisal, transaction records, market data, and Form 8283. Third, respond to the substance with the methodology and evidence. Fourth, evaluate the reasonable cause position separately from the value position — it is possible to concede value and still defeat the penalty where the taxpayer relied in good faith on a qualified appraisal.
