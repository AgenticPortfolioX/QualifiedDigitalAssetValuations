# Estate Administration for Digital Assets: A Fiduciary's Guide to Cryptocurrency Asset Discovery, Valuation, and IRS-Compliant Appraisal Requirements

*Published: July 14, 2026 | By Qualified Digital Asset Valuations (QDAV)*

## Executive Summary

Digital assets — including cryptocurrency, non-fungible tokens (NFTs), tokenized securities, and other blockchain-based property — present unique challenges in estate administration that traditional asset classes do not. Unlike a bank account, stock portfolio, or real estate holding, digital assets frequently lack a central custodian, exist in pseudonymous wallets without password recovery mechanisms, and require specialized technical and appraisal expertise to value, report, and distribute in compliance with IRS requirements and fiduciary duties.

For the executor or fiduciary tasked with administering an estate that includes digital assets, the stakes are significant. The IRS classifies cryptocurrency as property under IRS Notice 2014-21, meaning every transfer, disposition, and valuation event carries both tax and reporting consequences. The federal estate tax exemption for deaths in 2026 stands at $15 million per individual under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), with a top marginal rate of 40 percent — but estates exceeding this threshold must report all personal property, including digital assets, on Form 706 (United States Estate (and Generation-Skipping Transfer) Tax Return). Even estates below the filing threshold may need qualified appraisals for state-level estate tax purposes, date-of-death basis documentation, or Form 8971 (Information Regarding Beneficiaries Acquiring Property from a Decedent) reporting.

This guide provides a structured, five-phase framework for fiduciaries administering estates with digital assets, grounded in USPAP (Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice), IRC Section 170(f)(11), Treasury Circular 230, and the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA).

## The Digital Asset Estate Administration Challenge

The intersection of fiduciary duty and digital asset administration is a relatively recent phenomenon — but it is rapidly becoming a mainstream concern. According to a September 2025 Forbes survey, the average American holds approximately $200,000 in digital asset value, yet nearly 50 percent of respondents could not identify which specific digital assets they owned. For fiduciaries, this gap between ownership and awareness creates a primary risk: the estate may be unaware of assets that carry significant value and equally significant tax consequences.

### Why Digital Assets Are Different

Several characteristics of digital assets distinguish them from traditional estate property:

**No Central Authority.** Cryptocurrency held in self-custody (hardware wallets like Ledger or Trezor, software wallets like MetaMask, or mobile wallets) has no bank, broker, or institution that can reset credentials or provide account statements. There is no customer service hotline to call, no "forgot password" link, and no legal mechanism to compel private key disclosure from a decedent.

**Pseudonymity.** Blockchain addresses are alphanumeric strings with no inherent connection to personal identity. Without documentation linking an address to an individual, the asset may never be discovered.

**Volatility.** The fair market value of digital assets can fluctuate dramatically between the date of death and the date of distribution, creating valuation complexity for Form 706 reporting and fiduciary accounting.

**Technical Specialization.** Accessing, valuing, and transferring digital assets requires technical knowledge that most fiduciaries — and most financial advisors — do not possess. Improper handling can result in permanent loss of assets.

**Regulatory Ambiguity.** While the IRS has provided guidance on basic classification (Notice 2014-21) and broker reporting (final regulations, 2025), the intersection of digital assets with estate tax law, fiduciary accounting, and trust administration remains an evolving area.

### The Fiduciary's Core Duties

Under the Uniform Probate Code and state law, estate fiduciaries owe the following duties with respect to digital assets:

1. **Duty to Discover:** Locate and inventory all digital assets owned by the decedent.
2. **Duty to Preserve:** Secure assets from loss, theft, or unauthorized access pending distribution.
3. **Duty to Value:** Determine the fair market value of each asset as of the date of death (or alternate valuation date, if elected) using professionally accepted methodologies.
4. **Duty to Report:** File accurate tax returns — including Form 706 if the gross estate exceeds the exemption amount — with proper valuation support.
5. **Duty to Distribute:** Transfer assets to beneficiaries or sell them for distribution, documenting each transaction for tax basis purposes.

Failure to satisfy any of these duties can result in personal liability for the fiduciary, including exposure to IRS penalties, surcharge actions by beneficiaries, and professional discipline for attorneys, CPAs, and trust officers.

## Phase 1: Asset Discovery and Documentation

The first and often most challenging phase of digital asset estate administration is discovery. Unlike traditional assets, digital holdings may exist across multiple wallets, exchanges, and blockchain networks with no consolidated record.

### Sources of Digital Asset Holdings

Assets may be held in any of the following forms:

**Exchange Accounts.** Coinbase, Kraken, Binance.US, Gemini, and other centralized exchanges provide the most accessible form of digital asset holding. Exchange accounts typically require login credentials and two-factor authentication. Unlike self-custody, exchange accounts can often be accessed through probate court orders and standard estate administration procedures — though each exchange has its own process for estate-related account access.

**Self-Custody Wallets.** Hardware wallets (Ledger, Trezor, KeepKey), software wallets (MetaMask, Phantom, Trust Wallet), and mobile wallets hold assets directly on the blockchain, with the private keys or recovery phrases under the decedent's control. Without the recovery phrase (typically a 12- or 24-word mnemonic seed), these assets may be irretrievable.

**DeFi Positions.** Decentralized finance platforms (Uniswap, Aave, Compound, Lido) involve complex positions — liquidity pools, staked tokens, lending positions — that may not be immediately visible from a simple wallet balance. These positions may have additional rights, obligations, or unlock schedules that affect valuation.

**Tokenized Assets.** Tokenized real estate, tokenized securities, and asset-backed tokens require specialized valuation approaches that consider both the on-chain token value and the underlying off-chain asset.

**NFT Collections.** Non-fungible tokens — including profile-picture collections (Bored Ape Yacht Club, CryptoPunks), digital art, and tokenized real-world assets — require individual valuation based on specific traits, rarity, and recent comparable sales.

### Practical Discovery Protocol

The following steps are recommended for fiduciaries conducting digital asset discovery:

**1. Review Financial Records.** Check bank statements for transfers to known exchanges (Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini), hardware wallet purchases (Ledger, Trezor), and cryptocurrency tax software invoices (CoinTracker, Koinly, TokenTax).

**2. Examine Physical Belongings.** Search for hardware wallets, paper wallets, and written recovery phrases stored in safes, safety deposit boxes, desks, or other secure locations. Note that recovery phrases may be disguised as innocuous word lists.

**3. Review Digital Records.** With appropriate legal authority, examine the decedent's email accounts for exchange registration confirmations, wallet addresses in correspondence, and platform communications. Review browser bookmarks, password managers, and cloud storage for wallet files or seed phrase documents.

**4. Engage Blockchain Forensics.** For estates where digital assets are suspected but not documented, blockchain forensic analysis can trace transactions from known addresses, identify associated wallet clusters, and estimate the scope of holdings. This is particularly valuable when the decedent used multiple wallets or privacy-enhancing techniques.

**5. Contact Known Counterparties.** Reach out to any known custodians, exchange platforms, or digital asset service providers identified through the discovery process. Most major exchanges have established procedures for estate administration inquiries.

### Documentation Requirements

For each identified digital asset, the fiduciary should document:

| Field | Description | Purpose |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| Asset type | Cryptocurrency, NFT, tokenized asset, DeFi position | Classification for valuation approach |
| Blockchain | Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, Polygon, etc. | Access and transfer mechanism |
| Wallet address | Public key address | Verification and valuation |
| Custody form | Self-custody, exchange, custodian | Access requirements |
| Quantity | Number of tokens or NFTs | Valuation basis |
| Acquisition date | Date(s) of purchase or receipt | Basis determination |
| Cost basis | Original purchase price if known | Step-up calculation reference |

## Phase 2: Legal Access and Wallet Recovery

Once digital assets have been identified, the fiduciary must obtain lawful access to them. The legal framework for digital asset access varies depending on the custody structure, the applicable state's adoption of RUFADAA, and the terms of the decedent's estate planning documents.

### The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA)

RUFADAA, adopted in substantially similar form by the majority of U.S. states, establishes three tiers of fiduciary authority to access digital assets:

**Tier 1 — Online Tool.** If the digital asset platform (exchange, wallet service, social media platform) provides an online tool for designating account management upon death or incapacity, the fiduciary follows the tool's instructions.

**Tier 2 — Will or Trust.** If the decedent's will, trust, or other estate planning document explicitly authorizes the fiduciary to access digital assets, the terms of that document govern — provided they do not conflict with a Tier 1 tool.

**Tier 3 — Default Rules.** In the absence of a Tier 1 or Tier 2 directive, RUFADAA determines the fiduciary's authority. Generally, fiduciaries may access digital assets to the extent necessary to carry out their duties, but access to electronic communications (email, messaging) is more restricted than access to "digital property" (crypto, NFTs, digital files).

**Critical Implication:** If the decedent did not explicitly authorize fiduciary digital asset access in their estate planning documents — and did not use an online tool — the fiduciary may face significant barriers to accessing exchange accounts and may not be able to access self-custodied assets at all without the recovery phrase.

### Exchange Account Access

For assets held on centralized exchanges, the following documentation is typically required:

1. **Certified copy of the death certificate**
2. **Letters testamentary or letters of administration** from the probate court
3. **Completed exchange-specific estate claim forms** (each exchange has its own process)
4. **Two-factor authentication recovery** — if the decedent's phone number or authenticator app is inaccessible, the exchange's recovery process may take weeks or months

### Self-Custody Wallet Recovery

Self-custodied assets present the most significant access challenge. Without the 12- or 24-word recovery phrase, assets held in non-custodial wallets are effectively irrecoverable in many cases. The following options may be available:

**Recovery Phrase Found.** If the recovery phrase is located, it can be used to restore wallet access on compatible wallet software. The fiduciary should exercise extreme caution — entering an incorrect recovery phrase on certain devices (particularly Trezor devices after firmware updates) can trigger a device wipe.

**Recovery Phrase Partially Known.** Forensic specialists can reconstruct partial recovery phrases by analyzing entropy patterns and known word lists (BIP-39 standard). This is a specialized service that requires cryptographic expertise.

**PIN or Password Known but Device Locked.** Some hardware wallets (certain Ledger models) can be accessed with the PIN alone, even without the recovery phrase. However, devices that wipe after a set number of incorrect attempts (typically three) require extreme caution.

**No Recovery Options Available.** When a wallet's recovery phrase, PIN, and password are all unknown, the assets may be permanently inaccessible. This outcome should be documented in the estate's records for fiduciary protection purposes.

### Multi-Signature and Smart Contract Considerations

For estates where digital assets are held in multi-signature wallets (requiring multiple private keys for any transaction) or smart contracts (with programmable release conditions), the fiduciary must identify all required signatories and satisfy all code-level conditions before transfer. These structures are increasingly common among high-net-worth digital asset holders and may require specialized legal analysis.

## Phase 3: Date-of-Death Fair Market Value Determination

The central appraisal question in any estate involving digital assets is: "What was the fair market value (FMV) of each digital asset on the date of the decedent's death (or the alternate valuation date)?"

### Legal Foundation

Under IRC Section 2031, the value of the gross estate includes the value of all property — real or personal, tangible or intangible — at the time of the decedent's death. Treas. Reg. Section 20.2031-1(b) defines fair market value as "the price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or to sell and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts."

For property reported on Form 706, the executor may elect an alternate valuation date (six months after the date of death) under Section 2032, which may be advantageous when digital asset values decline after death.

### Why Exchange Prices Are Insufficient

A common misconception among executors and beneficiaries is that the spot price shown on Coinbase, CoinMarketCap, or CoinGecko at the time of death constitutes a sufficient valuation. In most cases, this is incorrect for the following reasons:

**Volume and Liquidity.** The reported "price" of an asset on a data aggregator reflects the last traded price on one or more exchanges. For liquid assets like Bitcoin or Ethereum on major exchanges, this price is generally a reliable indicator of FMV at a specific point in time. However, for less liquid tokens or assets traded primarily on decentralized exchanges with thin order books, the last traded price may not represent the price at which the estate's holdings could actually be sold.

**Illiquid Digital Assets.** NFTs, rare tokens, and positions in illiquid DeFi protocols cannot be valued by reference to an exchange price alone. Each asset requires individual analysis considering comparable sales, trait rarity (for NFTs), market conditions, and the size of the decedent's position relative to the overall market.

**Time-of-Day Variation.** Cryptocurrency prices are highly volatile and trade 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. The appropriate valuation point must be consistent with the standard established for other estate assets — typically the date of death (any time) or the alternate valuation date.

### USPAP-Compliant Valuation Methodology

A qualified appraisal for estate tax purposes must comply with USPAP Standards 7 and 9 (Personal Property Appraisal, Development and Reporting), which require the appraiser to consider all three approaches to value where applicable:

#### Market Approach

The market approach determines fair market value by reference to observable transactions in an active market. For digital assets:

- **Exchange-traded cryptocurrencies** (Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana): The market approach is generally applicable, using volume-weighted average prices from established exchanges for the date-of-death window.
- **NFTs and illiquid tokens:** The market approach requires analysis of comparable sales (comps), adjusting for trait differences, marketplace conditions, and time elapsed since the transaction.
- **Tokenized assets:** Comparison to similar assets with observable market prices.

#### Income Approach

The income approach values an asset based on the present value of expected future income streams. This approach is relevant for:

- **Staked cryptocurrency** that generates staking yields
- **DeFi liquidity positions** that earn trading fees
- **Yield-bearing protocols** (Lido, Aave, Compound) where positions generate ongoing returns
- **Tokenized revenue streams** tied to underlying business operations

The income approach requires careful analysis of historical yield rates, protocol risk, and appropriate discount rates.

#### Cost Approach

The cost approach considers the cost to create or replace the digital asset. This is most relevant for:

- **Minted NFTs:** The original minting cost plus any associated fees
- **Tokenized real-world assets:** The underlying physical asset's replacement cost
- **Custom smart contract development:** If the digital asset represents a proprietary blockchain implementation

In most estate valuation contexts, the market approach carries the greatest weight for digital assets, supplemented by the income approach where applicable.

### Date-of-Death Pricing Methodology

For liquid cryptocurrency assets, the following methodology is industry standard for qualified appraisals:

1. **Identify the applicable date:** Date of death (DoD) or alternate valuation date (AVD)
2. **Select pricing sources:** Use volume-weighted average prices from established exchanges (Coinbase, Kraken, Binance.US)
3. **Determine the pricing window:** For date-of-death valuations, use UTC midnight-to-midnight volume-weighted average prices for the death date; for time-of-death-specific appraisals, narrow the window to the known time of death
4. **Document sources:** Include exchange data with timestamps, volume figures, and source URLs in the appraisal workfile
5. **Cross-reference:** Verify pricing across multiple independent data providers (CoinMarketCap, CoinGecko, Kaiko) to confirm consistency

For illiquid assets (NFTs, exotic tokens, large positions), the appraisal must incorporate additional analysis, including market depth assessment, control premium or minority discount considerations, and the impact of the estate's position size on potential liquidation pricing.

### The Qualified Appraiser Requirement

Under IRC Section 170(f)(11), a "qualified appraisal" must be performed by a "qualified appraiser" who:

1. Has earned an appraisal designation from a recognized professional appraiser organization
2. Has demonstrated competency in the type of property being valued — for digital assets, this includes knowledge of blockchain technology, cryptocurrency markets, NFT valuation methodology, and USPAP Standards 7 and 9
3. Regularly performs appraisals for compensation
4. Meets the education and experience requirements of Treasury Circular 230

**Crypto tax software reports — including portfolio trackers, realized gain/loss reports, and position summaries generated by platforms like CoinTracker, Koinly, or TokenTax — do not constitute qualified appraisals.** These tools report historical transaction data and cost basis; they do not provide a USPAP-compliant fair market value determination with appraiser analysis, methodology documentation, and signed certification. Relying solely on tax software reports for Form 706 purposes exposes the estate to potential accuracy-related penalties under IRC Section 6662, which can reach 20 percent of any underpayment attributable to a valuation misstatement.

## Phase 4: IRS Reporting and Qualified Appraisal Requirements

### Form 706 Filing Threshold

For decedents dying in 2026, the federal estate tax basic exclusion amount is $15 million per individual (indexed for inflation) under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA). This means that estates with total gross assets below $15 million generally do not need to file Form 706, unless the executor elects portability of the decedent's unused exemption to the surviving spouse.

Estates exceeding the $15 million threshold must file Form 706 and report all assets — including digital assets — at their date-of-death fair market value.

### Schedule F: Personal Property

Digital assets are reported on **Schedule F** (Other Miscellaneous Property) of Form 706. Each asset should be listed individually, with:

- Description (nomenclature, blockchain, quantity)
- Date-of-death fair market value (and alternate valuation if elected)
- Valuation methodology used
- Appraiser certification (if a qualified appraisal was obtained)

### Qualified Appraisal Threshold for Digital Assets in Estates

While IRC Section 170(f)(11) specifically requires a qualified appraisal for charitable contributions exceeding $5,000, the estate tax context imposes a similar practical standard: any digital asset valuation that is not based on a USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal carries heightened audit risk. The IRS has increasingly focused on digital asset reporting in estate tax returns, and the agency's Digital Asset Advisory Group has signaled that USPAP-compliant appraisals are the expected standard for significant holdings.

### Basis Reporting (Form 8971)

Under Section 1014(f) and Section 6035, executors of estates that file Form 706 must also file Form 8971 (Information Regarding Beneficiaries Acquiring Property from a Decedent), which reports to both the IRS and each beneficiary the value of property inherited from the estate. This form is critical for digital asset beneficiaries because it establishes the basis from which future capital gains taxes will be calculated.

### Penalty Framework

| Violation | Penalty | Authority |
|-----------|---------|-----------|
| Valuation understatement (estate tax) | 20% of underpayment | IRC §6662 |
| Gross valuation misstatement | 40% of underpayment | IRC §6662(h) |
| Failure to file Form 706 | 5% per month, up to 25% | IRC §6651 |
| Failure to file Form 8971 | $270 per beneficiary per failure | IRC §6721 |
| Fraud or willful neglect | 75% of underpayment | IRC §6663 |

For a digital asset estate valued at $20 million where the digital assets constitute $5 million, a 20-percent valuation understatement penalty would be $200,000 on a hypothetical $1 million underpayment — before interest and professional fees. The cost of a qualified appraisal is typically a small fraction of this exposure.

## Phase 5: Distribution and Step-Up in Basis Documentation

### Step-Up in Basis

Under IRC Section 1014, property acquired from a decedent receives a basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death (or alternate valuation date). This "step-up" rule applies to cryptocurrency and other digital assets classified as property under IRS Notice 2014-21.

**Example:** If the decedent purchased 10 Bitcoin for a total of $50,000 and the date-of-death FMV is $600,000, the beneficiary's new cost basis is $600,000. If the beneficiary sells immediately at $600,000, no capital gains tax is owed; the gain from $50,000 to $600,000 is permanently eliminated.

**Exception:** Digital assets held within retirement accounts (self-directed IRAs, Solo 401(k)s, or other tax-advantaged accounts) do not receive a step-up in basis. Instead, these accounts follow the distribution and income taxation rules applicable to the specific retirement account type.

**Community Property States:** In community property states (including California, Texas, Wisconsin, and several others), both halves of community-property digital assets generally receive a step-up for the surviving spouse — effectively doubling the tax benefit.

### Transfer Mechanics

Transferring digital assets from the estate to beneficiaries involves several technical steps:

**Exchange Assets.** Assets held on centralized exchanges can be transferred to beneficiary exchange accounts once the exchange processes the estate claim documentation. Transfers should be documented with blockchain transaction IDs and exchange confirmation records.

**Self-Custody Assets.** Self-custodied assets must be transferred from the decedent's wallet address to beneficiary wallet addresses via blockchain transactions. Each transaction requires:
- The decedent's private key (to authorize the outgoing transfer)
- The beneficiary's public address (as the destination)
- An appropriate gas or transaction fee (in the blockchain's native token)
- Documentation of the transaction for basis reporting

**NFT and DeFi Positions.** Complex positions may require unwinding before distribution. For example, a staked position on Lido may need to be unstaked (with an unbonding period of 7-21 days depending on the protocol), and an NFT collection may need to be appraised individually before transfer.

### Documenting Basis for Beneficiaries

The fiduciary should provide each beneficiary with written documentation of:
- The date of death (or alternate valuation date) FMV for each digital asset received
- The appraisal report or valuation support
- The asset's acquisition date from the estate
- The original cost basis (if known and different from date-of-death FMV)
- Any applicable discounts or premiums applied in the valuation

This documentation is critical for the beneficiary's future tax reporting when the digital asset is sold.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. What is a qualified appraisal for digital assets in an estate?

A qualified appraisal is a USPAP-compliant valuation performed by a qualified appraiser meeting Treasury Circular 230 standards. For digital assets, this means a signed appraisal report that analyzes fair market value using appropriate methodology — typically the market approach for liquid cryptocurrency, supplemented by the income approach for yield-bearing positions and the cost approach where applicable. The appraisal includes workfile documentation, appraiser certification, and a detailed explanation of the valuation rationale. It is distinct from a crypto tax software report or exchange-generated pricing.

### 2. Do I need a qualified appraisal for cryptocurrency on Form 706?

If the digital assets in the estate exceed the $5,000 threshold applicable to charitable contributions under IRC Section 170(f)(11), or if the total estate value requires Form 706 filing and the digital holdings represent a material component of the gross estate, a qualified appraisal is strongly recommended. The IRS has heightened scrutiny on digital asset valuations, and a USPAP-compliant appraisal provides the defensible documentation necessary to withstand audit, penalties, and fiduciary breach claims.

### 3. How do executors find and access cryptocurrency after death?

Executors should begin by searching financial records for exchange transfers, hardware wallet purchases, and tax software invoices. Physical searches should target wallets, seed phrases, and password records. Digital records — email, password managers, browser bookmarks — should be reviewed with appropriate legal authority. For suspected but undocumented holdings, blockchain forensic analysis can trace transactions and identify associated wallet addresses. Accessing found assets requires the decedent's private keys or recovery phrases for self-custody assets, or estate documentation for exchange accounts.

### 4. How is date-of-death fair market value determined for crypto?

For liquid cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin or Ethereum, date-of-death FMV is determined using volume-weighted average prices from established exchanges on the date of death. For illiquid assets like NFTs, rare tokens, or large positions, the analysis incorporates comparable sales, trait rarity analysis, market depth assessment, and applicable discounts. The valuation must be performed by a qualified appraiser in compliance with USPAP Standards 7 and 9, with full documentation of pricing sources, volume data, and methodology.

### 5. What is the estate tax treatment of cryptocurrency in 2026?

Cryptocurrency is treated as property for federal tax purposes (IRS Notice 2014-21). Digital assets are included in the gross estate at their date-of-death fair market value under IRC Section 2031. The 2026 federal estate tax exemption is $15 million per individual ($30 million for married couples) under OBBBA, with a top rate of 40 percent. Beneficiaries receive a step-up in basis to the date-of-death FMV under IRC Section 1014, eliminating capital gains on appreciation that occurred during the decedent's lifetime.

### 6. Does cryptocurrency get a step-up in basis upon death?

Yes. Under IRC Section 1014 and consistent with the IRS's classification of cryptocurrency as property (Notice 2014-21), digital assets held personally by the decedent receive a step-up in basis to the date-of-death fair market value. This means that if the decedent purchased Bitcoin for $10,000 and it is worth $100,000 at death, the beneficiary's basis becomes $100,000. The exception is cryptocurrency held in retirement accounts (SDIRAs, Solo 401(k)s), which follow retirement account distribution rules rather than capital gains treatment.

### 7. What happens if an executor cannot access a hardware wallet?

If the recovery phrase, PIN, and password for a hardware wallet are all unknown, the assets may be permanently inaccessible. This outcome should be documented in the estate's fiduciary accounting records to protect the executor from beneficiary claims. Forensic recovery specialists may be able to reconstruct partial recovery phrases or extract data from damaged devices, but there is no guarantee. The best prevention is proactive estate planning — ensuring that wallet credentials and recovery phrases are securely documented and accessible to the designated fiduciary.

### 8. What IRS penalties apply for undervaluing digital assets in an estate?

The accuracy-related penalty under IRC Section 6662 imposes a 20 percent penalty on any underpayment of tax attributable to a valuation understatement (where the claimed value is 50 percent or less of the correct value). For gross valuation misstatements (25 percent or less of the correct value), the penalty doubles to 40 percent. For an estate with $1 million in undervalued digital assets and a 40 percent marginal estate tax rate, the potential penalty exposure is $80,000 to $160,000 — substantially exceeding the cost of a qualified appraisal.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

Estate administration for digital assets requires a structured, professionally informed approach that addresses the unique challenges of asset discovery, legal access, USPAP-compliant valuation, IRS reporting, and beneficiary distribution. The fiduciary who approaches digital assets with the same rigor applied to traditional estate assets — but with an understanding of the technical and regulatory distinctions — can properly discharge their duties and protect themselves from personal liability.

### When to Engage a Qualified Digital Asset Appraiser

A USPAP-compliant qualified appraisal should be obtained when:

- The estate includes digital assets with a total value exceeding $5,000 (the IRC Section 170 threshold applicable to charitable contributions, which serves as a general diligence benchmark)
- Form 706 is required or may be required for the estate
- The digital assets include illiquid holdings (NFTs, large token positions, DeFi positions) that cannot be valued by reference to exchange prices alone
- The fiduciary seeks protection from beneficiary claims regarding valuation accuracy
- State-level estate tax, inheritance tax, or fiduciary accounting requirements apply
