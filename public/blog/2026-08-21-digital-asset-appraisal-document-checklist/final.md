# How to Gather the Right Documents for a Digital Asset Appraisal: A Checklist for Attorneys, CPAs, and Their Clients

*Published: August 21, 2026 | By QDAV*

---

## Why the Document Package Determines the Outcome

Every digital asset appraisal engagement begins the same way: with a document request. And in our experience, the single largest driver of appraisal speed, cost, and defensibility is not the complexity of the asset — it is the quality of the document package the client brings to the table.

A qualified appraisal is a professional opinion of Fair Market Value, supported by credible evidence and prepared in accordance with USPAP. A USPAP Compliant appraisal is defined by what it documents, not by what it claims: the methodology, the evidence, and the appraiser's certification must all be verifiable. The IRS does not require an appraiser to be clairvoyant. It requires the appraiser's conclusions to be supportable. That supportability is built, document by document, from the records a taxpayer, estate, or trust can produce.

For attorneys and CPAs, the stakes are direct. When you refer a client for a digital asset appraisal — for an estate, a gift, a charitable contribution, an SDIRA attestation, or in response to IRS scrutiny — the quality of the intake determines whether that appraisal will protect your client or become a liability. A valuation that cannot be defended is worse than no valuation at all, because it creates a false sense of compliance.

This guide is written for the professionals who prepare clients for appraisal engagements. It explains what documents a qualified appraiser actually needs, why each category matters, how the evidence differs by asset type, and the mistakes that most commonly delay or invalidate digital asset appraisals.

## The Professional Standard: Why Records Matter

Before the checklist, the framework. Two bodies of rules govern what an appraiser must document, and both flow directly from the material the client provides.

### The USPAP Record Keeping Rule

The Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice — USPAP — is the professional standard of appraisal practice in the United States. Its Record Keeping Rule requires an appraiser to prepare a workfile for every appraisal assignment. That workfile must include:

- The name of the client and the identity, by name or type, of any other intended users;
- True copies of any written report;
- Summaries of the appraiser's research and analyses; and
- **All other data, information, and documentation necessary to support the appraiser's opinions and conclusions and to show compliance with USPAP.**

That final requirement is the hook. Whatever the appraiser concludes must be traceable to documented evidence. If a client provides only a screenshot of a price, the workfile contains only a screenshot — and a screenshot, as we will discuss, is rarely sufficient. If a client provides exchange statements, verified wallet addresses, and acquisition records, the workfile contains a chain of evidence that supports the opinion under examination.

The retention requirement is equally important: USPAP requires the workfile to be retained for a minimum of five years after preparation, and in many jurisdictions, for the period required by statute or regulation. That retention obligation exists because appraisals are challenged long after they are prepared. An estate appraisal prepared in 2026 can be examined in 2030. The documents gathered today are the documents that will be available then.

### What the IRS Expects

The IRS does not prescribe a single format for digital asset documentation. It requires that Fair Market Value be determined under the willing buyer–willing seller standard and supported by credible evidence. In practice, the IRS has been explicit about what is not enough: in Chief Counsel Advice 202302012, issued in January 2023, the IRS confirmed that a qualified appraisal is required for charitable contributions of cryptocurrency exceeding $5,000 under IRS Section 170, and rejected exchange-generated value as a substitute for a qualified appraisal. The same logic applies across contexts: a price quote is data, not substantiation. The professional appraisal is the substantiation, and the documents are the foundation under it.

For CPAs, the accounting and audit side has moved in the same direction. The AICPA's Digital Assets Working Group has updated its practice aid for the accounting and auditing of digital assets, emphasizing that confirming existence and ownership of assets held in self-custody — as opposed to assets held at a platform — requires distinct procedures. The documents we describe below are precisely what make those procedures possible.

## The Complete Document Inventory

The following is the core document inventory for a digital asset appraisal engagement. Not every engagement requires every item, but a client who can produce this package will have a materially faster, more cost-effective, and more defensible appraisal than one who cannot.

### 1. Ownership and Identity Records

The appraiser must first establish that the client actually owns the assets being valued. This is the existence-and-control question, and it is the first thing an IRS examiner will ask.

- **Wallet addresses** — the public addresses of every wallet holding the assets, including hardware wallets, software wallets, exchange wallets, and custodial accounts.
- **Exchange and custodial account statements** — full statements from every platform holding digital assets, covering the valuation date and the period leading up to it.
- **Account registration records** — the name, tax identification details, and identity verification records associated with each platform account.
- **Custody documentation** — for institutional custodians, the account agreement or custody acknowledgment.
- **Proof of private key control** — the client need not disclose private keys, and should not. What is needed is a representation, verified where possible, of control: a signed attestation listing addresses and the access method (hardware device, multi-signature arrangement, custodial account).

### 2. Acquisition Records and Cost Basis

Cost basis is a tax recordkeeping concept — generally, what the client paid to acquire an asset. It is not the same as Fair Market Value, but it is essential context for the appraisal, for the tax return, and for the audit trail.

- **Purchase confirmations** — from exchanges, marketplaces, or OTC counterparties.
- **Mint records** — for NFTs and tokens created directly: the mint transaction, the mint cost, and the platform used.
- **OTC purchase agreements** — for private or negotiated purchases.
- **Fiat purchase receipts** — bank records, wire confirmations, or card statements showing the fiat amount paid.
- **Crypto-to-crypto purchase records** — for assets acquired with other digital assets: the transaction hash and the USD value of the asset used at the time of the transaction.
- **Prior tax returns and schedules** — Forms 8949, Schedule D, or crypto tax reports that establish previously reported basis and positions.

### 3. Transaction History

- **Complete transaction logs** — exports from exchanges and wallets covering all relevant transactions, with timestamps, amounts, counterparty addresses, and transaction hashes.
- **Trade fill reports** — for active traders, the exchange-level fills that show actual executed prices.
- **Deposit and withdrawal logs** — showing movement of assets into and out of platforms.
- **On-chain transaction data** — raw blockchain records tied to the verified wallet addresses (explorer exports or service-generated reports).

### 4. Transfer and Movement Records

Digital assets move. Between wallets, between exchanges, between individuals. The appraisal must account for every movement that affects the holdings at the valuation date.

- **Internal transfer records** — wallet-to-wallet transfers, including the addresses, dates, and amounts.
- **Gift and inheritance records** — documentation of assets received as gifts or inherited, including the date of receipt and the donor's basis information where available.
- **Loan and collateral records** — for assets used in lending protocols or posted as collateral: the loan agreement, the collateral position, and the terms.
- **Lost and recovered records** — documentation of lost private keys, abandoned wallets, or recovered access, with dates.

### 5. Income and Activity Records

Certain digital asset activities generate income, and that income has valuation consequences.

- **Staking records** — validator or staking position statements, reward schedules, and reward amounts.
- **Airdrop records** — the receipt date of any airdropped tokens and the fair market value at receipt.
- **Mining records** — for miners: hardware, power agreements, pool statements, and coinbase reward records.
- **DeFi yield records** — liquidity provision statements, yield-farming positions, and reward claims.
- **Fork and spin-off records** — documentation of assets received through protocol forks or spin-offs.

### 6. Loan, Lien, and Encumbrance Records

Digital assets can be encumbered, and encumbrances affect value and ownership.

- **Loan agreements** — any borrowing against the assets.
- **Security agreements or liens** — any third-party claims against the assets.
- **Dispute or litigation records** — any ongoing disputes affecting title.

### 7. Prior Valuations and Professional Reports

- **Prior appraisals** — earlier qualified appraisals of the same or related assets.
- **Portfolio reports** — valuation reports from custodians or wealth platforms.
- **Insurance valuations** — for significant collections, any insurance-appraisal documentation.

## Asset-Type-Specific Documentation

Different digital assets require different evidence. The table below summarizes the additional documentation by asset class.

| Asset Type | Core Evidence | Special Considerations |
|------------|---------------|------------------------|
| **Bitcoin and major cryptocurrencies** | Wallet addresses, exchange statements, transaction logs | Verify on-chain balances against reported holdings; account for exchange vs. self-custody splits |
| **Ethereum and ERC-20 tokens** | On-chain transaction history, wallet addresses, contract addresses | Confirm token contract legitimacy; check for wrapped or bridged versions |
| **NFTs (profile-picture, art, collectibles)** | Mint records, purchase confirmations, collection and trait data, provenance | Rarity and collection-floor data must be date-stamped; provenance matters for 1-of-1 works |
| **DeFi positions** | Liquidity pool statements, LP token records, deposit/withdrawal logs, protocol documentation | Positions can change minute to minute; establish the exact state at the valuation date |
| **Stablecoins** | Wallet and exchange records | Usually valued near $1, but peg history and reserves documentation matter in illiquid or distressed cases |
| **Wrapped assets** | Wrapping/unwrapping transaction records, underlying asset documentation | Value derives from the underlying asset; document the wrap contract and mechanism |
| **Airdropped tokens** | Receipt date, protocol announcement, fair market value at receipt | Establish the receipt value for both income and basis purposes |
| **Mined assets** | Hardware inventory, power agreements, pool statements, reward records | The coins are valued by market; the mining operation may require a separate cost-based analysis |
| **Pre-market / seed-round tokens** | Purchase agreement, term sheet, vesting schedule, project documentation | Little or no market evidence; valuation relies on documented terms and cost evidence |
| **Delisted or thinly traded assets** | Delisting notices, last trading records, community/over-the-counter evidence | Identify the valuation approach (often cost or limited market) and document why |

The unifying principle: the appraiser needs **what the asset is, when it was acquired, what was paid, where it is held, and what the market evidence says at the valuation date.**

## What the Appraiser Does with the Documents

Understanding the appraiser's process helps attorneys and CPAs prepare clients — and helps clients understand why the document request is not bureaucratic friction.

### Verification

The appraiser verifies existence and ownership. On-chain data is checked against reported holdings. Exchange statements are reviewed for consistency with wallet activity. For self-custodied assets, the appraiser relies on the address attestation and supporting transaction records. For platform-held assets, custody documentation and account statements are the evidence. This is the same discipline the AICPA practice aid describes for auditors: existence and ownership are confirmed through procedures appropriate to the custody model.

### Method Selection

With verified holdings, the appraiser selects the appropriate valuation methodology. For actively traded assets, the market approach controls — comparable sales, quoted prices, and trading volume-adjusted analyses. For NFTs, trait rarity, collection floors, and comparable sales are weighed. For illiquid or pre-market assets, the appraiser may rely on cost evidence or income considerations. The methodology must be appropriate to the asset and defensible under the willing buyer–willing seller standard.

### Workfile Assembly

Every document, analysis, and conclusion is assembled into the USPAP workfile. This is the artifact that survives an IRS examination. When the appraiser signs the report, the appraiser is certifying that the workfile supports the opinions expressed. A complete document package from the client is what makes that certification meaningful.

## The Evidence Hierarchy: Primary vs. Secondary

Not all records are created equal. The appraisal profession — and the IRS — treat evidence on a hierarchy. Attorneys and CPAs should understand this hierarchy when advising clients about what to preserve.

**Primary evidence** is independently verifiable and timestamped:

- Exchange statements and trade fill reports
- On-chain transaction data tied to verified wallet addresses
- Deposit and withdrawal logs from platforms
- Custody and account records
- Signed purchase agreements and term sheets
- Bank and wire records

**Secondary evidence** is useful but less authoritative:

- Email trade confirmations
- Screenshots of prices or balances
- Support-ticket records from exchanges
- Community or social-media records
- Self-prepared spreadsheets

**The rule of thumb:** if a document can be independently verified — by the exchange, by the blockchain, by a counterparty — it is primary. If it depends on the client's own representation, it is secondary.

This is why exchange screenshots, so commonly offered, are insufficient on their own. A screenshot has no metadata chain, no verifiable source, and no timestamp that the IRS can independently confirm. It is evidence of what a screen displayed, not proof of what a wallet held. The appraiser can use a screenshot as a clue — and then must verify the underlying fact through primary evidence.

For cost basis specifically, the hierarchy is well established: primary evidence includes exchange statements, complete trade fill reports, deposit and withdrawal logs, and on-chain transaction data tied to verified wallet addresses. Secondary evidence includes email confirmations and support tickets. When primary evidence is missing, the appraiser and the tax professional must work through reconstruction — and the appraisal report must disclose the limits of that reconstruction.

## Checklists by Engagement Type

The core inventory applies broadly, but each engagement type has specific requirements. These checklists give attorneys and CPAs a quick reference at intake.

### Estate Administration (Form 706)

When a decedent held digital assets, the estate must report them at Fair Market Value as of the date of death (or the alternate valuation date, if validly elected).

- [ ] Complete inventory of the decedent's wallets and accounts, discovered through device review, password managers, and prior tax returns
- [ ] Date-of-death valuation evidence: on-chain snapshots, exchange statements, and market data as of the valuation date
- [ ] Acquisition and cost basis records to the extent available
- [ ] Documentation of transfers before death (to identify gifts or loans)
- [ ] Executor's attestation of the asset inventory
- [ ] Qualified appraisal report for inclusion in the estate's records and the return

### Gift Tax (Form 709)

Gifts of digital assets are valued at Fair Market Value on the date of the gift.

- [ ] Gift date and transfer records (transaction hash, timestamp)
- [ ] Documentation of what was transferred, from and to whom
- [ ] Market evidence as of the gift date
- [ ] Basis records for the donor (relevant for future disposition)
- [ ] Annual exclusion analysis documentation

### Charitable Contributions (Form 8283 / IRS Section 170)

Donations of digital assets exceeding $5,000 require a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser, with Form 8283 attached to the return.

- [ ] Donation date and the donee organization's name
- [ ] Transfer records documenting the contribution
- [ ] The qualified appraisal, completed no earlier than 60 days before the contribution date
- [ ] Form 8283, with the appraiser's information and signature
- [ ] Donee acknowledgment for contributions over $250
- [ ] Documentation of the asset's cost basis and holding period

### Self-Directed IRA Annual Attestation

SDIRA custodians require an annual fair market value for digital assets held in the account.

- [ ] Current holdings statement from the custodian
- [ ] Wallet and platform records for the account's assets
- [ ] Valuation-date market evidence
- [ ] Prior year's valuation, for consistency
- [ ] A USPAP-compliant appraisal for illiquid or hard-to-value assets (NFTs, private token positions)

### Audit Defense

When the IRS has questioned a valuation, documentation is everything.

- [ ] All records from the original engagement (the workfile, if available)
- [ ] Complete transaction and ownership documentation
- [ ] The appraiser's report and credentials
- [ ] The appraiser's availability for examination support
- [ ] A clear timeline of the valuation and the events around it

## Common Mistakes That Delay or Invalidate Appraisals

These are the failures we see most often — and every one of them is avoidable.

### 1. Incomplete Wallet Discovery

Clients routinely forget wallets: an old hardware wallet in a drawer, a forgotten exchange account, a wallet created for a single transaction years ago. An incomplete inventory produces an appraisal that covers only part of the holdings — which can be worse than no appraisal, because it creates a false record. For estates, the failure to discover wallets is the single most common cause of later complications.

### 2. Missing Cost Basis Records

The most common gap. Clients who bought crypto years ago on an exchange that no longer provides reports, or who moved assets between wallets without records, often cannot document basis. The appraiser can work with secondary evidence and reconstruction, but the report must disclose the limitation — and the client's tax exposure may be higher as a result.

### 3. Screenshots Instead of Statements

Price screenshots are not evidence of holdings. They are not timestamped by any authoritative source, and they prove nothing about ownership. Clients who bring only screenshots will be sent back for real records — and the clock starts over.

### 4. Unrecorded Wallet-to-Wallet Transfers

Assets that move between wallets without documentation create gaps the appraiser cannot bridge. The chain of custody must be reconstructable. Clients should be advised to keep transfer records — at minimum, the transaction hashes — from the moment they first acquire digital assets.

### 5. Ignoring Off-Exchange Holdings

Exchange statements capture only what is on the exchange. Self-custodied holdings — often the majority for serious holders — exist only on-chain. An appraisal based on exchange statements alone understates the position.

### 6. Waiting Until the Filing Deadline

A qualified appraisal cannot be responsibly completed in 48 hours for a complex portfolio. NFT collections, DeFi positions, and estate inventories take time to document and analyze. Attorneys and CPAs who engage the appraiser at the planning stage — not the filing stage — protect their clients and their own deadlines.

### 7. Confusing Tax Software Reports with Appraisals

Crypto tax software produces transaction reports and gain-loss calculations. Those are valuable records — but they are not qualified appraisals. They contain no methodology documentation, no appraiser credentials, and no USPAP compliance. Presenting them as appraisal support invites challenge.

### 8. Overlooking Foreign Platforms

Clients with assets on foreign exchanges face additional documentation challenges: statements in other languages, different reporting standards, and sometimes unavailable historical data. These should be identified early.

## A Practical Intake Workflow for Attorneys and CPAs

Turning this into practice, here is the workflow we recommend for any professional preparing a client for a digital asset appraisal.

**Step 1 — Identify the purpose.** Establish the engagement type (estate, gift, donation, SDIRA, audit defense) and the valuation date. The purpose determines the checklist.

**Step 2 — Inventory the assets.** Have the client list every wallet, exchange account, custodial account, and known asset — including forgotten and dormant ones. Use prior tax returns as a cross-check.

**Step 3 — Request the document package.** Send the client the relevant checklist above. Make clear that primary evidence is required, and that screenshots are not sufficient.

**Step 4 — Verify the package.** Review the package for completeness before engaging the appraiser. This single step prevents the most common delays.

**Step 5 — Engage the qualified appraiser.** Provide the appraiser with the complete package, the engagement purpose, and the valuation date. Confirm the appraiser's qualifications: USPAP compliance, relevant digital asset methodology, and, where applicable, qualified appraiser status under IRS Section 170.

**Step 6 — Preserve everything.** Advise the client to retain all records indefinitely, and to preserve them in a form that will survive — offline backups, printed statements, and a documented handoff plan for the next of kin.

**Step 7 — Reconcile with the tax return.** For donations, ensure Form 8283 is completed and attached. For estates and gifts, ensure the valuation is reflected on the appropriate return with the appraisal retained in the file.

This workflow converts a vague "we need an appraisal" into a managed process. It is the difference between a referral that strengthens your client relationship and one that creates risk.

## Working with a Qualified Appraiser: Engagement Expectations

A few expectations will make the engagement smooth.

**Timing.** A straightforward single-asset appraisal can be completed relatively quickly. A complex estate with multiple wallets, NFT collections, and DeFi positions requires weeks. Engage early — at the planning stage, not the deadline.

**Cost.** Appraisal fees reflect asset complexity, portfolio size, and report type. Custom quotes are standard. The cost of a qualified appraisal is a small fraction of the penalty exposure it prevents: accuracy-related penalties under IRC Section 6662 can reach 20 to 40 percent of the underpayment.

**Disclosure.** A professional appraiser will ask questions the client may find uncomfortable — about lost keys, undisclosed wallets, or previous valuations. That questioning is the sign of a serious professional. The report will disclose material limitations, and those disclosures are what make the opinion defensible.

**Ongoing support.** The appraisal does not end at the report. The appraiser should be prepared to support the valuation in examination — to explain the methodology, the evidence, and the conclusions. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What documents do I need for a digital asset appraisal?

At minimum: wallet addresses and ownership attestation, exchange and custodial account statements, acquisition records and cost basis documentation, complete transaction logs, and transfer records. The precise list depends on the asset types and the engagement purpose — estates, gifts, donations, and SDIRA attestations each have additional requirements.

### What is the difference between a qualified appraisal and an exchange statement?

An exchange statement is a record of transactions and balances at one platform. A qualified appraisal is a professional opinion of Fair Market Value, prepared by a qualified appraiser in accordance with USPAP, supported by documented methodology and evidence, and signed with professional certification. The IRS requires the latter for substantiation in charitable contributions over $5,000, estates, gifts, and other contexts where a defensible valuation is required.

### How long must appraisal records be kept under USPAP?

The USPAP Record Keeping Rule requires the appraiser's workfile to be retained for a minimum of five years after preparation. In practice, records should be kept longer: IRS examinations, estate administration, and litigation can extend far beyond five years, and the documents supporting a valuation should be preserved for as long as the tax position is open.

### Does the IRS require a qualified appraisal for crypto donations over $5,000?

Yes. Under IRS Section 170(f)(11), a charitable contribution deduction exceeding $5,000 for property — including cryptocurrency — requires a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser, with Form 8283 attached to the return. The IRS confirmed in CCA 202302012 that exchange-reported value does not substitute for a qualified appraisal.

### What documents does an appraiser need to value an NFT?

Mint records, purchase confirmations, the NFT's contract address and token ID, collection and trait data with dates, historical sales records for comparable items, and provenance documentation. For 1-of-1 works, the appraiser will also consider the artist, the market context, and any prior sales or offers.

### Can screenshots of exchange prices support an appraisal?

Not on their own. Screenshots have no verifiable metadata chain, no authoritative timestamp, and no proof of ownership. They can serve as clues, but the appraiser must verify the underlying facts through primary evidence: exchange statements, on-chain data tied to verified wallet addresses, and custody records.

### What happens if my client cannot provide cost basis records?

The appraiser can work with secondary evidence and documented reconstruction, but the report will disclose the limitation, and the client's tax exposure may increase. Where possible, primary evidence should be reconstructed first: exchange statements, trade fills, and on-chain data tied to verified wallets. Clients should be advised to preserve records from the moment of first acquisition.

### What should an attorney or CPA gather before referring a client for a digital asset appraisal?

Confirm the engagement purpose and valuation date, inventory all wallets and accounts, request the full document package with primary evidence, verify completeness, and engage the qualified appraiser early — before filing deadlines. Preserve all records and reconcile the appraisal with the appropriate tax return.
