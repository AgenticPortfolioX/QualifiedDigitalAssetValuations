# Who Values What a Trustee Holds: The Duty to Marshal Digital Assets, the Michigan Prudent Investor Rule's Treatment of a Concentrated Crypto Position, the Principal-and-Income Adjustment Power That Only Functions With a Valuation, and the Fiduciary Accounting That Turns Volatility Into a Surcharge Claim

## The Asset With No Statement

A trustee who takes office holding a brokerage account receives a statement on the first business day: positions, cost basis, and a value as of the prior close. The number is the beginning of the fiduciary analysis, not a substitute for it.

A trustee who takes office holding a self-custodied digital asset position receives nothing. There is no custodian to issue a statement, no transfer agent to confirm a balance, no regulated intermediary obligated to report a value. There is a blockchain, which will report a balance to anyone holding an address, and a market price, which will report a per-unit trading level. Neither is an appraisal. Neither answers what fiduciary law actually asks: what a specific position, held under specific restrictions, by a specific fiduciary, is worth on a specific date.

That distinction is where fiduciary administration of digital assets goes wrong, quietly. The trustee is not accused of self-dealing. The trustee is accused of treating a trading price as though it settled three questions it does not settle: whether the asset should have been retained, how the year's return should have been divided between income and remainder beneficiaries, and what the annual accounting should have reported. Each question has its own standard. Each consumes a valuation.

## Three Duties, One Missing Number

Three duties attach the moment a fiduciary accepts appointment, and all three converge on a number nobody has produced.

The first is the duty to identify and take control of trust property. A fiduciary cannot administer an asset it cannot locate, or account for an asset it cannot quantify. For conventional property, identification and quantification arrive together, because the custodian's statement does both. For a self-custodied position, identification means discovering that a wallet exists and locating the recovery material; quantification is a separate exercise performed against a chain and a market.

The second is the duty to review at inception and decide. Under MCL 700.1505, within a reasonable time after accepting appointment or receiving fiduciary assets, a fiduciary must review the assets and "make and implement decisions concerning the retention and disposition of assets," bringing the portfolio into compliance with the governing instrument and the Michigan prudent investor rule. Retention is not a passive state. It is a decision, made on a clock that started before anyone obtained a value.

The third is the duty to account. Under MCL 700.7814(3), a trustee must send beneficiaries, at least annually and at termination, a report of trust property, liabilities, receipts, disbursements, and compensation, including a listing of the trust property and, "if feasible, their respective market values." The statute does not say "their respective prices." It says market values, qualified by a feasibility standard that presupposes the trustee has thought about what is feasible to determine and how.

A fiduciary that has not obtained a defensible valuation cannot demonstrate compliance with any of the three, because each is measured against a value the fiduciary never established.

## Authority, Ability, and Proof

The law governing fiduciary access to digital assets was not written with self-custody in mind.

Most states have enacted a version of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, giving a fiduciary a path to "electronic records" held by a custodian. The architecture is a petition: the fiduciary presents authority under the governing instrument or a court order, and the custodian is permitted, or sometimes directed, to disclose. On a centralized exchange, that architecture works.

Where the asset sits in a hardware or software wallet controlled by a recovery phrase, there is no custodian. No petition, no form, no reset. A court order addressed to a decentralized network is a document with no addressee. This is not a gap in enforcement; it is the absence of a party against whom enforcement could run.

The consequence is that a fiduciary holds three different things, and conflating them is the most common planning failure:

- **Authority** — the legal power to act, granted by the governing instrument, a statute, or a court.
- **Ability** — the technical capacity to move the asset, which requires the key material and nothing else.
- **Proof** — evidence sufficient to establish, to a court, a beneficiary, or an examiner, that the asset exists, that it belongs to the trust, and what it is worth.

A trustee can have authority without ability, and ability without proof: the successor holds the seed phrase and can move funds, but nothing in the file establishes that the wallet was trust property. Only a contemporaneous inventory — wallet, address, chain, acquisition history, custody arrangement, signed while the person with knowledge is available — supplies the third.

## The Clock Starts Before Anyone Has Valued the Asset

MCL 700.1505's "reasonable time" imposes a deadline without specifying it, and measures compliance from the moment of receipt rather than the moment of understanding.

Consider a typical administration. The fiduciary accepts appointment, receives a wallet device and a written recovery phrase, and confirms a balance. What the fiduciary does not yet have is any basis for a retention decision: no acquisition history, no cost basis, no information about whether the position is staked, lent, or subject to a vesting schedule, and no view on whether the instrument speaks to digital assets at all. Obtaining those facts takes weeks. The statutory clock is already running.

Two errors follow, pulling in opposite directions. The first is premature liquidation: a fiduciary who sells immediately because prudence means diversification converts a concentrated position into cash, triggers whatever gain is embedded in it, and forecloses the analysis the statute requires. MCL 700.1503(2)(c) requires consideration of expected tax consequences, and for a low-basis position that cost is material, so a sale undertaken without quantifying it is not obviously more prudent than retention. The second is indefinite drift: a fiduciary that never decides, because the asset is complicated and the beneficiaries are not complaining, has failed the affirmative obligation outright.

The defensible path is documentary rather than decisional: record the date the assets were received, when each material fact was obtained, the retention determination and its basis, and who made it. Under MCL 700.1509, compliance is judged "in light of the facts and circumstances that exist at the time of a fiduciary's decision or action, and not by hindsight," and the rule "requires a standard of conduct, not outcome or performance." That protects a documented process, and does nothing for an undocumented one.

## The Prudent Investor Rule Reads the Portfolio, Not the Coin

The Michigan rule excludes no asset class. MCL 700.1503(4) provides that a fiduciary "may invest in any kind of property or type of investment consistent with the standards of the Michigan prudent investor rule," and that "a particular investment is not inherently prudent or imprudent." Digital assets are therefore neither permitted nor forbidden by category. They are permitted or forbidden by role.

That role is determined at the portfolio level. MCL 700.1503(1) requires that decisions about individual assets "be evaluated not in isolation, but rather in the context of the fiduciary estate portfolio as a whole and as a part of an overall investment strategy having risk and return objectives reasonably suited to the fiduciary estate." The question is never whether Bitcoin is a prudent investment. It is whether a position of this size, in this portfolio, for these beneficiaries, under this instrument, serves the trust's objectives.

MCL 700.1503(2) then lists circumstances the fiduciary must consider, and three are valuation problems in disguise. Subsection (2)(c) requires expected tax consequences — unquantifiable without basis, holding period, and current value. Subsection (2)(e) requires the expected total return from income and capital appreciation — for a non-distributing asset, the entire return is an estimate that cannot be stated without a value at both ends of the period. Subsection (2)(h) requires consideration of an asset's "special relationship or special value" to the purposes of the estate — the provision a fiduciary invokes when retention is right.

Then comes the sentence tying the rule to appraisal. MCL 700.1503(3): "A fiduciary shall make a reasonable effort to verify facts relevant to the investment and management of fiduciary assets." Not may. Not should consider. The statute imposes an affirmative verification obligation, and the market value of the largest, least liquid, most volatile asset in the portfolio is a relevant fact by any reading. MCL 700.1503(5) adds that a fiduciary with special skill or expertise, or named in reliance on a representation of special skill, has a duty to use it.

## Diversification Is a Default, Not a Suggestion

MCL 700.1504 is one sentence, and it is the sentence most digital asset trusts will be litigated against: "A fiduciary shall diversify the investments of a fiduciary estate unless the fiduciary reasonably determines that, because of special circumstances, the purposes of the fiduciary estate are better served without diversifying."

Diversification is the requirement. Non-diversification is the exception, available only on a reasonable determination that special circumstances exist and that the purposes of the estate are better served without diversifying. Three elements, each a documentation requirement rather than a state of mind.

The controlling authority outside the digital asset context is *Estate of Janes*, where the New York Court of Appeals held a fiduciary liable for failing to diversify a large, concentrated position in a single publicly traded stock. The holding is not that concentration is per se imprudent. It is that the fiduciary must analyze the concentration, consider the consequences of retaining it, and act on a reasoned conclusion — and that a fiduciary who simply holds what was handed to it, without analysis, has not met the standard. The holding involves a legacy industrial position rather than a token, but the analytical structure transfers without modification.

Four points follow. First, "the settlor wanted it kept" is not self-proving. Where the instrument contains an express retention clause naming the asset or asset class, MCL 700.1502(2) matters: the prudent investor rule is a default that may be expanded, restricted, eliminated, or otherwise altered by the governing instrument, and a fiduciary is not liable to the extent it acted in reasonable reliance on the instrument. An express, specific retention direction is powerful. A general power to retain "any property" is much weaker. Silence is not a direction at all.

Second, the determination must be contemporaneous. A conclusion reached after the position has fallen by two-thirds, explaining why retention was always appropriate, is not a determination but a rationalization — and MCL 700.1509's bar on hindsight cuts against the fiduciary here, because the test is the facts at the time of the decision.

Third, tax cost belongs in the analysis under MCL 700.1503(2)(c) but does not justify indefinite retention of a position large enough to dominate the portfolio. Fourth, partial diversification is a real option: a staged reduction or a sale sufficient to bring the position within a defensible band responds to concentration without forcing the entire gain, and a file that analyzes only full retention and full liquidation has not analyzed the problem.

## Impartiality: The Beneficiary Who Gets Nothing This Year

The duty volatility breaks most visibly is impartiality, and it is the one most often overlooked because it does not appear in the investment statutes at all.

Section 103 of the Michigan Uniform Principal and Income Act (MCL 555.503) requires a fiduciary to administer a trust or estate impartially, based on what is fair and reasonable to all beneficiaries, except to the extent the instrument clearly manifests a different intent. That duty sits alongside, and partially in tension with, the prudent investor rule's instruction to maximize total return.

The tension is structural. An income beneficiary receives what the instrument defines as income — traditionally interest, dividends, and rents. A digital asset that produces no distribution produces no income. Every year the position is held, the income beneficiary receives nothing from it while the remainder interest compounds: if the position doubles, the remainder beneficiary's expectancy doubles and the income beneficiary's receipt is unchanged. If it halves, the remainder beneficiary absorbs the loss and the receipt is still unchanged — which is why the income beneficiary's complaint tends to arrive in the years the asset rises.

The traditional answer was to invest for income, which is precisely what the prudent investor rule was written to end. The modern answer is total return: invest for the best overall result, then allocate so neither class is unfairly advantaged. That answer requires a mechanism, and the mechanism requires a value.

There is no way to determine whether an income beneficiary has been treated impartially without knowing what the principal produced, and no way to determine whether a remainder beneficiary has been treated impartially without knowing what the principal is worth at both ends of the period. A trustee reporting that the position "increased in value" has stated an observation. The duty requires a comparison, and a comparison requires two valuations on a consistent basis.

## The Adjustment Power, and the Four Conditions

The Michigan Uniform Principal and Income Act (Act 159 of 2004) supplies the mechanism. MCL 555.504, tracking Section 104 of the uniform act, gives a fiduciary a power to adjust between principal and income — but the power is conditional, and the conditions are the whole analysis.

Under MCL 555.504(1), a fiduciary may adjust "to the extent the fiduciary considers necessary" only if all three of the following hold:

1. The fiduciary invests and manages trust or estate assets as a prudent investor.
2. The terms of the trust or will describe the amount that may or must be distributed to a beneficiary by reference to the trust's or estate's income.
3. The fiduciary determines, after applying Section 103(1) and (2), that it is unable to comply with Section 103(3) — the impartiality requirement.

The third condition is operative. The power is not a general license to reallocate returns. It exists to resolve an inability to satisfy impartiality. A fiduciary that has not first determined it cannot comply impartially has no power to adjust, because the precondition has not been met.

Once the precondition is satisfied, MCL 555.504(2) requires the fiduciary to consider "all factors relevant to the trust or estate and its beneficiaries." The uniform act's factor list is almost entirely a valuation checklist: the nature, purpose, and expected duration of the trust; the settlor's intent; the identity and circumstances of the beneficiaries; needs for liquidity, regularity of income, and preservation of capital; the assets held and whether each was purchased by the trustee or received from the settlor; the net amount allocated to income and the change in principal value, "which the trustee may estimate as to assets for which market values are not readily available"; invasion and accumulation powers and their exercise; economic conditions, inflation, and deflation; and the anticipated tax consequences of an adjustment.

Two factors are written directly at the digital asset problem. The statute contemplates estimated principal values where market values are not readily available, which means the drafters expected fiduciaries to confront assets without a reliable quoted price and expected them to estimate rather than abstain. And the distinction between an asset purchased by the trustee and one received from the settlor recognizes that a fiduciary inheriting a concentrated position deserves more latitude than one that built the concentration itself.

MCL 555.504(3) adds a tool professional fiduciaries underuse: a written policy, applying to all trusts or defined classes, stating whether and under what conditions the adjustment power will be exercised. A published policy converts discretionary judgments into a reviewable practice.

## Why the Power to Adjust Is Missing Where It Is Needed Most

MCL 555.504(4) lists eight circumstances in which no adjustment may be made, and the list is where planning collides with statutory design. An adjustment is prohibited if it diminishes the income interest in a marital deduction trust requiring all income to be paid annually to a spouse; reduces the actuarial value of an income interest funded to qualify for a gift tax exclusion; changes an amount payable as a fixed annuity or fixed fraction of asset value; diminishes an amount permanently set aside for charitable purposes unless both income and principal are so set aside; triggers grantor-trust or estate-inclusion treatment that would not otherwise apply; is made where the fiduciary is a beneficiary; or benefits the fiduciary directly or indirectly.

The consequence is counterintuitive. The marital deduction trust — a QTIP arrangement in which the surviving spouse must receive all income annually — is simultaneously the trust most exposed to the income/remainder mismatch when funded with a non-distributing asset, and the trust in which the adjustment power is flatly unavailable. The statute resolves the tension in favor of the marital deduction, because an adjustment diminishing the spouse's income interest could jeopardize the deduction itself. The fiduciary is left with the impartiality duty, no adjustment power, and, if the trust holds only a non-distributing digital asset, no lawful mechanism for producing income at all.

The beneficiary and self-benefit prohibitions create a parallel trap for the individual trustee: a co-fiduciary to whom the prohibition does not apply may act, but only if the instrument does not forbid it, and MCL 555.504(6) permits the fiduciary to release the power where possessing it threatens a tax benefit.

The planning implication is direct. A trust that will hold digital assets and serve a current income beneficiary should be drafted with an express allocation mechanism — a unitrust definition, a specific power to allocate capital gain to income, or an explicit grant of the adjustment power with the disqualifying circumstances addressed — rather than relying on the default statutory power, which may not be available when it is needed.

## Two Allocations, One Number

The fiduciary's allocation problem is not one problem. It is two, running in parallel, governed by different bodies of law, both consuming the same valuation.

The first is **fiduciary accounting income**, determined under state law and the governing instrument. It decides what the income beneficiary is entitled to receive, and for a non-distributing asset it is frequently zero. The second is **distributable net income** under IRC Section 643(a), which decides how much of a distribution is deductible by the trust under Section 661 and includible by the beneficiary under Section 662. DNI is a tax construct, and it does not track fiduciary accounting income.

| Question | Fiduciary accounting income | Distributable net income |
|---|---|---|
| Governing authority | State principal and income act; instrument | IRC Section 643(a); Treasury regulations |
| What it determines | Amount payable to the income beneficiary | Deduction under Section 661; inclusion under Section 662 |
| Treatment of capital gain | Allocated to principal unless the instrument or statute provides otherwise | Excluded unless allocated to income or actually distributed to a beneficiary |
| Treatment of unrealized appreciation | None; appreciation is not income | None; unrealized gain is not in DNI |
| Effect of a non-distributing digital asset | Zero income to the income beneficiary | Nothing to distribute; no deduction, no inclusion |
| Effect of a sale at a gain | Gain typically to principal | Gain generally outside DNI, so the trust bears the tax |

Read the last two rows together, because they describe the trap. A trust holding an appreciated digital asset that produces no distributions generates no DNI and therefore no distribution deduction. If the trustee sells and realizes a large gain, that gain is generally allocated to principal and excluded from DNI, so it is taxed to the trust — which reaches the top marginal rate at a level of taxable income a single year's realized gain will exceed many times over.

That is the mechanical reason a fiduciary considering diversification must model the tax consequence before acting, and the reason MCL 700.1503(2)(c) requires it. It is also why the 65-day rule matters at the margin. Under IRC Section 663(b) and Treas. Reg. §1.663(b)-1, a complex trust or an estate may elect to treat distributions made within 65 days after year end as paid on the last day of that year, limited to the greater of DNI or taxable income. The election shifts income onto beneficiaries who may be in lower brackets — but it is unavailable to a simple trust, and it cannot create DNI where the trust has none.

Two provisions complete the picture. IRC Section 642(c) allows a deduction for amounts paid from gross income to a charitable beneficiary under the governing instrument, with the set-aside rule of Section 642(c)(2) confined to trusts created before 1969. IRC Section 645 permits a qualified revocable trust to elect treatment as part of the decedent's estate.

Every one of these determinations takes a value as an input. A trustee with no defensible value is making tax elections and distribution decisions on an unstated assumption.

## What the Fiduciary Engagement Must State

A valuation obtained for fiduciary purposes is not the same instrument as one obtained for a charitable deduction, and the difference is in the scope language rather than the conclusion.

The appraisal must state the **valuation date**, and the date is dictated by purpose. Date of death matters under IRC Section 2031 and Treas. Reg. §20.2031-1(b), where fair market value is the price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither under compulsion, both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts. A distribution date matters for a distribution in kind, a fiscal year end for the annual accounting under MCL 700.7814(3), and a date selected for an adjustment analysis because the determination is made as of a moment. One appraisal cannot serve four dates.

The appraisal must state the **standard of value and premise**. Fair market value on a going-concern basis, fair market value in liquidation, and fair value for judicial dissolution are three different numbers for the same token on the same day.

The appraisal must address **position size and market impact**, because a quoted price is a marginal price. A position large enough that liquidation would move the market is not worth its quoted price multiplied by quantity, and the discount is a matter of evidence — depth, average daily volume, realistic execution period — not assertion. The same analysis governs restricted, staked, lent, or vesting positions, where the quoted price describes an asset the trust cannot presently sell.

The appraisal must also state its **methodology**, disclose **extraordinary assumptions and limiting conditions** — most commonly that the appraiser has not verified control of the private keys and is valuing on the assumption that the trust has or can obtain access — and establish the appraiser's **independence** in the engagement, with no fee contingent on the conclusion. If the access assumption is false, the conclusion is false, and the fiduciary holds the report that says so.

## The Annual Report Asks for Market Value

Return to MCL 700.7814(3). The trustee must provide a report of trust property, liabilities, receipts, disbursements, trustee compensation, a listing of the trust property, and, "if feasible, their respective market values."

For most assets, market value is a data pull. For a digital asset position, the trustee faces a choice it rarely articulates: report the last traded price times quantity, or report a market value reflecting the position as held.

The two are the same for a small position in a deep market. They diverge for a large position, for one subject to lockup or vesting, for a staked position subject to an unbonding period, for a token with thin liquidity, and for any position where the trust's ability to sell is constrained by something other than the market. In each case the quoted price overstates what the trust could realize.

There is a defensible middle path: report the quoted value, identify the basis on which it was computed, and disclose the constraint where one exists. A beneficiary alleging that the accounting overstated the position has a harder case against a report that said, in terms, "valued at quoted market price; position exceeds ten percent of average daily volume; no discount applied." A bare number with no stated basis invites the inference that the trustee never asked the question.

## Surcharge Is a Process Failure

A surcharge claim for digital asset losses is rarely a claim that the asset fell. Assets fall. The claim is that the fiduciary failed to do something the statute required, and that the failure is visible in the absence of a record.

Three failure patterns recur. **No inception review:** MCL 700.1505 requires review and decision within a reasonable time, and a file with no dated retention analysis has no answer to when the review happened. **No diversification analysis:** MCL 700.1504 permits non-diversification only on a reasonable determination of special circumstances, and a file with no written determination has not made one. **No allocation analysis:** where an income beneficiary received nothing for years while the remainder interest grew, the fiduciary needs a record showing it identified the mismatch, considered the adjustment power, and either exercised it or documented why it could not.

Against those patterns, MCL 700.1509 is both the fiduciary's best provision and its least useful one. Compliance is judged on the facts at the time of the decision, not by hindsight, and the rule requires a standard of conduct rather than a particular outcome. That protects a fiduciary that analyzed the concentration, quantified the tax cost, considered partial reduction, documented the determination, and then watched the asset fall. It offers nothing to a fiduciary that did none of those things and happened to be right.

The asymmetry is the lesson: good outcomes do not cure bad process, and bad outcomes do not defeat good process. The file is the defense, and it is built contemporaneously or not at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a trustee have to diversify a concentrated cryptocurrency position?

Under MCL 700.1504, a fiduciary must diversify unless it reasonably determines that, because of special circumstances, the purposes of the estate are better served without diversifying. Diversification is the default; departure requires a documented, contemporaneous determination addressing the three elements of the exception.

### Is the current market price an acceptable trust valuation?

For a small position in a liquid market, a quoted price is a reasonable measure of market value. For a position large enough to move the market, or one subject to staking, lending, vesting, or lockup restrictions, the quoted price overstates what the trust could realize. Obtain a valuation reflecting the position as held, or disclose the basis and the constraint in the accounting.

### Can a trustee shift value between principal and income when a digital asset produces no income?

Only if the conditions of MCL 555.504(1) are met: the fiduciary invests as a prudent investor, the instrument defines distributions by reference to income, and the fiduciary determines it cannot comply with the impartiality requirement after applying Section 103(1) and (2). Even then, the eight prohibitions in MCL 555.504(4) may foreclose the adjustment — notably in marital deduction trusts.

### What happens if the trustee cannot obtain access to a self-custodied wallet?

There is no custodian to petition and no administrative reset. RUFADAA reaches electronic records held by a custodian, and a self-custodied wallet has none. The trustee's remedies are forensic recovery of key material, a court determination of ownership where access can later be established, and an accounting disclosing the position as inaccessible. Prevention is the only reliable remedy.

### How is a gain on the sale of trust-held cryptocurrency taxed?

Realized capital gain is generally allocated to principal under state law and excluded from distributable net income, so the trust bears the tax at compressed trust rates. A complex trust or estate may elect under IRC Section 663(b) to treat distributions within 65 days after year end as made on the last day of the year, but only to the extent of the greater of DNI or taxable income.

### When should a fiduciary engage an appraiser for a digital asset position?

At the point the fiduciary must make a decision the statute measures against a value: the inception review under MCL 700.1505, a diversification determination under MCL 700.1504, an adjustment analysis under MCL 555.504, a distribution in kind, and each annual accounting under MCL 700.7814(3). Engaging after the decision produces a report that documents a conclusion rather than supporting one.

## The Number the Trustee Signs

Fiduciary administration of digital assets is usually described as an access problem. It is not. Access is the threshold, and planning solves it. The problem that remains is that fiduciary law measures everything a trustee does against a value, and the digital asset world supplies prices rather than values.

Three duties consume that number: the duty to review and decide at inception, which runs on a clock that starts before the number exists; the duty to diversify, satisfied by a documented determination rather than a market outcome; and the duty to account, which asks for market values and receives, too often, a multiplication.

The trustee who gets this right does not predict the market and does not need to. The trustee documents what it knew, when it knew it, what it concluded, and on what evidence — and obtains that evidence from an appraiser whose scope matches the legal question being answered.
