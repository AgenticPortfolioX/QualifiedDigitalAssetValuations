# Retaining a Digital Asset Appraiser: Engagement Letters, Scope of Work, Independence, and Privilege for Attorneys and CPAs
*Published: September 25, 2026 | By QDAV*
---
## Why the Retention Decision Is the Valuation Decision

By the time an appraisal report exists, most of the outcome has already been determined. The valuation effective date was set in the engagement letter. The scope was fixed — or left vague — before any work began. The fee structure either preserved the appraiser's qualification to sign or quietly destroyed it. The privilege posture was settled the moment someone decided who would be the client of record and what would be written in an email.

Attorneys and CPAs who engage appraisers for digital assets are not buying a document. They are designing an assignment. The difference matters because digital asset valuations are examined under two frameworks at once: the appraisal profession's standards, which govern how the appraiser works, and the Internal Revenue Code and Treasury Regulations, which govern whether the resulting report satisfies a filing obligation. A defect in either framework is not curable at the report stage.

This guide is written for the professionals who retain appraisers — estate attorneys, tax counsel, CPAs, fiduciary officers, and litigation teams that need a defensible valuation opinion. It sets out what the engagement must accomplish, what it must never contain, and where the lines of privilege actually fall when a report is destined for a government file.

## Who Is the Client, and Who Are the Intended Users?

USPAP requires the appraiser to identify the client — the party who engages the appraiser — and separately to identify the intended users, the parties for whom the assignment results are intended. These are not the same thing, and the distinction has practical consequences for confidentiality, for how much work the appraiser performs, and sometimes for privilege.

Three retention models dominate digital asset practice.

| Retention model | Client of record | Typical intended users | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Client retains directly** | The taxpayer, donor, estate, or fiduciary | The client; the CPA; the attorney; the IRS and state taxing authorities as filing recipients | Simplest and least expensive; no privilege overlay; the report belongs to the client and will be produced when filed |
| **Attorney retains as agent** | The attorney's client, with the attorney as the engaging party and point of contact | The client; counsel; any testifying role the appraiser may later assume | Supports a Kovel-style protection claim over counsel's communications with the appraiser; the appraiser's own report and workfile remain producible |
| **Joint or multi-party retention** | Two or more parties (for example, co-trustees, spouses, or an estate and a beneficiary) | All retaining parties; their advisors | Requires an express allocation of confidentiality, authority to direct the assignment, and responsibility for the fee in the engagement letter |

Two mistakes recur here. The first is failing to state intended users at all. Intended users drive scope: a valuation for internal planning may reasonably use a narrower scope than one prepared for a Form 8283 Section B charitable deduction that will be attached to a return under IRS Section 170 and, in the ordinary course, reviewed. If the appraiser does not know the IRS is an intended user, the scope may be insufficient to produce a credible result for the purpose it serves — a defect that surfaces exactly when it is least affordable.

The second mistake is assuming the model is invisible. It is not. Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17(a)(3)(iv) requires the appraisal to disclose the appraiser's name, address, and taxpayer identification number, and — where the appraiser is acting as a partner, an employee, or an independent contractor engaged by someone other than the donor — the name, address, and TIN of the party who engaged the appraiser. An attorney-directed engagement is therefore disclosed on the face of the document that goes to the IRS. Practitioners who retain an appraiser expressly to keep their own role out of the file have misunderstood the regulation.

## The Engagement Letter: A Clause-by-Clause Specification

An engagement letter for a digital asset appraisal should be read as the assignment's specification. Each clause below corresponds to a decision that is difficult or impossible to change after work begins.

| Clause | What it must establish | Failure mode if omitted or vague |
|---|---|---|
| Parties and capacity | Who is the client; the capacity in which the retaining party acts; whether the appraiser is engaged by the donor, the fiduciary, or counsel | Ambiguity over who may direct the assignment and who receives the report |
| Assignment identification | The specific property or positions to be valued, by asset, quantity, and wallet or account identifier | Scope creep; disputed asset population at examination |
| Intended use | The filing obligation or proceeding the report supports — charitable deduction, estate tax return, gift tax return, income tax reporting, SDIRA attestation, litigation | Appraiser cannot confirm the scope is sufficient for the use |
| Intended users | Named parties the appraiser is addressing, including the client, named advisors, and any filing recipient | Scope calibrated to the wrong audience |
| Definition of value | Express statement of Fair Market Value under the applicable standard, with the governing language quoted or cited | Standard-of-value drift between valuation approaches |
| Valuation effective date | The single date to which the opinion applies, stated to the day | Retro-fitted dates; in charitable matters, a report that cannot be reconciled with §1.170A-17(a)(5) |
| Scope of work | The approaches to be developed or excluded, the market evidence to be gathered, and any limitation, expressly disclosed | Unsupported conclusions; undisclosed departure from expected practice |
| Data and representations | What the client will provide, in what form, and the requirement of written representations | Unverified inputs; later disputes over what the appraiser was told |
| Digital asset access protocol | Verification of holdings without custody, and an express prohibition on sharing private keys or seed phrases | Breach exposure and a compromised independence narrative |
| Assumptions and limiting conditions | Stated assumptions, and what happens if they prove false | Silent assumptions that the examiner will find first |
| Reporting format | The report type and whether a signed certification is included | Non-compliant submissions |
| Fee structure | Fixed, hourly, asset-count, or complexity-based compensation — never measured by the value conclusion | Disqualification under §1.170A-17(a)(9) |
| Reliance and re-use | Who may rely on the report, for what purpose, and whether third-party reliance is permitted | Unauthorized reliance claims; unintended distribution |
| Confidentiality | What the appraiser may and may not disclose, recognizing that filed documents and workfiles are not confidential | False expectations of confidentiality |
| Certification and signature | Confirmation that the appraiser will sign and date the report and provide the required declarations | Unusable appraisal |

The valuation effective date deserves separate attention because it is the clause most often negotiated incorrectly. For a charitable contribution, §1.170A-17(a)(4) requires the report to be signed and dated no earlier than 60 days before the contribution and no later than the return due date including extensions. Section 1.170A-17(a)(5)(ii) then fixes the date to which the value applies: if the report is dated before the contribution, the valuation effective date must fall between 60 days before the contribution and the contribution date; if the report is dated on or after the contribution, the valuation effective date must be the contribution date itself. A report cannot be commissioned in March and dated to a February contribution value.

Other timing rules follow the same logic. An estate tax valuation under IRC Section 2032 involves two dates, and the engagement must scope both or the alternate-date comparison cannot be made. A gift tax valuation must be measured with reference to each separate gift. A valuation prepared for litigation is usually fixed to a date set by the dispute, not by the engagement. In every case, the date that governs the opinion should appear in the engagement letter before the appraiser starts work, because an appraiser who has already formed a view on a different date has an independence problem she should not have been put in.

## The Fee Clause: The Prohibited-Fee Rule

This is the provision that most often comes as a surprise. In appraisals supporting charitable deductions, the fee itself is a qualification requirement.

Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17(a)(9) states the rule without qualification: "The fee for a qualified appraisal cannot be based to any extent on the appraised value of the property." The regulation then supplies the operative example: a fee is treated as based on the appraised value "if any part of the fee depends on the amount of the appraised value that is allowed by the Internal Revenue Service after an examination."

The disqualification is personal. Section 1.170A-17(b)(5)(i) lists, as an individual who is not a qualified appraiser for the appraised property, any individual who receives a fee prohibited by paragraph (a)(9). A percentage-of-value fee, a success-based fee, or an arrangement under which part of the compensation depends on the deduction ultimately allowed does not merely taint the invoice; it removes the appraiser's ability to sign a qualified appraisal at all.

USPAP imposes an independent constraint through the Ethics Rule, which prohibits compensation arrangements contingent on the reporting of a predetermined result, a direction in assignment results that favors the client's cause, the amount of a value opinion, the attainment of a stipulated result, or the occurrence of a subsequent event directly related to the appraiser's opinions and specific to the assignment's purpose. Ordinary billing arrangements — hourly, fixed fee, fee by asset count, fee by complexity — are unaffected, and nothing in either framework prevents a fee from being invoiced after the report is delivered. What is barred is compensation that varies with the conclusion.

The practical design is straightforward:

- Bill by time, by a fixed engagement fee, by asset or position count, or by documented complexity.
- If a multi-asset engagement requires tiering, tier the fee on the number and type of assets, never on the value.
- Never accept a proposal that includes basis points of appraised value, a percentage of the deduction, a bonus for a concluded value above a threshold, or a fee reduction if the IRS disallows the position.
- Confirm in writing, in the engagement response, that no part of the fee is contingent on the value conclusion or on a taxing authority's acceptance of it.

A parallel discipline applies to the CPA's own compensation: a fee for return positions should not be structured so that compensation depends on the tax outcome, which creates its own professional responsibility exposure.

## Independence and the Disqualification Screen

Before an engagement letter is signed, the advisor should confirm that the proposed appraiser is not disqualified from serving as a qualified appraiser for this property and this client. The disqualification list in §1.170A-17(b)(5) is specific, and every item on it is a question that can be answered before work begins:

1. Is the appraiser receiving a fee prohibited by §1.170A-17(a)(9)? (See above.)
2. Is the appraiser the donor?
3. Was the appraiser a party to the transaction in which the donor acquired the property — the seller, the exchanger, the giver, or an agent for either side? A narrow exception applies where the property is contributed within two months of acquisition and the appraised value does not exceed the acquisition price.
4. Is the appraiser the donee?
5. Is the appraiser related within the meaning of IRC Section 267(b) to, an employee of, or married to a person described above?
6. Is the appraiser an independent contractor who is regularly used as an appraiser by the donor, a party to the acquisition transaction, or the donee — and who does not perform a majority of appraisals for others during the taxable year?
7. Has the appraiser been barred from practice before the Treasury or the IRS under 31 U.S.C. §330(c) at any time during the three years ending on the date the appraisal is signed?

Item seven has an enforcement counterpart: under §1.170A-17(a)(11), an appraiser barred from practice before the IRS at any point in that three-year window produces an appraisal that is disregarded as to value. The declaration required by §1.170A-17(a)(3)(vi) affirms that no such bar exists and records the appraiser's acknowledgment that IRC Section 6695A penalties may apply.

The same discipline applies to the appraiser's claimed credentials. Section 1.170A-17(b)(1) defines a qualified appraiser as an individual with verifiable education and experience in valuing the type of property, and §1.170A-17(b)(4) makes verifiability a disclosure requirement rather than a credential: the appraiser must specify her education and experience in the appraisal itself and declare that, because of that education and experience, she is qualified to appraise that type of property. Section 1.170A-17(b)(2)(i) accepts either completed professional or college-level coursework in valuing the type of property plus at least two years of experience valuing that type, or a recognized appraiser designation for that type of property.

For digital assets, "type of property" does real work. Section 1.170A-17(b)(3)(i) defines it as the category of property customary in the appraisal field for an appraiser to value, and the regulation's examples confirm that where specialty coursework does not exist, coursework and experience in the broader recognized category can satisfy the test. A practitioner reviewing a proposal should look for three things: a valuation discipline that matches the asset; education and experience specific enough to be checked; and a commitment to include, in the report, the education and experience statement and the qualification declaration that make the credentials verifiable rather than asserted.

Because credentials can be asserted rather than held, verification belongs in the pre-engagement screen. Ask for the basis of the claimed designation, the location and date of relevant coursework, representative engagements of the same asset type, a sample completed report with client information redacted, and confirmation of professional liability coverage. Above all, ask for the appraiser's position on contingent fees, in writing. A proposal structured around a percentage of the value conclusion is not a negotiating starting point; it is a disqualification.

One further boundary belongs in the engagement, because clients and sometimes advisors find it counterintuitive: the appraiser is not the client's advocate. USPAP requires impartiality, objectivity, and independent judgment, and an appraiser who can be directed toward a preferred conclusion is an appraiser whose report will not survive scrutiny. Counsel and CPAs who understand this protect the engagement by never asking the appraiser to reach a number. The correct request — and the one this firm expects — is for the appraiser to identify, in advance and in writing, what evidence would support the conclusion. Anything else is a request to compromise the report.

## The Diligence Chain: Circular 230, Reliance, and Where Exposure Sits

When an attorney or CPA relies on an appraisal in advising a client or preparing a return, the reliance is governed rather than unlimited. Circular 230 §10.22 requires a practitioner to exercise due diligence in preparing or approving returns, documents, and other papers, and in determining the correctness of representations made to the Treasury. Reliance on information furnished by the client or another person is permitted only where the practitioner does not know, and has no reason to know, that the information is incorrect. Reliance is not reasonable where the provider is not competent or lacks the qualifications necessary for the task, or where the provider has a conflict of interest.

That standard converts the engagement screen into a compliance step rather than a courtesy. If the appraiser lacks the qualifications the task requires, or if the fee arrangement creates a conflict, reliance on the resulting conclusion is not defensible — and the practitioner, not the appraiser, is the one answering for the return.

Exposure on the appraiser's side is equally direct. IRC Section 6695A imposes a penalty on a person who prepares an appraisal knowing, or having reason to know, that it will be used in connection with a return or claim for refund, where the claimed value based on that appraisal results in a substantial valuation misstatement, a substantial estate or gift tax valuation understatement, or a gross valuation misstatement. The amount is the lesser of (1) the greater of 10 percent of the underpayment attributable to the misstatement or $1,000, and (2) 125 percent of the gross income received from preparing the appraisal. The exception is narrow and value-based: no penalty if the appraiser establishes that the value in the appraisal was more likely than not the proper value. Because the IRS instructs examiners to open a Section 6695A penalty case where the appraisal-based value produces a substantial or gross misstatement, the appraiser's file — not simply the report — becomes the subject.

Advisors face their own exposure: IRC Section 6701 imposes a civil penalty for aiding and abetting an understatement of tax liability on a person who knows or has reason to believe a document will be used to understate liability; IRC Section 6694 governs return preparer penalties; and under IRC Section 6662 an understatement reaching the substantial or gross misstatement thresholds carries an accuracy-related penalty, with the reasonable-cause defense framework of Section 6664 depending, in practice, on the quality of the file behind the appraisal.

The lesson for retention is that the diligence chain runs in one direction only. The advisor can hire a competent appraiser, give her complete and accurate information, and document both. The advisor cannot outsource diligence to the appraiser, and the appraiser cannot outsource the conclusion to the advisor.

## Privilege: What Is Protected, What Is Not, and Why the Engagement Letter Decides

Privilege questions in digital asset valuation go wrong when practitioners assume that hiring an appraiser sets up a protective bubble. It does not. Three separate protections exist, each with its own conditions, and each has a hard outer boundary in this context.

### The attorney-client privilege and the Kovel doctrine

Communications with a non-attorney professional retained to assist counsel can fall within the attorney-client privilege under the doctrine of United States v. Kovel, 296 F.2d 918 (2d Cir. 1961). Kovel extended the privilege to an accountant whose role was "necessary, or at least highly useful, for the effective consultation between the client and the lawyer," analogizing that role to an interpreter's. The limit was precise: the privilege applies where the communication is made in confidence for the purpose of obtaining legal advice, and "if what is sought is not legal advice but only accounting service … or if the advice sought is the accountant's rather than the lawyer's, no privilege exists."

Courts applying Kovel to valuation and consulting experts require a specific structure. The district court in Cavallaro v. United States, 153 F. Supp. 2d 52 (D. Mass. 2001), required a showing that (1) the professional was an agent of the attorney or the client; (2) the professional's role was to facilitate the client's communications with counsel for the purpose of seeking legal advice; (3) the communications were intended to be confidential; and (4) no waiver occurred. Cavallaro failed on the first and second elements, in part because the engagement letter expressly limited the professional's advice "solely for the benefit of" the corporation and its shareholders. The First Circuit affirmed on Kovel grounds, 284 F.3d 236 (1st Cir. 2002), holding that the third party's involvement "must be nearly indispensable or serve some specialized purpose in facilitating the attorney-client communications" — mere usefulness or convenience is not enough.

Two operational conclusions follow. A Kovel structure is created by the engagement letter and reinforced by billing, not by intention: where protection is the goal, counsel engages the appraiser, the appraiser bills counsel, the engagement letter states that the services are obtained to assist counsel in providing legal advice, and communications are confined to those who need them. And decisively for tax work, protection is unavailable for the appraisal itself where the appraisal's purpose is to support a return. The communications may be privileged; the report is not.

### Section 7525 and the absence of an appraiser-client privilege

There is no accountant-client privilege at common law, as the Supreme Court confirmed in United States v. Arthur Young & Co., 465 U.S. 805 (1984). IRC Section 7525 supplies a limited statutory substitute: with respect to tax advice, taxpayer communications with a federally authorized tax practitioner receive the same confidentiality protections that would apply between a taxpayer and an attorney. The statute's limits matter as much as its grant — the privilege applies to noncriminal tax matters before the IRS and does not apply to written communications regarding tax shelters.

Section 7525 is not a substitute for counsel's involvement, and it is not an appraiser privilege. It protects confidential communications; it does not shield the underlying valuation work, the appraiser's workfile, or documents required to be filed. A CPA-directed engagement does not create the protections an attorney-directed engagement may create, and no engagement letter can change that.

### Why a filed appraisal is designed to be produced

The most important privilege point in digital asset valuation is structural rather than doctrinal. A qualified appraisal for a charitable contribution must contain the appraiser's name, address, and taxpayer identification number; must identify the engaging party where the appraiser is engaged by someone other than the donor; must include the appraiser's signature, date, and declaration; must state that the appraisal was prepared for income tax purposes; and must disclose the method of valuation and the specific basis for the conclusion. Section 1.170A-17(a)(10) then requires the donor to retain the appraisal for so long as it may be relevant in the administration of any internal revenue law.

A document with those characteristics, attached to a return and retained indefinitely for tax administration purposes, cannot be confidential in any meaningful sense. The workfile is likewise producible: the USPAP Record Keeping Rule requires retention for at least five years after preparation, or at least two years after final disposition of any judicial proceeding in which the appraiser provided testimony related to the assignment, whichever period expires last — and the workfile must be made available when required by due process of law. The retention obligation is the appraiser's, and it operates independently of any arrangement between the client and the appraiser.

### The litigation boundary

Where the valuation supports litigation rather than a return, the analysis changes. A non-testifying consulting expert's facts and opinions are generally shielded from discovery except in exceptional circumstances, while a retained expert who will testify must produce a report identifying the facts and data considered, the opinions, and their bases under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(a)(2)(B), and is subject to examination on the materials she considered. Work-product protection covers documents prepared in anticipation of litigation and does not depend on the Kovel elements — but it protects counsel's file, not the appraiser's underlying analysis.

The decision to designate an appraiser as a testifying expert should therefore be deliberate, with the client informed that designation opens the underlying materials to the other side. An appraiser engaged to support a return should not be described as a consultant and then used as an expert; an appraiser engaged to testify should be told, in the engagement letter, that a litigation-ready file is a deliverable.

### The ethics overlay for counsel

Michigan practitioners retain the familiar obligations: competence, including technological competence sufficient to supervise a digital asset engagement; confidentiality of information relating to the representation, which extends to agents and vendors; and responsibility for nonlawyer assistants retained by the firm. Those duties shape the engagement concretely — the client's wallet data is confidential information, sharing it with a vendor requires reasonable care, and explaining the material risks of the engagement, including the discovery consequences of expert designation, is a communication obligation rather than a courtesy. The Michigan Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, MCL 700.1001 through 700.1018, supplies the parallel framework on the fiduciary side, governing how a personal representative or trustee obtains access to digital assets and what records a custodian may be compelled to produce.

## Operational Coordination: Getting the File Right Without Compromising It

The engagement letter sets the structure. Execution determines whether the file holds up.

**Holdings verification without custody.** The appraiser should verify that the assets exist and are held as represented without ever taking custody of private keys or seed phrases. In practice: confirming on-chain holdings against wallet addresses, comparing balances at the valuation effective date, reconciling exchange statements and transfer records, and documenting the verification method. Any clause or instruction that would put keys, seed phrases, or exchange credentials in the appraiser's hands should be refused in writing; it creates security exposure for the client and an independence problem for the appraiser.

**Contemporaneous market evidence.** Volume, order-book depth, venue data, and price observations should be captured at or near the valuation effective date and preserved in the workfile. Reconstructed evidence is weaker evidence, and it invites the argument that the conclusion was built backward from a client's preferred figure.

**Written representations.** Section 1.170A-17(a)(6) states that an appraisal is not a qualified appraisal, even if every other requirement is met, if the donor failed to disclose or misrepresented facts and a reasonable person would expect that failure to cause the appraiser to misstate the value. The client's representations about acquisition, holding, restrictions, and transfers are load-bearing: they belong in writing, in the appraiser's file, reviewed for completeness before the report is finalized.

**No value-directed communications.** Emails asking an appraiser to revise a conclusion to reach a desired number, or to "see what can be supported," become exhibit one in any challenge to the report. Ask the appraiser to identify the evidence that would support a position — before the analysis, not after.

**The handoff package.** Each filing obligation consumes a slightly different package:

| Obligation | Documents the appraisal engagement should produce |
|---|---|
| Charitable deduction — Form 8283 Section B, IRC Section 170 | The qualified appraisal meeting §1.170A-17(a), received before the return due date including extensions; the appraiser's signature and declaration for the form; the contribution-date valuation; disclosure of any agreement affecting use or disposition of the contributed asset |
| Estate tax — Form 706, IRC Section 2032 | Date-of-death valuation with developed alternate-date scope where the fiduciary will elect; a single file supporting both dates; coordination with the fiduciary's inventory obligations |
| Gift tax — Form 709 | Value as of each transfer; gift-by-gift measurement; support for any claimed discount and its adequate disclosure |
| Income tax — Form 1040, Schedule D, Form 1099-DA reconciliation | Value at the relevant transfer or disposition date; reconciliation of basis and proceeds reporting; support where broker reporting and taxpayer records diverge |
| SDIRA annual attestation | Annual value attestation supporting the custodian's reporting; scope matched to the custodian's requirements |
| Audit or litigation support | Workfile retrieval, analysis of the examiner's position, and support for the appraiser's role in the proceeding |

**Retention.** The donor retains the appraisal for as long as it may be relevant in the administration of any internal revenue law; the appraiser retains the workfile for at least five years after preparation, and longer where testimony was given in a proceeding. Both obligations should be confirmed in writing at engagement.

## Common Retention Failures and What Each One Costs

| Failure | The provision it collides with | The cost |
|---|---|---|
| Verbal scope, no engagement letter | USPAP Scope of Work Rule; §1.170A-17(a)(3) | Appraiser cannot show the scope was sufficient; the report cannot be tied to the intended use |
| Fee as a percentage of value, or contingent on the deduction | §1.170A-17(a)(9), §1.170A-17(b)(5)(i); USPAP Ethics Rule | The appraiser is not a qualified appraiser; the substantiation fails regardless of the analysis quality |
| Valuation effective date chosen after the analysis | §1.170A-17(a)(4)–(5) | Timeliness and date defects that cannot be cured by a revised report |
| Appraiser engaged by the wrong party in an estate or trust matter | §1.170A-17(b)(5); fiduciary duties | Disqualification questions, and a report the fiduciary may not be able to rely on |
| Credentials accepted without verification | §1.170A-17(b)(1)–(4); Circular 230 §10.22 | The education and experience statement cannot be substantiated; the advisor's reliance is not reasonable |
| Report issued without the signature, date, or declaration | §1.170A-17(a)(3)(v)–(vii) | The document is not a qualified appraisal |
| Appraiser instructed to reach a specific value | USPAP Ethics Rule; §6662 exposure | Impeachment of the report; potential penalty exposure for appraiser and advisor |
| Appraisal attached to a return but treated as confidential | Why a filed document is producible | Expectation management failure; adverse inference when the file is produced and contradicts the assumption |
| Private keys or exchange credentials transferred to the appraiser | Security and independence | Custody risk and an independence narrative the appraiser cannot support |
| Workfile not retrievable when the examination opens | USPAP Record Keeping Rule; §1.170A-17(a)(10) | The conclusion cannot be substantiated at the moment it must be |

## Pre-Engagement Checklist for Attorneys and CPAs

1. Confirm the filing obligation or proceeding the report must support, and state it in the request.
2. Confirm the valuation effective date, and the rule that fixes it.
3. Identify the client of record, the intended users, and whether counsel is engaging the appraiser.
4. Confirm in writing that no part of the fee is based on the value conclusion or on a taxing authority's acceptance of it.
5. Verify the lead appraiser's education, experience, and designations, and ask where the education and experience statement will appear in the report.
6. Screen the appraiser against the §1.170A-17(b)(5) disqualification list, including the three-year barred-practice question.
7. Confirm the appraiser will sign and date the report and provide the required declarations.
8. Agree the reporting format and the deliverable timeline against the filing deadline.
9. Establish the data protocol, including how holdings will be verified without any transfer of keys or credentials.
10. Decide, deliberately, whether the appraiser is engaged for return support, for litigation support, or for both — and paper the distinction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who is the client of a digital asset appraisal when an attorney retains the appraiser?

The client is the party who engages the appraiser. Where counsel retains the appraiser to assist in providing legal advice, the client of record should be identified in the engagement letter, and the appraiser must still identify intended users separately. USPAP treats client and intended users as distinct, and the distinction controls scope, reporting, and confidentiality.

### Who should be listed as intended users of a digital asset appraisal?

The parties for whom the results are intended — typically the client, named advisors, and any filing recipient such as a taxing authority. The appraiser's scope must be sufficient to produce a credible result for their use; an appraisal prepared without naming the intended user may be scoped to the wrong purpose.

### What must a digital asset appraisal engagement letter contain?

Parties and capacity; the specific assets and holdings; intended use; intended users; the definition of value; the valuation effective date; the scope of work and any disclosed exclusions; data responsibilities and written representations; the access protocol for holdings verification; assumptions and limiting conditions; the reporting format; the fee structure; reliance and re-use terms; confidentiality terms; and confirmation that the appraiser will sign, date, and certify the report.

### Can an appraiser's fee be based on a percentage of the appraised value?

Not in an appraisal supporting a charitable deduction. Treasury Regulation §1.170A-17(a)(9) prohibits any part of the fee being based on the appraised value, including a fee that depends on what the IRS ultimately allows, and §1.170A-17(b)(5)(i) makes an individual receiving a prohibited fee not a qualified appraiser for that property. USPAP separately bars compensation contingent on a predetermined result, the amount of a value opinion, or the attainment of a stipulated result.

### Does the fee rule affect non-charitable appraisals?

The express federal prohibition is tied to qualified appraisals under IRS Section 170. The USPAP Ethics Rule applies to appraisal practice generally and independently bars contingent compensation of the kinds described. For estate, gift, and litigation work, practitioners should apply the same structure as a matter of course, because a fee arrangement built on the conclusion undermines the credibility of the opinion.

### Is a digital asset appraisal protected by attorney-client privilege?

Generally not, where the appraisal supports a return. A qualified appraisal must include the appraiser's identity, taxpayer identification number, signature, and declaration, and must be retained for tax administration purposes; a document with those features is designed to be produced. Communications between counsel and an appraiser retained to assist in providing legal advice may be protected under the Kovel doctrine, but only if the engagement is structured for that purpose and the four-part showing can be made. There is no accountant-client privilege, and IRC Section 7525 is limited to noncriminal tax matters and excludes tax shelters.

### Can a CPA rely on an appraiser's valuation without additional work?

Circular 230 §10.22 permits reliance on information furnished by a client or third party only where the practitioner does not know, and has no reason to know, that the information is incorrect, and reliance is not reasonable where the provider lacks the necessary qualifications or has a conflict of interest. Reasonable reliance therefore requires the same qualification and independence screen that good engagement practice requires, documented in the file.

### How long must an appraiser retain the workfile for a digital asset appraisal?

Under the USPAP Record Keeping Rule, at least five years after preparation, or at least two years after final disposition of any judicial proceeding in which the appraiser provided testimony related to the assignment, whichever period expires last. The workfile must be retrievable throughout and produced when required by due process of law. Separately, the donor must retain the qualified appraisal for as long as it may be relevant in the administration of any internal revenue law.

### What is the single most common retention mistake?

Leaving the scope and the valuation effective date to a conversation rather than a document. Every downstream defect — a report that cannot support the filing obligation, a date that cannot be reconciled with the contribution, a disqualification question that should have been screened, an assumption nobody wrote down — traces back to an engagement that was never specified in writing.

## Retaining an Appraiser Who Can Sign the Report

The retention decision is where a digital asset valuation is won or lost. The scope, the date, the fee, the independence posture, and the privilege architecture are all settled before the appraiser opens a file — and none of them can be repaired by better analysis afterward. A practitioner who documents the assignment, screens the appraiser against the disqualification rules, structures the fee so the qualification survives, and understands what a filed document can and cannot keep confidential has done more for the client's position than any amount of post-hoc advocacy.
